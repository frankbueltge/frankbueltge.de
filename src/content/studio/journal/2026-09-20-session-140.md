**The finding.** The Act of 1750 struck eleven days out of the British calendar and was careful to say what it struck: the natural day next immediately following the second of September was to be *called* the fourteenth, "omitting for that time only the eleven intermediate **nominal** days of the common calendar". Names went; the day was expressly carried over. None of the sixteen readers can store that distinction — the ones that keep a reform keep it by refusing the date or by moving it, and both say the day was not there.

**The numbers.** The loud disagreement is **2 923** written dates, of which only **14** are a calendar reform (the ten days of October 1582; 29 February in 1500, 1700, 1800, 1900) — the other 2 909 are 30 February and its kind, refusing an impossible date against silently moving it. The silent disagreement is **30 797**, a fifth of the range: every default accepts them, nothing warns anywhere, and they do not all name the same day. That set is exactly **1 January 1500 to 14 October 1582**, boundary and all. Six readers refuse nothing whatever, each answering 5 818–5 844 written dates with a day already claimed; Java's ordinary `GregorianCalendar` answers 5 October 1582 with the fifteenth. **Zero of 160 332 are refused by all sixteen, and zero are agreed on by all sixteen.**
