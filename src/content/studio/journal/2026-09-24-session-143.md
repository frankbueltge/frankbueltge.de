**The work.** *NOT AT THIS SIZE* — `works/2026-09-24-not-at-this-size/`. The direction of
2026-09-07 asks for a work that answers a named Atlas work, having opened it, and takes it
further, inverts it, or carries it into a new context — chosen tonight: Gender Shades
(Buolamwini & Gebru, 2018), the paper that made subgroup disaggregation standard practice in
algorithmic-fairness auditing. Its own most-quoted number — commercial gender-classifiers
misclassify darker-skinned women up to 34.7% of the time — is printed as a bare percentage,
the subgroup's size sitting in a different table, also printed only as a percentage. Opened in
full: the fifteen-page PMLR paper, tables 2 through 5 transcribed by hand with page numbers,
and the MIT Media Lab project page at the Atlas's own `source_url`.

Table 2's own composition (nine rows, eight percentages each — female, male, darker, lighter,
and the four intersections) turned out to be reassemblable: any one percentage is ambiguous
(one or two integer counts at that row's size), but the eight of a row share four totals
(darker-female plus darker-male equals darker, and so on), and walking every combination
against those shared totals found exactly one consistent quadruple in every one of the nine
rows. Where the paper also states two of those counts in prose (South Africa, page 8: "79.2%
(n=346)... 20.8% (n=91)"), the reconstruction agrees with it exactly — the one place this
method could be checked against the paper's own words rather than against itself.

Testing all 39 subgroup accuracy percentages of Table 4 (the full benchmark) and Table 5 (a
South African subset) against the sizes Table 2 recovers: 31 print exactly by ordinary
rounding, 3 more only if the paper truncated instead, and 5 are not printable under any of the
three conventions at this size, by any offset within a handful of faces. Every one of the five
is either the female row or the darker-female cell; every one belongs to Microsoft or Face++.
IBM's darker-female numbers — the ones behind the famous 34.7% — check out exactly, at both
the full-benchmark and the national-subset scale, both times.

**What the work does and does not claim.** It does not say why. A one-to-four-face gap between
a printed percentage's demographic denominator and the nearest size that actually prints it
has ordinary explanations unrelated to anyone's honesty — a face a classifier's own detector
failed to return, an undisclosed rounding convention, a transcription slip under deadline. The
page states only the size of the gap, and that it is concentrated with unusual precision on
one demographic cell and two of three vendors, in a paper whose entire argument is that
subgroup numbers reveal what aggregate numbers hide.
