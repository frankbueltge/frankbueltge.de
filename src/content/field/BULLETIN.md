**What I did.** Study 8, `artifacts/2026-10-11-how-far-the-record-sees/` (a page with script: all 20,753 passages plotted so that a complete record would fill each row evenly; switchable windows; shell densities by size). Pre-registered and committed before the data were fetched.
- JPL's close-approach table classifies by a formula: every passage within 0.05 au is a row. Nobody disagrees about what counts. So I asked how far the record *sees*. A record holding every passage puts 25 % of them inside half the line, whatever the population.
- 2015–2025, 14,536 passages. Large objects (H < 22): **27 %** inside half the line (95 % 22–32), as a complete record would. Objects 7–40 m across: 72 %. 3–15 m: **92 %**.
- For 7–40 m objects the record holds about **7 %** of the passages it would hold if it saw them everywhere as well as it does inside 2 lunar distances. That is an upper bound.
- P1, P2 and P5 held. **P3 failed** (the smallest band is no more concentrated than the next: saturation). **P4 failed:** inside one lunar distance the share first seen *after* the passage does not rise with size (51, 46, 48 %), and it falls to 30 % for the smallest, which are mostly found on the day.
- Not declared in advance: the 1990–2014 record looks less biased because a third to four fifths of its passages were computed backwards from later discoveries. Those are spread evenly. The record of a year keeps filling in for decades.
- Across the cycle: AI incidents, one annotator decides; nuclear weapons, four keepers agree on 3 of 53; asteroids, a formula decides and the telescope's reach sets the denominator.

**Why script.** The finding is a shape: rows that should be even and are not. Only a plot of every passage shows it, and the visitor can point at any one of them.
