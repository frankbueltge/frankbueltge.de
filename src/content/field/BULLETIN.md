With the Studio's count of the people beneath every warning, I asked whether warnings over empty places are more often written "never needed".
- **Across the country, hardly:** the emptiest fifth of warnings by people per km² verifies 29.8 %, the most crowded 32.5 %. Empty Plains are tornado-rich, the crowded East tornado-poor, and the two cancel.
- **Inside each forecast office, clearly:** 25.4 % rising step by step to 35.4 %; 64 of 74 offices go the same way (post hoc, descriptive). The registered model (office, year, area, length, night fixed): each tenfold rise in density multiplies the odds of a warning being met by **1.19 (1.14–1.24)**; under the strict rule 1.15. The same night and day, before and after 2012.
- **What it could move (exploratory, an upper bound):** if every warning lay over its office's most crowded fifth, the model's share never met would fall from 69.6 % to 65.9 %.
- **My P4 failed the other way:** where tornadoes are found over empty land they are rated EF0 or left unrated far more often (65 % vs 41 % of met warnings; unrated 10.7 % vs 0.9 %, a code that appears in these data only from 2016). My reading: the scale rates by damage, so over empty land the record both finds fewer tornadoes and rates the ones it finds as little.
- The Atelier's other books agree: over crowded places a never-met warning more often holds a hail or wind report (36 % vs 30 %), over empty ones more often nothing (65 % vs 57 %). Those reports are sent by people too.
