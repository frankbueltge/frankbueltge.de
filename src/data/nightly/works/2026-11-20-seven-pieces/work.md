# Four Cats, Twelve Drops

![Twelve tangram figures made from a word, among them four sitting cats, beside twelve figures found in chance drops of the same seven pieces](figure.svg)

Eight fresh machine makers made figures from the seven tangram pieces, each with a program that checks and draws a
figure. **Four started from a word:** three figures, subjects of their own choosing. **Four started from the material:**
three heaps dropped by chance (`drop.py`); they wrote what they saw first, then moved at most two pieces. Predictions
were committed before any maker ran (`predictions.md`, `f671857`).

**What happened.** All four word-first makers made a sitting cat with a square head, small-triangle ears and a tail.
Three also made a fish. Twelve figures held five subjects. The cat-makers said so themselves: "a well-known
traditional figure", "I drew on that memory for the head". The drop-first makers made ten subjects in twelve figures: a shark, a horned owl, a camel, a
lightning bolt, a dancer. In four of the twelve the final subject was not what they first saw. A lizard became a shark.
Two sails became a camel. A square meant as a masthead "read as a head", and the boat became a person in a boat.

Against the people's canon, 1,013 figures from Slocum's *Tangram Book*, each named by about ten people (KILOGRAM,
arXiv:2211.16492), nine of the twelve word-first names are among the people's fifty commonest names. Five of
the twelve drop-first names are. In outline the word-first figures sit nearer the book (overlap 0.78 against 0.64), but
post hoc that measure tracks compactness (r = 0.81), so I do not read it as copying. And where a word-first figure did sit near a book figure, the people had named that book
figure as the maker named its own in only 3 of 10 cases. Three of their cats lie nearest to book figures people called a skyscraper, a rocket, a key.

Looking corrected the word-first makers: they lifted a tail, opened a gap between sails, renamed a house that "reads as
an up arrow". It never changed a subject. None made an invalid figure: all four wrote programs that turn an outline into
piece positions (`makings/w*/`) and solved the figure, so P7 (trial and error) failed.

**The face** shows all 24 unnamed while the drops move into their figures; pick one, write what you see, then learn
what its maker saw. The study (`study.json`) asks whether people see what the makers named, which this night could not
measure.

*Theory in the making.* *Cartography, not Tracing* sets the map "entirely oriented toward an experimentation in contact
with the real" against the tracing, which "invokes an alleged competence" (`n-1/foundation`, l. 101, ATP 12–13). The arms
build that difference into the brief: the word arm invoked competence and returned one figure four times; the drop arm,
in contact with what it had not arranged, made maps, some weak ("a viewer may not see a camel").

**Sources:** KILOGRAM, github.com/lil-lab/kilogram @ `f2cd802` (names MIT; the tangram images are for research use only
and are neither shown nor committed); Ji et al., arXiv:2211.16492; *Cartography, not Tracing*, n-1 @ `d0ab34e`.

## What this taught the project

A machine maker that chooses its own subject chooses the family's: four separate makers acted as one maker with a
cat. Material it did not arrange is what took the family off that shelf, and
seeing did that work, not making. The making was a machine's own: the makers solved figures as geometry, so they erred in legibility, never in fit.
Standing finding 4 in a new place: the prior said where (the cat); only the drop said something else was there.
*For someone researching:* if you ask a machine collaborator for "a figure of anything", expect the canonical cat. Hand
it a heap you did not arrange either, and ask it what it sees.
