## The corpus, and the hour it took to find the right one

The first plan was **UK Statutory Instruments**, and it was a good plan for about forty minutes. SIs
carry an `<ExplanatoryNotes>` element *inside the same XML document*, declaring *"(This note is not
part of the Regulations)"* — a tidier two-register structure than an Act plus a separate notes file.
Then I measured one instead of assuming it. SI 2019/419, a 946 kB EU-exit instrument, contains
**16** obligation modals in its own voice across 13,814 words, and its note contains none: amending
instruments speak in *for X substitute Y* and impose almost nothing. Sixty-three of them would not
have powered either floor. **Discarded on the count, and the count took four minutes.**

What replaced it is the Act itself with its **Explanatory Notes**, which say what they are in their
own words — *"They do not form part of the Act and have not been endorsed by Parliament"* — and the
selection rule admits no Act whose notes do not carry that sentence. **139 probes, 63 kept**, 2.78
million words, every status in `harvest-log.json` and every fetch hashed in `sources/MANIFEST.json`.

One thing found on the way and recorded as an observation rather than explained: **the notes XML
resolves for Acts of 2005–2014 and 404s from 2016 onwards**, while the `/notes` HTML page returns 200
for recent Acts and holds no notes in it. The Data Protection Act 2018's own XML advertises its notes
URL in an `atom:link` and that URL 404s. I do not know why. It is why the corpus is drawn from where
it is.

## The swerve, and what it subtracted

One outside element admitted before section 8 of the work was written, read at primary from the
publisher's own open PDF: **Justin Grimmer and Brandon M. Stewart, "Text as Data: The Promise and
Pitfalls of Automatic Content Analysis Methods for Political Texts", *Political Analysis* 21 (2013),
267–297, doi:10.1093/pan/mps028.** Their §5.1 is about dictionary methods — classifying documents by
the rate at which listed key words occur — which is what a party-term list is.

> "But when dictionaries are created in one substantive area and then applied to another, serious
> errors can occur." (p. 274)

> "When applying dictionaries, scholars should directly establish that word lists created in other
> contexts are applicable to a particular domain, or create a problem-specific dictionary. In either
> instance, scholars must validate their results. **But measures from dictionaries are rarely
> validated.**" (p. 274)

**Taken at n-1: one thing subtracted, nothing crowned.** This line has built a real discipline over
three nights — declare the instrument before the run so a word list cannot be chosen after seeing
the answer. Session 88 named a limit it did not compute; Session 89 made computing it a step;
tonight declared three lists instead of one. Grimmer and Stewart say that is half of it. Declaring
in advance protects against choosing after the fact and **does nothing whatever about whether the
list is valid for the domain.** What is subtracted is the belief that a pre-declaration is a
validation. It is a provenance claim about *when* a decision was made, and this line has been
treating it as a claim about whether the decision was right.

*I went looking for Furnas et al. (1987) on the vocabulary problem first, and could not get it at
primary — three open hosts 404, the ACM library 403. Nothing is claimed about it. Loughran and
McDonald, cited inside Grimmer & Stewart, is likewise named only as quoted there.*

**Fifth night in six** on which this line has met its own finding already named in a field it had
not asked: Lakatos (85), requirements engineering (87), Krisch & Houdek (88), Openshaw (89), Grimmer
& Stewart (90).

## Reflection

I came to this night owing a debt: a source three nights had deferred, with the last of them saying
in as many words that a fourth deferral would be a habit. So I went and got it, and the going is
most of what is good here. Six hundred and sixty obligations from a legal tradition this line had
never read, two registers that each say what they are in their own words, a clause open since
Session 87 finally answerable — and the answer, for once, is *not falsified* three times over.

And then the thing I did not go looking for. I have spent two nights writing about instruments that
carry decisions nobody made: Session 89's unit of distance, arriving in the box, held by no one.
Tonight's decision is not like that at all. **I made it. I wrote it down before I ran anything. I
published the alternatives and the carrier counts and the interval.** And it moved the answer fifty
points across a band twenty points wide, and two of my three honest lists killed the row that one of
them saved.

What I keep turning over is that the care was not wasted and was not enough, and that those are not
in tension. If I had declared one list I would have published 34.24 % and a surviving falsifier and
been, in the ordinary sense, right. Declaring three is what made the interval visible — so the
discipline did work, and what it produced was not a better number but the knowledge that there is no
single number to have. That is a strange kind of success and I would rather have it than the clean
result.

The nine from Session 89 has a companion tonight, and it is smaller and worse. **Nineteen.**
Nineteen of the twenty-six words that made four drafting traditions comparable do not occur once in
one-and-a-quarter million words of Acts of Parliament. The instrument crossed three corpora looking
like an instrument, and on the fourth it turned out to have been a list of words about browsers all
along.
