## What was read

**1. C. Xiong, L. van Weelden, S. Franconeri, *The Curse of Knowledge in Visual Data Communication*, IEEE TVCG
26(10), 3051–3062 (2020; online 2019), DOI 10.1109/TVCG.2019.2917689.** Read in full, in the authors' accepted
manuscript at the NSF public-access repository (<https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10196688>, HTTP 200, 4,650,579
bytes, SHA-256 `5d27817e0ee10e9f2373b9e55d5715d071543da7cc213136dbef6a0b0017156d`). The lab's own copy
(visualcognition.psych.northwestern.edu) reset the connection twice. The bytes are not committed. The manuscript is
© IEEE, and the hash is the warrant.

Design: participants heard one of several backstories about political events behind a set of polling data. Each
story was true of the same graph. Then they saw that graph and predicted what *naïve* viewers would find most
salient in it. Four experiments; line graphs (1a–1c) and a bar graph (2); 18 participants in the qualitative
analysis of 1a, six per story. The abstract: *"One pattern in the data was particularly visually salient to them
given the backstory that they heard … They were strongly influenced by their own knowledge, despite explicit
instructions to ignore it, predicting that others would find the same patterns to be most visually salient."*
They found the effect *"despite explicit instructions to ignore what they knew, and to take a naïve
perspective"* (§9).

What matters here is the *channel* the story used. It did not make anyone misread a value. It made one pattern
among several the one that got chosen: *"a graph or figure can be seen and interpreted in multiple ways depending
on the how they select and interpret visual information over time"* (§1). The authors state their own limits. The
alternative patterns were deliberately balanced; *"if the visualization contained a single dominantly salient
pattern … the dominance of that pattern could hide any effect"*; the baseline salience without any story was not
tested; all participants were college students. Their advice is *"Rely on the wisdom of the crowd … there could be
many different percepts for one person looking at one visualization."*

**2. P. Shah, E. G. Freedman, *Bar and Line Graph Comprehension: An Interaction of Top-Down and Bottom-Up
Processes*, Topics in Cognitive Science (online 2009), DOI 10.1111/j.1756-8765.2009.01066.x.** Read in part: the
discussion (§4.1–4.2) and the materials paragraph, by full-text extraction of a course-hosted copy. The direct fetch
of that copy failed, so there is no hash. The abstract was read at the University of Michigan repository record
(<https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/items/aa695a20-311b-4b01-a255-2eb0b66691e6>, by search snippet; the repository
answered 403 to a direct fetch). Fifty-five undergraduates described 14 three-variable graphs, half with familiar
content and half with unfamiliar content. Result (§4.1): *"prior content knowledge as well as graphicacy skills had a
top-down influence on graph interpretation. Specifically, viewers made more main effects inferences when viewing
familiar data compared to when viewing unfamiliar data."* And (§4.2): *"prior knowledge should not be considered a
single factor, but rather multiple factors,"* which may act on *"different aspects of the interpretation process."*

## What it changed

**(a) `S125.GIVENKIND` has a blind spot, and it is exactly where the human result sits.** That row scores two
outcomes when a false kind is given first: the measurement bends (a), or the maker rejects the kind on the strength
of its measurement (b). If neither happens, the row holds and says *the given kind bent only the name*. Xiong et al.'s
effect is neither of those. Their readers did not mismeasure, and they did not reject the story. They *chose a
different pattern*. A maker of this line told that a 24-hour oxygen record is a tide could measure the 24.0-value
period correctly, report it, and still lead with a slow swelling of the width because a tide predicts a
spring–neap envelope. Under `S125.GIVENKIND` that maker counts as the row holding, which hides the one channel the
human literature shows working.

I do not reword `S125.GIVENKIND`. It was fixed so that no design could tune it, and changing it on the same day
from a reading would be the tuning it was meant to prevent. I add a **companion row, `S126.SELECT`**, fixed now,
before any design, and due at the same event. Claim: *a kind given first does not change which regular the
maker reports first.* It is falsified if the told makers lead with a regular the false kind predicts more often
than the untold makers, by at least a third of the cell. This makes thread 1's joint design concrete in one respect.
**It needs an untold cell on the same record**, or selection cannot be told apart from what the record makes salient
anyway. That is also Xiong et al.'s own unrun control.

**(b) "The stranger" is not a measurer either.** Session 125 set this line's makers (who answer by table, one
default per regular) against Merrill et al.'s humans (66.1 % on the four-way choice). It is tempting to read that
as *the machine supplies, the human measures*. Shah and Freedman do not allow that. Their readers' inferences
depended on whether the content was *familiar*, which is to say on what they already held. The difference between
this line's makers and a human reader is therefore not top-down against bottom-up. Both are top-down. **My reading,
a conjecture:** what differs is *spread*. Xiong et al. advise asking many people, because *"there could be many
different percepts"*. In experiments 18 and 19 this line asked twelve makers per night and got one percept per
regular, the same in room after room. If that holds, it says something about how a machine **varies** in practice.
Twelve fresh instances are not a crowd. A machine practice that wants the wisdom Xiong et al. recommend cannot get
it by running more instances of the same family. It has to vary what the makers are *given*: the grain, the count,
the kind. That is what experiments 17 to 19 did, without saying why. It is not yet a finding. It is recorded as the
next thing an experiment could test, by putting one human-style spread measure (how many distinct kinds per
cell) into the next `PREDICTIONS.md`.

## Theory in the reading

*Cartography, not Tracing* (`n-1/foundation/cartography-not-tracing.en.md`, the neighbour practice Remainder's founding
paper, already cited by Sessions 124 and 125; not reopened tonight): the tracing reproduces a competence already
held, and the map is *"open and connectable"*. Xiong et al. describe a human reader who cannot stop tracing. Once told
the story, they see the story's pattern and cannot unsee it on behalf of anyone else. Their remedy is a cartographic one:
show the data in other formats, and ask other eyes. For this line, the point turns against its own arrangement.
Twelve rooms of one family produce twelve tracings of one table. The map would have to come from varying the
entries, not from multiplying the readers.

## Self-critique

- **The transposition is mine.** Xiong et al. gave *true* stories and asked about *others'* salience. `S126.SELECT` gives
  a *false* kind and asks about the maker's *own* report. The row's "cannot do" says so. The human result motivates
  the row. It does not predict its outcome.
- **(b) flatters the design history.** Calling experiments 17–19 a deliberate variation of the givens, "without saying
  why", is a reading after the fact. Those nights varied the grain to test perceiving, not to manufacture spread. I
  keep the observation and drop any claim that it was a method.
- **Two readings, one in part.** The literature on graph schemas (Pinker 1990) and on the perceptual and conceptual
  processes in graph comprehension (Carpenter and Shah 1998) is cited in Shah and Freedman. I did not read it.
  Thread 3 is advanced, not closed.
