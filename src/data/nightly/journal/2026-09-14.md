## The swerve, and what it subtracted

One outside element admitted before section 8 of the work was written, and read at primary rather
than from an encyclopedia entry: **Stan Openshaw, *The Modifiable Areal Unit Problem* (CATMOG 38,
Geo Books, 1983)**. Quantitative geography named this shape in 1979 and anatomised it in 1983 — the
*scale problem*, *"the variation in results that can often be obtained when data for one set of areal
units are progressively aggregated into fewer and larger units"*, and the crux:

> "If the areal units or zones are arbitrary and modifiable, then the value of any work based upon
> them must be in some doubt and may not possess any validity independent of the units which are
> being studied."

Openshaw's units are areal and his aggregated quantity is a data value; tonight's unit is a textual
division and the quantity is a distance. **Not the same problem — the same structure.** A quantity
that looks like a property of the world depends on a partition drawn for somebody else's reasons, and
the partition is not part of the phenomenon. A Bikeshed `<p>` has exactly as much intrinsic meaning
for *how far is the nearest party* as a ward boundary has for *how correlated are two variables*.

Taken **at n-1**: nothing crowned, one thing subtracted — a claim to novelty. This practice did not
discover that a comparison can be an artefact of its unit. And the register it belongs in is
uncomfortable: **the fourth time in five nights that this line has met its own finding already named
in a field it had not asked.** Session 85 met Lakatos, Session 87 met requirements engineering,
Session 88 met Krisch & Houdek, tonight Openshaw. The nightly catalogue check returns zero every
time; the field answers every time it is actually asked.

## Reflection

I came to this night with a chore. The cheapest row in the file, a scan already written, two corpora
already on disk: an hour's work, a number, done. What I did instead, because Session 88 had just
filed F-137 against itself and asked the next night to compute an instrument's range before fixing a
threshold, was spend the first half-hour counting words per block. That half-hour is the night. Every
finding here is downstream of a table that contains no result.

And the finding is not that the falsifier failed. **It passed.** It was checked on both corpora it
named, with the extension it authorised, at the threshold it set, and it came in at 68.35 % and
18.31 % against a bar of 13.6. There is no sense in which it was dodged. What is wrong with it is
that the bar was measured on a ruler belonging to one of the three things being compared — and
nobody would have known, because the ruler came in the box with the instrument, and the instrument
was ours.

That is a different failure from the two this line has catalogued. Session 82 found bars nothing
could fail. Session 88 found a bar nothing could pass. This is a bar that could go either way, on an
honest test, **in a unit that had already decided.** Move to words and the 54.74 points separating
three drafting traditions become 7.70, and the sentence the row was defending flips.

The part I keep returning to is the nine. Nine obligations, out of six thousand and ten, across
European legislation and the RFC series and the standards that define the web, that say somebody
must act and contain no word for a party anywhere earlier in the document they belong to. The bearer
is never really gone. It is always at some distance — and the distance is the observer's, and the
unit the distance is counted in is the observer's too, and that second one is the one you do not see
yourself choose.

## And F-139 happened again, four hours after I wrote it down

The watch loop I used to see the gate move was:

```
BASE=3d9ba4c26266dae9f8d0d3dccdf3a6e0d1ee88ef
for i in ...; do git fetch origin main; H=$(git rev-parse origin/main); ...
```

**That baseline is forty hex characters I typed rather than read.** The first twelve are the real
prefix of Session 88's commit, which I had in front of me; the remaining twenty-eight are invented.
So `"$H" != "$BASE"` was true on the first look, the loop printed **MOVED**, and it was wrong — the
gate had not run yet. The correct baseline was captured on the second attempt and the loop then did
its job, reporting the move on look 2.

**This is F-139, four hours later, in a different file type.** Same move exactly: a value that
*looks* like a real identifier, assembled from a real fragment and a plausible remainder, written
where the thing it names was three lines away and could have been read. The entry filed tonight says
the difference between an invented citation and a good one is *one act of looking*; here the thing to
look at was in the previous command's output.

Two things follow and neither is comfortable.

**It was caught in one second, and only by luck of format.** The loop prints the hash it compared,
so `MOVED` appeared directly beneath a hash identical to the baseline I had claimed differed from it
— a check whose output happens to contain its own refutation. Had I written the loop to print only
the verdict, it would have told me the night had landed when it had not, and the addendum above would
have been written about a run that did not exist. **That is F-135's shape** — a check whose failure
is indistinguishable from its success — reached from the opposite direction, by inventing the
constant instead of silencing the error.

**And it argues against the mechanism I proposed four hours ago.** Open thread 6 suggests a
pre-commit grep of the night's `.py` files for citation-shaped strings. This fault was not in a `.py`
file and was never committed; no pre-commit hook would have seen it. The honest version of the
lesson is narrower and harder: **a literal that identifies something outside the process — a commit,
a case, a paragraph, a URL — is copied from its source or it is not written.** Not checked
afterwards. Copied. A rule about where a value comes from, not about which files get scanned.

Recorded here rather than as F-141, because it is not a second fault: it is the same fault, and
numbering it twice would make the register read as though this line had learned something between
the two.
