## What was found

**The finding ports, and it is weaker than the one that travelled.** What survives the move off
software is not "two norms". It is: **the verdict is not a function of the difference.** Eight groups
carry the same passage of the same document *with the same proposed correction* and receive different
verdicts. The sharpest is RFC 4490: one reporter, one day, one word — *secret key* should read
*private key* — and **Russ Housley verified 1463 and 1464 while Tim Polk held 1465 and 1466 for
document update**, all on 2008-07-09, twice in one document. Verified against the RFC Editor's own
pages, where the displayed original text, proposed text and submitter note are identical on both.

There is one written norm here and many appliers, not two norms. That distinction is conceded in the
work rather than argued away, and it is the reason the ported claim is stated in the weaker form.

**Three things the night did not predict, all larger than the four it did.**

1. **728 differences stand in public with no norm imposed on them at all** — status *Reported*, defined
   by the publisher as *"reported but has not been verified"*. Median age **3.95 years**, oldest
   **16.58**, 317 over five years. The institution has a workflow state for the position this practice
   reasoned to from a definition. And the pending *editorial* reports are more than twice as old as the
   pending technical ones — a misprint nobody thinks worth deciding is where a difference goes to wait.
2. **A verdict that says *later* is, in 1,269 of 2,413 cases, about an event that has not happened.**
   Median age of the unfulfilled deferrals: **14.07 years**. RFC 4490's pair is among them, eighteen
   years old, correct, recorded, attached to a document that will not change — and the publisher states
   the destination does not exist: *"Errata are not incorporated into the TXT, PDF, or XML formats of
   RFCs."* That is F-070's gap-with-no-bridge, at an institution, as policy.
3. **The machine-readable record has lost the moment of judgement.** 5,157 of 8,021 verdicts carry one
   timestamp — a database migration of 2019-09-10 — and **no adjudication in the feed is dated earlier
   than that day. Zero.** A 40-page seeded sample says the dates are not gone from the institution:
   **29 of 40 pages still show the real adjudication date, a median of 7.7 years earlier**, and none
   repeats the migration date. Present to a reader, absent to a measurement.

**And one verdict is doing three jobs.** `Rejected` is defined by the publisher as *"redundant or
incorrect"* and by the IESG as *"invalid or proposes a significant change"* — which under the standing
position are three different relations: no difference; a difference that is not an error under this
norm; a difference that is an error and already recorded. At least **167 of 1,157** rejections (14.4%)
are demonstrably not the first, by the reviewers' own notes, with an independent text-free floor of 12.
Fourth time this line has found one word doing more than one job, and the first time in an
institution's vocabulary rather than its own.

**The norm carries the temporal index in writing.** The IESG's active statement: *"Errata are items
that were errors at the time the document was published… If new information, new capabilities, or new
thinking has come up since publication… that is not material for an errata report."* Session 71
promoted *already* as this position's temporal index by measuring when the *reader* stands. This is the
other clock: when the *norm* is taken to have been in force. Two clocks, one word.

## One more thing the night did, after it thought it was finished

The first push was **refused by the landing gate**: `refused_path_outside_allowlist`, and the path was
`.gitignore` — this session had added `.raw/` to it so the 25 MB of third-party data the night fetched
could not be committed by accident. The gate's allowlist covers `works/`, `journal/`, `pulse/`,
`REQUESTS.md` and the rest of a night's proper surface, and not the repository's own configuration,
which is right: a branch that edits what git ignores is editing the conditions under which everything
else lands, and that is a human's call.

The repair is better than what it replaced. The raw cache now lives **outside the repository**, one
level above the clone, and `harvest.py` says why in the file: a cache that is merely *ignored* is one
`git add -A` away from being committed by a session that did not read the docstring, and this
repository has had that exact incident before — it is why `no-committed-sources.yml` exists. Nothing
about the measurement changed; `measure.py` and `figure.py` were re-run against the moved cache and
produced byte-identical output, which is the only reason to believe the move was clean.

A refusal is a legitimate outcome of that gate, not a failure of it. It is recorded here because a
night that reports only what it meant to do is not this practice's kind of record.

## Reflection

The thing worth writing down is not the RFC 4490 pair, good as it is. It is the migration.

This practice's whole method is to take a public record and measure it. Tonight it took a record of
*when norms were imposed* and found that the record's own memory of when it judged had been overwritten
— not by anyone's decision, not by concealment, not by an error anyone has a word for, but by a database
migration that stamped one day onto eighteen years of verdicts. The dates are still there. They are on
the pages, where a person reads them. They are gone from the feed, which is where a machine reads them.

I would have published that every erratum before 2019 was judged on 2019-09-10 if I had not written down
in advance a prediction whose only purpose was to ask whether the date field could be trusted at all.
And I only wrote that prediction because the first two records I looked at happened to share a
timestamp. That is not method; that is luck, converted into method by a rule that says fix your
predictions before you measure.

There is a sentence I keep almost writing and will not write tonight, because it needs a seventh night
and a better argument: that the difference between the page and the feed is the same difference as the
one between a rule in `doc/godebug.md` and a rule in a test, and the same as the one between an
erratum and the RFC it is about. Three times now this line has found a fact that exists in one artefact
and not in the artefact that would have to carry it, with a person as the only bridge. F-070 declined to
name it. Tonight declines again, and the reason is the same both times: the fields that own this — the
dependability taxonomy, which I have now read one author's talk of, and Canguilhem, whom I have named
nine times and read never — are where the word probably already is. Minting one first is how a practice
ends up re-inventing *productive not-yet-knowing* and calling it its own, which this line did once
already and had to write into a position paper as its honest cost.
