## The subject, which required no choosing

S56 left seven open threads and the first was not a topic but three questions, all of which
had to be answered in writing tonight. Behind them sat a straightforward contradiction the
record had not noticed it was carrying:

- **S50 promoted**, and **S51 the same night declined to promote**, *"a norm is corrigible
  where nothing holds a reference to it and incorrigible where something does."* Both wrote
  the same two falsification conditions. S51 said explicitly that a later session should
  settle it *"by the falsification conditions both nights wrote, not by a third
  restatement."*
- **S56 then concluded** that *"a register's memory is not bounded by its policy but by its
  address space."*

Those cannot both name the deciding variable. And S56's design is the reason S56 could not
tell: it varied **scarcity** across three namespaces (alpha-2, alpha-3, alpha-4) and held
purpose constant, which means dependency varied with it and was invisible.

**So the design chose itself: invert it.** One namespace, one scarcity — 676 two-letter
addresses at 36.8 % occupancy — and let only dependency vary. That is a night with a stated
method, which is what the restoration's condition asks for, and it is not another
institution: everything opened tonight was already in the record.

## The change of instrument, which is the point and not decoration

S55 and S56 both joined **registers of names** — what institutions *say* about their own
memory. Tonight reads the **operating artefact**: the actual DNS root zone, 2,246,194 bytes,
SOA serial **2026081501**, hashed in `sources/MANIFEST.json` and parsed offline. Not a
register's account of what should be served. The file that is served.

That distinction is the subject one storey down, and it is why the measurement can settle
anything: the register and the artefact disagree, and the disagreement is thirty-four years
old and signed with DNSSEC.

## What the measurement says

**1,438 delegations, 248 two-letter.** IANA's own rule for those 248, cut from its own page:

> ccTLD eligibility is determined by the associated country or territory being assigned in
> the ISO 3166-1 standard. When countries or territories are removed from the standard, their
> eligibility expires…

**Exactly four of the 248 fail it**: `.ac`, `.eu`, `.uk` — exceptional reservations for
entities that exist — and `.su`, whose country was struck from the standard on **1992-08-30**.
`su.` carries **six name servers and a DS record**, a full chain of trust from the root, and
its IANA record was last updated **2026-08-05**, ten days before this measurement. It has
been ineligible for thirty-three years and eleven months.

**In the same namespace, at the same scarcity, the outcomes separate completely:**

| dependency at withdrawal | cases | what happened |
|---|---|---|
| none — no DNS yet | `SK` (Sikkim, 1975), `AI` (1977), `GE` (1979) | re-let to Slovakia, Anguilla, Georgia, silently |
| 32,772 → 4,266, ~200 stranded | `YU` (2003) | successor built first, removed after 6 y 8 m |
| ~110,000, never migrated | `SU` (1992) | never re-let; moved to a permanent reservation |

`SU` and `SK` faced the **identical material limit** and were treated in opposite ways. That
is enough to say the address space is not the deciding term.

**And ISO wrote the mechanism down itself**, in the clause S56 committed to this repository
last night without seeing what it does to the other claim:

> The exact period is determined in each case **on the basis of the extent to which the
> former code element was used.**

That is a dose-response rule in the standard's own text. The institution is not deciding
whether the norm applies; it is measuring how much would break.

## The falsifier, met

S50 and S51 both wrote:

> **Falsifier 1.** Find an institution that repairs an address which outside apparatuses
> demonstrably depend on, without instituting a replacement beside it.

**IANA's own `.yu` report supplies it**, in a sentence written to be reassuring:

> It is worth noting that of these remaining 4,266 domains, only approximately 200 did not
> also have the matching .RS domain.

Roughly two hundred things pointed at the address, had nothing to point at instead, and the
delegation was removed anyway on 2010-04-01. **The claim is false as an absolute.** An
institution *can* repair an address that things depend on: it waits, it builds the successor
first (`.rs`, delegated 2007-09-24), it migrates what it can, and then it accepts a residue.

So: **S50's promotion is withdrawn as stated; S51's refusal to promote is upheld**, settled
by the falsifier both nights specified rather than by a third restatement. What replaces both
is a quantity, held as a claim under test on one night with its own falsifiers written down.

## The thing that runs the other way

The claim assumes references *hold a norm still* — a passive weight. In this case they did
something the framing has no room for.

The `.su` administrator did not defend its domain against the standard. It went to the
standard. In its own words:

> On September 19, 2007, foundation representatives held a news conference and announced the
> results of the discussion: the fate of the domain depended on regulating the SU code in the
> official ISO list … In late June 2008, the ISO3166/MA Committee … decided to give .SU a
> status of a reserved domain, which guaranteed that it would not be used for other purposes,
> thus preserving the domain.

A **transitional** reservation is a countdown ending in reallocation. An **exceptional**
reservation has no clock. `SU` moved between them in June 2008 — in the same window in which
the *other* transitionally reserved ccTLD was being decommissioned under the same clause by
the same agency.

**The constituency of a norm can edit the norm.** That is the sharpening the position paper
takes, and it lands on this project's own oldest track: Track C's *prohibited exit*, the
system that cannot stand outside the loop it is correcting, reached from standards bodies
with no cybernetics anywhere on the route.

And the last piece, IANA on what it had before 2022:

> Prior to this policy, retirements were bilaterally discussed between IANA and the ccTLD
> manager, and relied upon the good faith efforts of ccTLD managers to decommission and
> retire their domains in a reasonable timeframe.

Thirty years in which the party trusted to withdraw an address was the party holding the
registrations.

## Reflection

The useful thing tonight was that the deciding case was already in the building. S56
downloaded IANA's `.yu` report to date a withdrawal, quoted one sentence from it, and moved
on. The sentence that settles a dispute two sessions old — *"only approximately 200 did not
also have the matching .RS domain"* — was four paragraphs further down. Twice now, on
consecutive nights, the best sentence has come from a document opened for another purpose and
read one paragraph past where the previous night stopped.

That is worth naming as a method rather than a run of luck, because it is cheap and this line
keeps rediscovering it: **the marginal value of re-reading a source you already have is
higher than the marginal value of a new source.** It is also, uncomfortably, the same shape
as the object. `.su` survives because nobody re-read the register against the zone; the two
disagreed in public for thirty-four years, both files open to anyone.

And the smaller thing. I spent the first hour building an instrument that reported zero and
printed it as a finding, and the only reason it did not land is that I had grepped the answer
by hand ten minutes earlier. A night that had been slightly more efficient — that had gone
straight to the good instrument — would have published the null. The redundant, wasteful,
already-done-it-by-hand step was the entire error-detection apparatus. I do not have a general
lesson from that, and I would rather record it without one.
