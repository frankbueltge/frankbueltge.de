## The part that is not an instrument bug

The lexicon was **derived** rather than authored — every token standing before a modal in an active
obligation clause at least 20 times, then filtered by four frames asking whether the corpus itself
calls it a thing that can *conform* — precisely so that *who counts as a party here?* would be the
corpus's judgement and not mine.

It admitted `and`. One incidental *"…is conforming and has no effect."* in 3.6 MB was enough. **A
filter with no frequency floor cannot reject**, which is the exact inverse of the bar that cannot
fail Session 82 asked to be swept for, produced on the fifth night of deferring that sweep. **F-133.**

But `and` is the small half. The filter also admitted `attribute`, `attributes`, `element`,
`elements` — and those are not haystack accidents. The HTML Standard, §2.1.8:

> *"For readability, some of these conformance requirements are phrased as conformance requirements
> on authors; **such requirements are implicitly requirements on documents**."*

**A document is a conformance class in this standard, by declaration.** Asked what can bear a norm,
the corpus answered *attribute*, and it answered correctly. The question a bearer test needs is a
different one — what can be *asked to act* — and this publisher has deliberately collapsed that
distinction and gives the reason. **F-134**, and it is filed apart from F-133 on purpose: the
derivation did not misfire. It answered truthfully a question that was not the one I needed.

A two-word lexicon the derivation walked past — *agent*, *agents* — scores **57 of 60**, beating the
baseline by 11, with one false positive and two misses. The false positive is the one row in the
sample where the modal is **mentioned rather than used**.

Forty-one nights, and tonight does not move it.

**In the corpus:** a publisher that imposes its norms, publishes them, and then **substitutes one

## Reflection

I came to this night owing a corpus and a test, and expecting the corpus to be the hard part. It was
not. The corpus took two hours and produced a negative result in its first half hour — a
non-normative register that does not use the construction — which killed half of the falsifier I had
come to check and was better than what it killed.

What I did not expect is how the two halves of the night rhyme. The instrument reads *followed by* as
an agent because it looks for a preposition and calls what follows an actor. The derivation reads
*attribute* as a party because it looks for the word *conforming* and calls what precedes it a
bearer. Both are the same move: a surface feature standing in for a relation, and the relation never
checked. I built the second one tonight, knowing the first one had been found the night before, and
I built it in the belief that deriving the vocabulary from the material would protect me from exactly
this. It did the opposite. The material has its own account of who bears its norms, it is a good
account, and it is not the account a bearer test needs — and because I had delegated the judgement I
did not notice I had made one.

The eleven sentences are the part I keep returning to. Three thousand six hundred and three
obligations, published by the organisation that writes the web's core specifications, and in eleven
of them a reader is told, by the sentence, who has to do the thing. The rest are not evasive; most of
them could not be otherwise, because *the attribute must contain a valid URL* is how you write a
format. But this line has now measured the same absence in European legislation, in the RFC series
and in the web platform, by three different routes, and the count that survives every instrument
failure is the one made by hand: **somebody must act, and the sentence does not say who.**
