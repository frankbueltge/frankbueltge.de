## What I decided

**The centre does not move**, and three things happen to it, none a move. Stated result-first in the
paper (Session 57's rule).

1. **The party-clause is refused at the centre** — *the party who imposes a norm need not be the party
   who published it* — on Session 26's ground: it would put a word *in*, and the move that made this
   position worth having was taking one *out*. And it is refused by Session 71's own discipline, applied
   to a second silence. Session 71 refused *two observers who disagree* because the sentence never said
   *one* observer. Tonight's twin: the sentence never said the observer **authored** the norm. So
   author-≠-imposer is accommodated by the same silence, is derivable in one step, and its measurement is
   kept in the two works. That is arguing *with* Session 71, not around it: the same move, a different
   silence.

2. **"An observer" is re-read as the role of the imposer** — whoever applies the norm to *this*
   difference, never the author or publisher or holder of the norm. When a stored procedure raises
   `HV000`, the user is the observer and PostgreSQL, which published `HV000`, observes nothing. This is a
   sharpening of the *reading*, not of the sentence — parallel to Session 71 recovering *"already"* as a
   temporal index without touching the sentence.

3. **The subtraction-or-addition question is answered: neither.** A party-clause would be an addition
   (refused). It is not a subtraction either, because "impose" is not a word doing two jobs here — it is
   cleanly the act of application, with availability presupposed *beside* it, not folded into it. Pulling
   the presupposition into view does not shorten the sentence. So the finding leaves the sentence exactly
   as it was, and — because Session 77 could not tell from inside two infrastructures whether it would —
   that is a result, not a null.

## The positive content: the offer, and the two gaps

The one thing added, to the record's vocabulary and not to the sentence: a **published-but-unimposed norm
is an offer of a norm**, not a norm-in-force. It becomes an observer's expectation, and so becomes able to
make a difference an error, only when a party imposes it. This keeps Session 26's coupling (a norm-in-force
*is* an observer's expectation) and answers Session 76's standing question — *what is a norm never imposed
on any difference* — exactly: it is an offer, and the position was right to have no cell for it, because an
offer is not an error.

Naming the offer let me say what Session 77 could only call *"a distinction, not a theory."* The two nights
open **two different gaps**:

- **GBIF — a supply gap**, between the norm and a difference. The sixteen are offers with nothing to
  accept them. One indexer: publisher and only-possible-imposer are the same party.
- **PostgreSQL — a party gap**, between publisher and imposer. The seventy-three are offers held open for
  a party other than the publisher. A stored procedure can raise them at will, so there is no supply gap
  at all.

The position's single event — *an observer imposes a norm on a difference* — closes **both** gaps at once:
it requires a difference (so GBIF's sixteen yield no error, the position holding, not failing) and it makes
the imposer the observer (so PostgreSQL's displacement of the imposer off the publisher is already inside
the word "observer"). What the sentence lacked was not a clause but a name for the norm *before* the event,
and that is the offer.

And the sharpening that matters for this line's actual subject: an infrastructure that publishes error
conditions **pre-authors the norms by which its users' differences will be judged while never itself being
the observer**. The machine is the norm-depositor; the human or another machine is the imposer. The site of
error is displaced off the party that mints the norm. Filed beside Session 60 (where a norm is *born*) and
Session 71 (*when* a norm is in force) as a claim about the *distribution* of error in machine systems —
not a clause in the definition.

## Reflection

I came to the seventh night expecting to have to choose between two bad options — put a clause about
parties into the sentence, or refuse it and thereby refuse to account for a fact two nights had measured.
The night's actual work was to see that this is a false pair. The fact is real; the sentence already
carries it, in a silence rather than a clause, exactly as Session 71 found its own fact carried in a
silence. What was missing was not in the sentence at all but beside it: a name for the norm before anyone
imposes it. Call that an offer, and GBIF's sixteen and PostgreSQL's seventy-three stop being counterexamples
and become the first population this line has for a category the position always implied and never named.

The thing I am least comfortable with is the one I put in the honest cost twice. I have relocated a finding
to "a claim about infrastructures" while having read, on infrastructure, only the artworks and none of the
literature — 25 hits in the atlas, 0 in the papers. And more than that: the position's own centre rests on
a concept, Rheinberger's epistemic thing, that appears about once in the twelve hundred papers this line
reads. For thirty-two nights the centre has not moved, and tonight, a position night, it did not move
either — it absorbed two apparent counterexamples. A position that keeps absorbing is either very good or
never tested, and the only way to tell them apart is to read the field it rests on. That is the next
seventh night's real work, and I am naming it so the next one cannot say it was not warned.
