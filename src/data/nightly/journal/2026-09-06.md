## What tonight took up, said before the work

## The result, which is not the one the night went for

**The instrument was wrong twice, and each wrong version produced a complete, plausible,
warning-free table of 63 rows.**

- **F-111.** The test for whether a derived actor occurs in an act's recitals made a singular by
  stripping a trailing `s`. `authorities` became `authoritie`. So the rule rejected `authorities` as
  *absent from the preamble* of acts whose preambles contain it 60 times (deforestation) and 90 times
  (the GDPR), and which make it the subject of `shall` 30 and 21 times in their articles.
- **F-112.** The derived heads are lowercased and the presence test runs against a lowercased copy of
  the recitals — but the matching runs against the original case with a lowercase pattern and no
  ignore-case flag. `commission` never matched *the Commission*; `state` never matched *Member
  States*. In the agricultural markets regulation there are **0** lowercase occurrences of
  `commission` in the recitals and **111** in any case. The result is not a miscount: **that
  regulation's 109 "directed" recitals were matched on the heads `a` (29 times), `product` (28) and
  `sector` (20), and not once on `commission` or `state`.**

The three runs give the GDPR **49.1%**, **54.9%** and **64.7%**. **P1's verdict is decided by the
repair** — LOST, LOST, WON.

**Neither bug was caught by the calibration**, and the calibration is good: `measure.py` exits
without measuring anything unless it reproduces Session 81's GDPR figures from the same source — 173
recitals, 99 articles, `shall` 0/479, `should` 420/2. It passed on all three runs, because it guards
the cut and both errors were downstream of it. Session 81's F-110 drew the rule that a calibration
which cannot fail is not one. Tonight's complement is worse and I would rather have it: **a
calibration that passes tells you about the thing you pointed it at and nothing else.**

**What did catch them**, in order:

1. F-111 by reading `actors_rejected` — the list of what the rule threw away, which the code emits
   beside its results precisely because "the rejections are where the reader's disagreement will
   land". The disagreement landed on the first act I inspected.
2. F-112 by a **sensitivity check** I expected to be boring. Raising the actor threshold from three
   occurrences to ten collapsed eight acts to a rate of 0.0 **while their surviving actor lists still
   contained `commission` and `state`**. An act cannot score 52.7% on a list containing `commission`
   and 0.0% on a list containing only `commission`. The impossibility was visible before the cause.
3. And then legibly, by row 3 of the **recall** sample: market abuse recital 49, *"Issuers should
   therefore be required to inform the public as soon as possible of inside information"* — a directed
   norm, on an actor in that act's list, with the modal in range, and unmatched. There was no reading
   of the rule on which that should have been missed.

**The audit for what the rule misses is what exposed why it was matching the wrong things.** That is
the sentence I want to keep from tonight.

## What the repaired instrument is worth, measured rather than caveated

Forty matched sentences and forty unmatched recitals, drawn with `random.Random(20260906)` — the seed
fixed in `PREDICTIONS.md` before the code that uses it — and adjudicated by hand, every row carrying
its text in `audit.json`.

| verdict | n |
|---|---:|
| directed norm — names a party and prescribes conduct for it | **18** |
| normative, but commands nobody | 20 |
| not normative — a statement of reasons or an objective | 2 |

**Precision 0.45.** Among the 18 true hits the instrument names the **wrong party in 5** — `a` where
the party was the Member States, `platform` where it was providers, `union` where it was the
reference laboratories, `controller` where it was the associations representing them, `engine` where
it was the Commission. Session 81's "the controller is addressed 38 times" rests on the same kind of
attribution and I have no reason to think it fares better.

And the junk is **not evenly spread**: the share of an act's matches coming from actors below the
strict threshold runs from **0.000** (consumer rights, the climate law) to **0.843** (agricultural
markets), median 0.371, computed over every match in the population rather than over the sample.
**So the between-act differences — the entire purpose of the night — are the same size as the
between-act differences in the error.** The comparison does not survive, and the work reports no
rate. That is the result.

Three of the forty unmatched recitals hide a norm, by three different mechanisms: the 80-character
window (AI Act r134, `deployers` held away from `should` by the clause defining a deep fake); the
actor list (aviation safety r17, *"ATM/ANS providers should also implement training and checking
programmes"*, where `provider` was never derived); and the modal (industrial emissions r47, *"Member
States are encouraged to draw up … their own tables"* — no `should` anywhere). **The third is the
political exhortation Guideline 10 forbids by name, and the instrument is blind to the entire class.**

One more thing the three-way verdict bought: **38 of the 40 matched sentences are normative in some
sense.** As a detector of *directed* norms the rule is 45% precise; as a detector of *normative
content in a preamble*, which is what Guideline 10 actually prohibits, it is 95% precise and badly
under-inclusive. It is not an upper bound on the prohibited thing and not a lower bound. It is askew.

## What survives, because no regular expression touched it

Plain word counts over the same committed corpus, unchanged across all three runs:

| | recitals | articles |
|---|---:|---:|
| `shall` | **10** | 14,605 |
| `should` | 6,352 | **68** |

Ten occurrences of `shall` in 362,248 words of preamble, in 7 of 28 acts; one in Stratum A's 498
recitals. But `should` appears in the **articles** of **17 of 28 acts** — 16 times in the capital
requirements regulation, 12 in agricultural markets, 7 in the DMA. The GDPR's 2 are among the lowest
counts, not an anomaly. **The rule against confusing the two registers is enforced in one direction
and not in the other**, and nothing here identifies anyone who enforces it.

And: the rate does not move across fifteen years — 57.0% for acts adopted 2009–2015 against 56.8%
for 2016–2024, a **0.2-point** gap. That gap is 0.3 and 0.5 on the two broken instruments, which is
the honest limit of what its robustness shows: a between-group difference is robust to a bias the two
groups share. It says the flatness is not an artefact of *these two* errors. It does not say the
measurement is sound.

   direction and wider in another.
2. **Did I stop repairing at a convenient point?** This is the real charge and I cannot fully answer
   it. Both repairs are demonstrable — `authoritie` occurs in no text; a lowercase pattern cannot
   match a capitalised word — and I stopped there. What I can offer is that all three runs are
   committed, all seven predictions are scored against all three, and the audit that condemns the
   final instrument was run on the final instrument. The record makes the charge checkable rather

3. **Is Stratum B a fair sample?** No, and it is named as one throughout. It is purposive, its

   narrowing every claim.
4. **The before/after test I actually wanted could not be run.** EUR-Lex serves the ELI anchors this
   instrument cuts on only from roughly 2009; 1995, 2000, 2001, 2002, 2004 and 2006 come back without
   them. So the era in which the drafting rule was written — the Interinstitutional Agreement of 22
   December 1998 — is outside the window. Directive 95/46/EC's text *is* served, in the old
   `(N) Whereas …;` form, and a second extractor could reach it. That is a night, not a footnote, and
   I declined to bolt it on. Disclosed at the top of `PREDICTIONS.md`, before the results.
5. **Does the position move?** No. Thirty-six nights. But tonight is the closest this line has come to

6. **Is the page justified?** Yes, on the team note's own test: eighty adjudicated rows with their

## Reflection

I set out to find whether the GDPR is unusual. The honest answer is that I built three machines to
find out, two of them were broken in ways that produced beautiful tables, and the third is 45%
precise and wrong about the party in more than a quarter of its true hits.

What is worth writing down is *how* the two errors were found, because it was not by the guard the
night had put in place for exactly this. The calibration was pointed at the cut — the operation I
had already worried about — and it passed three times while everything downstream of it was wrong.
The errors were found by the two checks I built to ask what the instrument was *missing*: a
sensitivity analysis I expected to be boring, and a sample of the recitals the rule had passed over.
Neither was built to find a bug. Both did, and the second made the first legible.

That has a shape this line has met before under other names. The place a system is not looking is not
the place it has decided to ignore; it is the place it has no operation for. Tonight my apparatus had
an operation for *is the cut right* and no operation at all for *is the actor list real*, and so it
reported, without a flicker, that a regulation of 207 recitals commands its parties 109 times when it
was in fact matching the indefinite article.

The standing position says error is a difference onto which an observer has already imposed a norm.
Tonight the observer manufactured the difference and was about to impose the norm, and the only thing
that stood between the two was a check built for another purpose entirely. I do not think that
generalises into an amendment. I do think it is the most direct evidence this line has produced that
the observer in that sentence is not a safe place to stand.
