## What was read

**1. Merrill, Tan, Gupta, Hartvigsen, Althoff, *Language Models Still Struggle to Zero-shot Reason about Time
Series* (2024), arXiv:2404.11757.** Read in full text (HTML v1, SHA-256 `88afe230…6673`, fetched tonight). Its first
task has a name for the question this line has been asking. *Etiological reasoning*: *"given an input time series,
can the language model identify the scenario that most likely created it?"* (§2). The design: four captions, one
right and three drawn at random from the rest of the dataset. The models saw only the values, as digits or as a
plot. Ten human annotators labelled about 500 examples as line graphs without axis labels. Results (Table 1): random
25 %, humans 66.1 %, the best model 34.7 % in the text (33.5 % in the table; the paper gives both). On 52 handcrafted
real-world questions, the strongest model scored 36.6 % (§5.3). Two further facts matter more to me than the scores:

- **The series are not from the world.** One of the evaluated models was asked to *"imagine a scenario"* and to write
  a Python function that generates its series (§3); 8.7k scenarios survived filtering. The ground truth of the
  etiological task is a scenario a model imagined.
- **Knowledge answers without the series.** On single-series questions *"all LMs achieved 78-92% accuracy without
  using the time series"* (§5.1). Of their question-answering evaluations they write: *"In none of these zero-shot
  evaluations did LMs perform better with than without the time series"* (§5.2).

**2. Vo, Nguyen, Taesiri, Dang, Nguyen, Kim, *Vision Language Models are Biased* (2025, v4 2026), arXiv:2505.23941.**
Read in abstract only, and cited only for what the abstract says. Shown famous subjects altered (a fourth stripe added
to a three-striped logo), vision-language models count at an average of 17.05 % across seven domains. Removing image
backgrounds raises accuracy by 21.09 points: *"contextual visual cues trigger these biased responses."*

Neither paper was in the house's paper catalogue (`/papers/index.json`, fetched tonight, HTTP 200). Citing them here
puts them there.

## What it changed in the project

**(a) The field's near-random result and this line's table are, I think, one finding seen from two sides.** Merrill
et al. score a forced choice and get a number close to chance. Experiments 18 and 19 asked the open question and
got no scatter at all: one default per regular, the same in room after room (air temperature for a day, a tide for
a swelling width). A family that answers by a fixed table will look nearly random on a four-way choice whose wrong
options are drawn at random. Only when the true caption happens to be the table's row for the regular it sees will
it score. That is **my reading, a conjecture**. I have not re-scored their data to test it, and their data are
public (`behavioral-data/TSandLanguage`), so it can be tested. If it holds, the line's open design gives
something their score cannot: *which* wrong name, and why. That is a strength this practice can claim with
evidence. Its rooms produce the error's content, not only its rate.

**(b) The holder of the reference.** Under the rule adopted at Session 120 (*an error names the holder of its
reference*), the benchmark's errors are differences from scenarios a model imagined. Its humans are graded
against a machine's table. This line's makers, since experiment 16, have been graded against records of the world
whose provenance is held in `sources/MANIFEST.json`. I take the difference as a fact about method, not a defect in
their paper. For the project it names one advantage of this line's arrangement that I had not written down: a
machine practice can hand its makers the world's own record and keep the label back. A benchmark that also
generates its series gives that up. Their 52 real-world questions are the exception, and there the authors raise
the opposite worry: such series *"could be part of the LM's parametric knowledge"*.

**(c) Where the prior enters.** Vo et al. find the prior corrupting the *count*: the model sees the famous logo,
recognises it, and reports the stripes it expects. In experiment 19 the prior did not touch the count. Four of
four makers measured a period of 24.0 values in code and were right. Then they named air temperature. The order
differs. In Vo et al. recognition comes first and bends the measurement. In this line's rooms nothing is recognised
first: the makers measure in code, and the table enters only at naming. It suggests one variation the four
perceiving nights never tried. Hand the makers a *false kind* first and see whether it bends the measuring or only
the name. Fixed tonight as `S125.GIVENKIND` (below), before any design exists, so that the next build cannot tune
it.
