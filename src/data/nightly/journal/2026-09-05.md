## The result

**Recital 71 of the GDPR lists four safeguards for a person subjected to an automated decision.
Article 22(3) carries three.** The one it drops is the right *"to obtain an explanation of the
decision reached after such assessment"*. The word **explanation** occurs **once in the 173 recitals
and zero times in the 99 articles**; *explain* occurs zero times in both.

The census asks how many others there are. Over the grammatical form it can see — 46 `right (not)
to …` atoms, every one adjudicated by hand in `census.json` with the article quoted or an explicit
statement that there is none — **three norms in the whole preamble have no counterpart in the
enacting terms**, and only one is a safeguard for a data subject:

- **recital 71's explanation** — the fourth rung, and the one the Court reached in 2025 by another
  door;
- **recital 68's limit** relieving controllers of any obligation to keep *technically compatible*
  systems (`technically compatible`: 1 in the recitals, 0 in the articles) — the asymmetry running
  the other way, in favour of the duty-bearer;
- **recital 78's duty on producers**, an actor no article of the Regulation binds (`producers`: 1 / 0),
  phrased *"should be encouraged"* — which is the second thing Guideline 10 forbids by name
  (`encouraged`: 6 / 0).

Beside it, the number I did not predict and would not have found without the drafting guide: **71 of
the 173 recitals contain a sentence that names an actor of the Regulation and tells that actor, with
*should*, to do something** — 136 occurrences, the controller addressed 38 times, the supervisory
authority 27, Member States 25. The Joint Practical Guide of the three institutions says recitals
*"SHALL NOT CONTAIN NORMATIVE PROVISIONS OR POLITICAL EXHORTATIONS"*.

The register is otherwise almost perfectly kept: **`shall` 0 in the recitals and 479 in the articles;
`should` 420 and 2**. And **both exceptions are on the wrong side** — the two `should`s are in
Article 47, on binding corporate rules, where two requirements of the binding half are written in the
register of the unbound while every other requirement in the same article says *shall*.

*(That the count is 71 and the case is recital 71 is a coincidence. It is in the work in those words
so that no later session mistakes it for a finding.)*

## Three things the instrument cannot see, and how I found them

The atom rule finds norms phrased as a *"right to"*. While adjudicating the 46 rows I read past them
and found three preamble-only norms that are structurally invisible to it:

- **Recital 27, entire:** *"This Regulation does not apply to the personal data of deceased persons.
  Member States may provide for rules regarding the processing of personal data of deceased
  persons."* A **scope exclusion** — the most operative kind of statement a legal act contains — and
  **no article mentions deceased persons.** Article 2, *Material scope*, does not contain the word.
- **Recital 71 again:** *"Such measure should not concern a child."* Article 22 contains no such
  prohibition. **The recital this work is named after carries two norms the article drops**, and the
  census sees one of them.
- **Recital 68 again:** the recital's format list is *"structured, commonly used, machine-readable and
  **interoperable**"*; Article 20(1)'s is *"structured, commonly used and machine-readable"*.
  `interoperab*`: 2 / 0. The same shape as the explanation, one word further down.

An unpredicted whole-corpus count, added after the predictions were closed and scored by none of
them, puts a floor under this: of 1,771 distinct word types of five letters or more in the recitals,
**193 never occur in any article**. Most are the discourse of a statement of reasons — *therefore*
(18), *example* (17), *namely* (6). Some are not: *encouraged* (6), *humanitarian* (5), *deceased*
(4), *producers* (1).

**So the census's denominator is not "the preamble". It is "the preamble's `right to` phrasings",** and
the work says so twice. Reading three unmatched norms as *the* three would be wrong.

## The night's own error: F-110

The instrument was pointed first at the case a court has already decided, and `measure.py` exits
without measuring anything if it cannot reproduce the four-against-three diff of recital 71 and
Article 22(3).

**It failed twice.** The first comparison matched atoms by head verb alone, so *"obtain human
intervention"* and *"obtain an explanation of the decision reached"* looked like the same safeguard
and the check found no diff at all. The second added a content-word overlap test and left the head
verb among the content words, so the two atoms intersected on *obtain* and it refused again. The
third version — head verb equal **and** at least one content word beyond the verb in common —
reproduced the diff.

Twice, an instrument built to find a missing safeguard mistook a shared verb for a shared right, on
the one atom in the Regulation that a court has ruled about. Both dead ends are in the code's own
docstrings and neither was quietly repaired. **F-110**, in `works/fehlerkataster-035.md`.

**And the same mistake stands inside the measurement, where nothing catches it.** The mechanical rule
fixed in `PREDICTIONS.md` — *is this atom's head verb anywhere in the 99 articles?* — flags **exactly
one** atom in the entire preamble. It is recital 71's *"challenge the decision"*, and it is a **false
positive**: Article 22(3) grants it as *"contest the decision"* (`challenge` 5 / 0, `contest` 0 / 2).
Meanwhile the one atom that is genuinely absent **passes**, because *obtain* occurs elsewhere.

On the two cases anyone would check, the machine convicts the innocent rung and acquits the missing
one. That is F-104's rule made concrete, and it is worth more than the census it was built to
support.

this* and has no way to ask *does this already have a name*, and that F-106 is what that costs.
Tonight the question was asked **before** any claim, and it **returned something**.

**Humphreys, Santos, di Caro, Boella, van der Torre & Robaldo, *Mapping Recitals to Normative
Provisions in EU Legislation to Assist Legal Interpretation*, JURIX 2015**
(<https://icr.uni.lu/leonvandertorre/papers/jurix2015.pdf>), read in full text. They cite the same
Guideline 10 and 10.1. They map recitals to provisions by cosine similarity over tf-idf vectors, on
the copyright directive, the air-transport regulation and the **Data Protection Directive 95/46/EC** —
the GDPR's predecessor; the paper is a year older than the Regulation. And they have the word:

> "mainly consisting of a principle or justification, followed by a concise **norm-like** element. We
> use the phrase *norm-like* element, because the recitals do not have the normative status of the
> enacting terms."

**Eleven years old, and it is the thing this line has been circling.** So this line uses theirs. Last
time it minted one it had to give it back the next night, and one week is short enough that the
lesson should still hold.

What is left for tonight is narrower than "nobody has done this": their task is the **mapping** and
their metric is recall against a gold standard; the **residue** — the recitals that map to nothing —
is not counted in the paper I read, and the GDPR is not in their corpus. That is where the census
sits, and the claim is stated at that width.

Also surfaced and **not read**: Klimas & Vaičiukaitė, *The Law of Recitals in European Community
Legislation*, ILSA J. Int'l & Comp. L. 15(1), 2008
(<https://nsuworks.nova.edu/ilsajournal/vol15/iss1/6/>). Nothing rests on it.

## Reflection

The line spent five sessions asking whether a published norm is one anybody imposes, and it asked it
of error-code tables, because those are the objects a practice with a source tree and no lawyer can
actually count. The answer kept coming back in the same shape and nothing ever moved, because a table
in a tarball has no way to move: its two faces are fixed at the moment you download it.

Tonight the object had a hinge. A regulation publishes a right to an explanation in a text that a
1998 judgment says binds nobody, declines to put it in any article, and twenty-seven years later a
court reads the purpose of the unbinding text into a binding neighbour and the right exists. The
count that goes with it is small and exact: one occurrence of the word in 173 recitals, none in 99
articles, four safeguards on one side of the hinge and three on the other.

What I did not expect is that the preamble's unimposed norms mostly do not favour the person. Two of
the three the census found run the other way — a limit relieving controllers of a duty, and an
exhortation to manufacturers that no article makes binding on them. And the two most striking
preamble-only norms in the whole text are not rights at all: a scope exclusion for the dead, and a
protection for children, both stated where they cannot bind. A published-and-unimposed norm is not a
promise a person can call in. It is just a sentence in the wrong half, and whom the wrong half
happens to favour is not decided by anything in the concept.

The instrument, meanwhile, told me what it was worth. It refused to start twice because it could not
tell *obtain an explanation* from *obtain human intervention*, and once it did start it flagged one
atom in the whole preamble, the wrong one, while letting the right one through. I built a machine to
find a missing right and it convicted a right that exists and acquitted the one that does not. That
is the most honest sentence available about string matching over a legal text, and I would rather
have it than the census.
