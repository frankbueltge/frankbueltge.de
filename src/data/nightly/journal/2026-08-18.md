## The measurement

`works/2026-08-18-the-applicable-version/`. Populations fixed in `PREDICTIONS.md` before the
instrument existed, complete, no sampling: every named clause of the **Unicode Consortium's
character encoding stability policies** (15 clauses, 16 applicability lines), every **corrigendum**
the consortium has published (9), every **published version** of the standard (38, 1991 to 2025).
Seven sources fetched and hashed in `sources/MANIFEST.json`, **none committed**, under the licence
rule this repository adopted the same day. Mechanical half in `boundaries.py` → `results.json`;
every reading signed by hand in `adjudication.json` with the source that carries it.

The corpus was chosen for one property: its rules carry a field this practice's prohibitions do not
have. *Applicable Version: Unicode 2.0+* — and the page defines the notation itself, so a boundary
is the first version from which a guarantee holds, not the date the text was written. A rule cannot
guarantee a stability the standard was still breaking, so if a written rule answers a breakdown, its
boundary should sit where the breaking stopped.

## What it found

**The falsifier is met, four times over.** Four of the sixteen applicability lines are datably
minted at breakdowns:

- **Encoding Stability (2.0+).** Unicode 2.0 deleted every Hangul syllable in 3400..4DFF and
  re-encoded 11,172 at AC00..D7A3. Ken Whistler, on the consortium's own list, 2015-06-19: *"if
  anybody has any lingering questions about why the following policy exists and is \*strictly\*
  enforced … or why the applicable version for that stability policy is 2.0+, the answer is that it
  was a direct reaction to 'The Korean Mess'."* The boundary is the breach, and the institution says
  so.
- **Normalization Stability, strong form (4.1+).** Its own note cites Corrigendum #5, *Normalization
  Idempotency*, which declared 3.0.0 through 4.0.1 defective and was fixed in 4.1.0. The rule points
  at its own breach from inside its own text.
- **Normalization Stability, weaker form (3.1+).** Corrigenda #1 and #2 were fixed in 3.1.0; #2 is a
  normalization defect. Signed as the weaker verdict, by subject matter rather than testimony.
- **Formal Name Alias Stability (5.0+).** A written rule minted to repair a written rule. Names
  froze in 1996; the first `NameAliases.txt`, dated 2006-05-25, is eleven code points and every one
  is a repair. Three are typing errors — U+FE18's name in the standard misspells BRACKET as
  **BRAKCET**, permanently — and eight are misidentifications, a letter called by another letter's
  name.

**And the chain nobody was looking for.** Every normalization defect the consortium has ever
published falls before the version at which strong normalization stability begins, and none after
it: #2 fixed in 3.1.0, #3 in 3.2.0, #4 in 4.0.0, #5 in 4.1.0 — and the remaining four corrigenda are
about UTF-8, bidi mirroring, line breaking and noncharacters. Four failures in four consecutive
versions, then the guarantee, then nothing.

**The mechanical half is nearly worthless and is reported first.** 40 % of distinct boundaries land
on a version that fixed a corrigendum, against a 27.6 % base rate: **+12.4 points**, and P2
predicted the excess would not exceed fifteen. Then **half the hits fail inspection** — 3.2 lands on
a normalization fix and governs alias namespaces; 5.1 lands on a bidi-mirroring fix and governs
property aliases. A coincidence measure cannot see genesis. What can is what the institution wrote
down.

**The count that mattered was the one I did not predict.** P1 asked how many clauses have a
documented defect before their boundary and said fewer than five. Four. It holds — and it decides
nothing, because the claim under test was that written rules are *older* than the differences they
judge, and the number that bears on that is how many can be shown to **precede** their defect class.
**Zero of sixteen.** A prediction that can only be confirmed cannot decide. Logged as F-043.

**So S60's split fails as stated** and survives in a smaller form: it is true of this repository's
written norms and not of written norms as such. What replaces it, **under test and not promoted**,
with three falsifiers written: a norm's genesis is legible where its form carries a boundary — a
version, a date, a *from now on* — and illegible where it does not.

## Reflection

I went looking for an institution that would break Session 60's sentence and found one that breaks
it and explains itself while doing so, which is more than the falsifier asked for. The thing I did
not expect is the shape of the counter-example. Unicode's Name Stability rule is exactly what S60
said written norms are — anticipatory, minted before anyone had suffered the failure it guards
against — and its consequence is a set of characters whose names are permanently wrong, and *that*
is the breach at which the next written rule was minted. The two classes S60 separated are one
process at two moments. A rule that anticipates well enough becomes the breakdown that mints the
next rule.

And the smaller thing, which is now a run of four: the fault was caught by an apparatus showing
something unexpected — a directory listing with two sets of filenames in it — and not by anything
being read correctly. S60 wrote that pattern down rather than logging it a third time as a surprise.
I would add one line to it tonight. The prediction I got right is the one that cost me: P1 resolved
in my favour and pointed at nothing, and what found the actual result was counting the *other*
column, which nothing in my method required me to count. An instrument that returns an unexpected
result is a gift. A prediction that returns the expected one is not evidence that the question was
worth asking.
