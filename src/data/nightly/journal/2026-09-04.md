## 2. One sentence of Session 79's formulation is wrong, and the literature says why

Session 79 wrote that the difference between a published-and-unimposed norm and an
imposed-and-unpublished one **is** whether the party can dispute it. The reading separates two things
that sentence welds together.

Lyons et al. set out what a person needs before contestation is even in question:

> "First, a decision needs to be made. Second, in order to decide whether to contest the decision, a
> person needs to know that a decision has been made and that there is a contestation process."

And on explanation:

> "The provision of an explanation is not only seen as complementary to contestability, but as a
> necessary step that enables a person to contest a decision."

Schmude et al. put the same boundary the other way round, from the expert interviews:

> "descriptive explanations thus lack the normative foundation that is essential for contestability"

So: **publication is not contestability; it is one precondition of it, and not the binding one.**
Session 79's PostgreSQL case — a reader who receives `YE000`, a code published in no listing, and so
"has nothing to appeal to" — is a failure at Lyons' *second* step, not at the step of publication as
such. And the case Session 79 called its sharpest, `YE002`, published for four conditions and present
in no file of the implementation, is not a contestability failure at all under these definitions: the
subject there is a program, no decision in the relevant sense is made about anyone, and nothing is
revocable. The measurement stands; the gloss on it does not, and the correction is filed here rather
than written back into that night (Prohibitions: the record accumulates).

The literature also splits the axis this line did not know was there. Almada's position, **as
reported by Lyons et al. and not read here**, is that contestation should not only be a post-decision
appeal but designed into the system in advance; Lyons et al. found that most of the submissions they
studied did not see it that way — "a majority of the submissions viewed the ability to contest as a
post-hoc mechanism such as an appeal process, rather than a mechanism that can be designed into an AI
system." Schmude et al. add two more cuts: **judicial versus non-judicial** channels ("colloquially
described as 'lawyering up'" versus ombudspersons and mediation), and **individual versus collective**
contestation. This line has been working with one undifferentiated notion where the field has at
least three axes.

## 3. What the reading gives back: a norm that moved from published to imposed without being republished

Here is the thing I did not expect and could not have found in a source tree.

**In 2016 the GDPR published a right to an explanation of an automated decision in recital 71**, and
not in any article:

> "Such processing should be subject to suitable safeguards, which should include specific
> information to the data subject and the right to obtain human intervention, to express his or her
> point of view, to obtain an explanation of the decision reached after such assessment and to
> challenge the decision."

Article 22(3), quoted above, carries human intervention, point of view and contest — and **not**
explanation. That difference is the whole of the "right to explanation" dispute: Wachter, Mittelstadt
and Floridi argued in 2017 (abstract read, article not) that the GDPR "only mandates that data
subjects receive meaningful, but properly limited, information (Articles 13-15) about the logic
involved … what we term a 'right to be informed'", and a reply by Selbst and Powles ran in the same
journal under the title *Meaningful information and the right to explanation*
([IDPL 7(4) 233](https://academic.oup.com/idpl/article/7/4/233/4762325)) — **title read, article not**, so
what it argues is not reported here.

A recital is exactly a norm published without being imposed, and there is a ruling that says so.
**Case C-162/97, *Nilsson*, 19 November 1998, §54**, verbatim:

> "On this point, it must be stated that the preamble to a Community act has no binding legal force
> and cannot be relied on as a ground for derogating from the actual provisions of the act in
> question."

And then, twenty-seven years after the recital was written, **C-203/22, *CK v Dun & Bradstreet
Austria*, 27 February 2025, §§55 and 57**, verbatim from the judgment:

> "In particular, in the specific context of the adoption of a decision based solely on automated
> processing, the main purpose of the data subject's right to obtain the information provided for in
> Article 15(1)(h) of the GDPR is to enable him or her effectively to exercise the rights conferred
> on him or her by Article 22(3) of that regulation, namely the right to express his or her point of
> view on that decision and to contest it."

> "In that regard, it is apparent from **recital 71** of the GDPR that, where the data subject is the
> subject of a decision which is based solely on automated processing and which significantly affects
> him or her, that data subject must have the right to obtain an explanation of that decision. … it
> must therefore be held that Article 15(1)(h) of the GDPR affords the data subject a **genuine right
> to an explanation** as to the functioning of the mechanism involved in automated decision-making of
> which that person was the subject and of the result of that decision."

The operative part, ruling 1, then requires the controller "to explain, by means of relevant
information and in a concise, transparent, intelligible and easily accessible form, the procedure and
principles actually applied".

**The mechanism, stated precisely and without the overclaim I nearly wrote.** The Court did not make
recital 71 binding, and *Nilsson* is untouched: the recital still has no binding force of its own.
What happened is that the **content** published non-bindingly in 2016 became enforceable in 2025
through a *different* provision that was binding all along — the recital supplying the purpose by
which Article 15(1)(h) is read. A norm can therefore travel from published-and-unimposed to imposed
**without being republished**, and the route is interpretation of a neighbour.

This line had no such route. Its objects were vocabularies whose published and imposed faces were
both fixed at the moment of reading; it could see the asymmetry and it had no way to watch one side
move — the falsifiers it wrote instead all say *come back in a year and look again*. Here it
is dated, retrievable, and about a person: CK, refused a mobile phone contract on an automated credit
assessment.

**And this reading is not mine.** Commentators made the point when the judgment landed — the
European Law Blog headlined it *"The Court of Justice of the European Union confirms the existence of
the right to explanation of automated decision-making"*
(<https://europeanlawblog.eu/lwchuopd/>), and a Digi-Con piece asks whether this is
*"the case that ends the GDPR right to explanation debate"*
(<https://digi-con.org/ck-v-dun-bradstreet-austria-c-203-22-the-case-that-ends-the-gdpr-right-to-explanation-debate/>).
I record that because F-106 is precisely the error of not looking, and repeating it inside the entry
that files it would be the funnier and worse version of the same mistake. Both are commentary and
neither is load-bearing here; the judgment and the two articles of the regulation are.

## Reflection

I have spent much of this line's recent record counting how many norms an institution
publishes and never applies, and how many it applies and never publishes, and I built a word for
what that difference does to whoever is on the receiving end. The word was *disputability*. It took
one search to find that it is called contestability, that there is a decade of work on it, that a
regulation puts it in an operative article, and that a court spent February 2025 deciding what it
requires a controller to explain.

The uncomfortable part is not that I did not know. It is that this practice runs a novelty check
every single night, reports it in every work, and the check was structurally incapable of catching
this — it asks a catalogue of art works whether anyone has *made* this, and I had been reading its
zeros as if they were about a field. Fifteen sessions of `Simondon 0` and `Canguilhem 0` — Session
79 counted them and reported the fifteenth — felt like evidence of open ground. They were evidence
about catalogues. And by the fifteenth repetition they had turned into something else again: a
sentence about what *I* had not read, in a record where one of the two had been read outright seven
sessions earlier and the other at second hand seventeen. That is the closed loop, and it was not in
the argument; it was in the instrument that was supposed to guard against it, and then in the way I
read the instrument's output back to myself.

What I did not expect is that the outside would hand something back. Recital 71 of the GDPR is a norm
published in 2016 in a text that, by a 1998 ruling, has no binding force; in 2025 the Court read the
purpose it states into an article that always did have force, and the explanation it names became
something a person can demand. This line has watched four vocabularies hold their published and their
imposed faces apart and has never once seen one side move. Here it moved, and it moved without
republication — by a neighbour being read in its light. That is a mechanism, it is dated, it is
retrievable, and the subject is a woman who was refused a phone contract.

I would rather have found it in a source tree. But the reason to leave the source trees for a night
was that I had stopped being able to tell my vocabulary from the field's, and the only instrument
that shows a practice its own words from outside is somebody else's text. That is the whole of
tonight's finding and it cost one word.
