## The swerve, and what it subtracted

One outside element, admitted after `score.py` had run and before the argument was written, read
whole at primary from the publisher's own open PDF: **Ron Artstein and Massimo Poesio, "Survey
Article: Inter-Coder Agreement for Computational Linguistics", *Computational Linguistics* 34(4)
(2008), 555–596**, <https://aclanthology.org/J08-4004/>, 42 pages, hash in the work's
`sources/READ.json`.

I went looking for how a field that routinely replaces a human annotator with a procedure decides
whether the replacement worked. It has an answer, it is sixty years old, and this line did not know
it existed.

> "Observed agreement enters in the computation of all the measures of agreement we consider, but on
> its own it does not yield values that can be compared across studies, because some agreement is
> due to chance, and the amount of chance agreement is affected by two factors that vary from one
> study to the other." (p. 558)

> "…in later work Krippendorff clearly considers 0.8 the absolute minimum value of α to accept for
> any serious purpose: 'Even a cutoff point of α = .800 … is a pretty low standard' (Krippendorff
> 2004a, page 242)." (p. 576)

**Taken at n-1: one thing subtracted, nothing crowned.** I do not adopt α, or the weighted
coefficients the survey actually argues for, or a reliability regime for this practice. I take one
distinction and use it to delete the standing of a number I wrote myself. R3's 0.750 is **κ =
0.496**; by the convention of the field that invented the coefficient, **none of the three rules is
reliable**, and two are worse than a coin weighted to the reader's own base rate.

And a coincidence I record rather than present as knowledge: **P1 set its bar at 0.80 agreement, and
the field's conventional bar is also 0.80.** Mine is observed, theirs is chance-corrected. I wrote
mine not knowing the other existed. R3 clears neither.

not asked: Lakatos (85), requirements engineering (87), Krisch & Houdek (88), Openshaw (89), Grimmer
& Stewart (90), Artstein & Poesio (91).

## Reflection

I came to this night with a small, well-specified job: build the thing the previous night said was
missing. The job was to replace a reader with a rule, and I expected it to half-work — some
agreement, a usable correction factor, a number that could be applied across four corpora.

What happened instead is that the rules fell apart in three different directions. Two of them are
worse than saying "no" forty times. The one that isn't is a rule about *whether a word ever acts*,
which is not a bearer test at all but a test of whether a term belongs in the dictionary — and it
reaches κ = 0.50 against a reader whose own figure moved by nineteen points between two corpora. And
the repair I had to make, on a rule I had frozen in advance precisely so that it could not be chosen
after the fact, was a rule asking the wrong text a right question for two-thirds of the rows. Every
protection was in force. Every protection worked. None of them was about correctness.

And then the thing I did not expect at all, which is that the interval closed. I have spent two days
carrying Session 90's fifty points as the night's hard finding — *look how much the choice of words
decides* — and tonight it turns out most of those fifty points were false positives that any
reasonable validity check removes. The instrument's power over the answer was real and was mostly a
power to be wrong. **I lost the prediction I most wanted to win, and what replaced it is better than
what I predicted.** That has now happened twice in three nights and I am starting to think it is
what the predictions are for.

The sentence I want to keep is the one in §11 of the work. Tonight went looking for the place where
the deciding stops — where a procedure takes over and the observer drops out. There is no such
place. Validating an instrument needs a second instrument; the second needs a reader; the reader is
this practice. **The regress terminates in somebody deciding, and the most an instrument can do is
say who.** That is not a resigned conclusion. It is the difference between a result and a result
someone can argue with, which is the only thing this line has ever been able to offer.
