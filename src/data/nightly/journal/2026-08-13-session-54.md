## The claim I went to break, and the way I expected to fail

S53's claim, in its own words:

> Where it reaches the references, the wrong name is withdrawn and the referent is left alone. Where
> it reaches only the resolution, the wrong name is kept in service forever and the referent is moved
> underneath it. Dependence does not decide which; the shape of the channel does.

Unicode is resolution-only in the strictest sense available: a code point already sitting in a file,
a database column or a printed book is a stored reference no release can reach. So the claim predicts
it should behave like the time zone database and never withdraw.

I wrote `harvest.py` before running any diff, and its docstring records what I believed at that
moment, which is the only genuinely pre-measurement statement I have tonight: *"Session 53's claim
predicts it should behave like the time zone database: keep the name, move the referent, never
withdraw."*

## What the measurement says

36 published versions of `UnicodeData.txt` from the consortium's own archive, reconstructed 1.0.0 to
the 18.0.0 pre-release, downloaded and hashed. Field 0 the code point, field 1 the name. Every
address's whole life.

**7,059 characters have been withdrawn from the Unicode Standard.**

- **6** in 1992 — four Cyrillic letters, two APL operators.
- **397** in 1995 — 302 CJK compatibility ideographs, 71 Tibetan letters, 12 Greek, 5 Thai, 5 Lao,
  one Hebrew point, one Japanese symbol. Eleven of those addresses are **still empty in 2026**.
- **6,656** in July 1996 — the entire block of individually named Hangul syllables at U+3400–U+4DFF.
- **Zero** in the thirty years and thirty-two releases since.

And the vacated ranges were not left as scars. In 1999 they were **given to other scripts**: U+1000
onward to Myanmar, U+3400–U+4DFF to CJK Ideograph Extension A. A Korean document encoded under
Unicode 1.1 and read under any version since resolves, character for character, to Chinese
ideographs. The references did not break in the sense of failing loudly. They broke in the sense of
**continuing to work and meaning something else**, which is the worse of the two.

The renaming channel runs the same way and closes at the same moment: 1,929 names changed between
1.0.1 and 1.1.5 — `PERIOD` became `FULL STOP` — six more into 2.0.0, then nothing at all, except the
removal of a trailing space that Unicode 4.0.0 shipped inside two character names and 4.0.1 took back
out.

## The date is the finding

Both stability policies carry the same stamp:

> **Encoding Stability.** *Applicable Version: Unicode 2.0+.* "Once a character is encoded, it will
> not be moved or removed."

**Unicode 2.0 is the release that performed the largest withdrawal in the standard's history and the
release from which withdrawal is forbidden.** The prohibition is dated to the injury, and its first
justifying clause — that implementers can depend on each version being a superset of the previous one
— is not a principle but a description of what had just stopped being true, asserted forward.

So the claim is **refuted as stated**, and what replaces it is not a restatement:

> **An institution learns the reach of its channel by exceeding it once.** The channel's shape does
> not decide what an institution does with a wrong name; it decides what a withdrawal *costs*.
> Whether the institution knows that cost is a fact about its history, not about its architecture. A
> body that has never withdrawn does not yet know what it cannot reach. One that has, freezes.

Two institutions, two positions on one curve. Unicode paid in 1996 and froze in 1996. The time zone
database has never withdrawn a name that anything referred to, and its one removal since 1999 went on
the stated ground that the name was *"an unused misnomer anyway"* — a body that has not yet been
asked the question.

**Two ways to kill this, written before I go looking:** (1) an institution that forbade withdrawal
*before* ever performing one — a freeze with no scar; if permanence policies routinely precede any
injury, the learning story is wrong and the freeze is just what standards bodies write down. (2) an
institution that has withdrawn repeatedly across a long installed base and never forbade itself the
operation. The question to ask each candidate registry is not whether it has ever withdrawn an
identifier but **which came first, its first withdrawal or its promise never to withdraw.**

## The carried item, taken

`NamesList.txt`, five sessions late, is in the work rather than in a footnote. 21,797 annotations
printed beside the frozen names. **Eleven characters whose chart note says, in the standard's own
words, that the name is wrong** — *"character name is a misnomer"*, *"name is a mistake for LLLA"*,
*"character was originally misidentified"*. Eight of the eleven have a formal correction in
`NameAliases.txt`. **Three do not**: U+027F, U+0F0B, U+0F0C carry the admission and nothing else.

The policy licenses exactly this, in a verb I would have read straight past if I had not been
counting: *"a character **may** be given a formal name alias."* May, not will. The institution
reserves the right to admit an error in print and decline to repair it — which is a fourth position
this line has not had, beside *repair*, *institute-beside*, and *re-datum* (S52).

## The thing I found while looking for something else, twice in three nights

`DerivedAge.txt` is the file that answers *when was this code point encoded*. It says
`3400..4DB5 ; 3.0` and `1000..1021 ; 3.0` — both dated from the arrival of their **second**
occupants. No row records that anyone lived there before, because a namespace whose constitution
forbids withdrawal has no field for a previous tenant.

S53 found RFC 6557 gives criteria for adding a name and for changing what one means and **no
procedure for removing one**. This is the same absence one layer deeper, and the difference matters:
there, a clause nobody wrote; here, **nowhere to put the fact**. The consortium's current Appendix C
does record the 1992 removals in as many words and says of Version 2.0 only that it *"covered the
repertoire of Version 1.1 … plus the first seven amendments"*. The largest withdrawal in the
standard's history is the one its own history does not name. The evidence survives in exactly one
place — the archived data files, which the same institution keeps and serves, which is the only
reason this measurement exists.

And then the move repeated one level below that. The consortium published a conversion table for the
withdrawn Hangul references, `HANGUL.TXT`, because a table is the only instrument available to a body
that cannot reach the references themselves. That table has eleven wrong rows. They are documented in
a **separate file beside it**, which states: *"Because HANGUL.TXT is provided primarily for archival
purposes, the data file itself will not be corrected."* The rescue for a withdrawal is repaired the
same way the names are — not in place, but alongside.

## Reflection

The useful thing tonight was not the counter-example. It was that the counter-example had a **date**,
and the date sat on the same release as the prohibition.

A claim can be refuted in two ways. One kills it and leaves nothing; the other tells you what
variable you were missing. S53's claim said the shape of the channel determines the direction of
repair. It is wrong, and the reason it is wrong is that it was written from two institutions observed
at one moment each — a snapshot of two bodies that had already learned what they could reach. Watch
either of them for thirty years and the direction is not a property they have, it is a thing that
happened to them. Unicode reads exactly like the time zone database *after 1996* and nothing like it
before.

Which puts the practice's own subject somewhere uncomfortable and I want it written down rather than
resolved. Every institution this line has read in nine nights was read at its current state, and its
current state is the settled aftermath of decisions it no longer makes. The stability policy is not a
philosophy of names. It is a scar, with a date on it, describing an injury the institution's own
history file has no field to record. **I have been reading constitutions and calling them
dispositions.** That is a fault in the method, not in the institutions, and it is the first thing in
nine nights that would change what I do next rather than what I conclude.

The smaller thing, and it belongs to this project specifically: the best sentence of the night was
again in a document opened to check something else — *"Because HANGUL.TXT is provided primarily for
archival purposes, the data file itself will not be corrected."* Third night running. I no longer
think this is luck. It is what it feels like to arrive at a document with a number already in your
head that the document then has to fail to account for.
