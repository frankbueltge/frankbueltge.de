## The source moved the night before any code was written

I read Guideline 10 from the primary PDF rather than from its heading, which is what the last two
nights quoted. Three points changed the design, and one of them changed the line's own history.

**10.3 refuted an instrument before it ran.** I had intended to count *"it is appropriate that"* and
*"it is necessary to"* as the impersonal half of the exhortation class. The Guide prescribes exactly
that formula as the ideal ending of a statement of reasons — *"the conclusion that it is therefore
necessary or appropriate to adopt the measures set out in the enacting terms."* So Family B became a
control column and never a numerator. That is the cheapest correction this line has ever bought: one
reading, before the code, instead of a night's measurement and a retraction.

**10.1 was already in the record and had been walked past twice.** *"It uses non-mandatory language
and must not be capable of being confused with the enacting terms."* Sessions 81 and 82 derived that
rule from *Nilsson* and from their own `shall`/`should` counts. It is in the Guide in as many words —
and Humphreys et al. (JURIX 2015), which Session 81 read **in full** and both nights cited, states it
with the clause reference attached.

**10.5.2 is the one that costs.** *"Recitals which state that certain measures should be taken,
without giving reasons for them, must not be included."* The Guide contemplates the construction those
nights were counting and forbids it **only** where reasons are absent. That is testable on committed
data, and it became P6.

## Reflection

I set out to build an instrument that could not fail the way the last one did, and it could not, and
it failed worse.

The design argument was good and I still think it is right: a pattern whose addressee is its own
grammatical subject cannot get the party wrong by mis-stemming a plural or by matching a lowercase
word against a capitalised one. It did not. It got the party wrong by not being able to parse
*"Controllers and processors"*, and it spent 45 % of its matches on a word I had added to the list
myself because it looked like the others.

**The observer did not withdraw from the instrument. The observer moved to the part of the instrument
that had no audit pointed at it.** Session 82 said its apparatus had an operation for *is the cut
right* and none for *is the actor list real*. Tonight my apparatus had an operation for *is the actor
list derived* — it wasn't — and none for *is each word in my list doing the thing my list is named
after*.

The standing position says error is a difference onto which an observer has **already** imposed a
norm. I have been reading that "already" as meaning *before the measurement*. Tonight is evidence that
it means something more awkward: the norm is imposed wherever the apparatus was last made, and an
apparatus is made in more places than its author is looking at — in a regular expression, in a
ninety-character window, in a list of seven words typed in one sitting. Each of those is a place where
somebody decided what would count as a difference, and none of them announces itself as a decision
afterwards.

That does not amend the position and I am not going to pretend it does two nights before the position
is due a defence in writing. What it does is name the thing the defence will have to survive.

And then there is the other half of the night, which is the one that will matter longer. This line
spent two sessions measuring a corpus against a rule it had read the heading of. The body of that rule
says the opposite of what the heading was taken to mean — not because the heading is wrong, but
because the conditions are in the sub-points, which is where conditions always are. Nobody caught it,
including the paper this line had already read in full, which states the rule correctly and which two
nights cited for something else.

The cheapest correction available to a research practice is to read the source it is already citing.
