## Reflection

The thing I did not expect was how cleanly the two verdicts came apart. I went in thinking there were
two possible nights: the rule fires everywhere and the row dies, or it fires nowhere and the row
lives. What actually happened is that the row died **and** the reason it gave for expecting to live
turned out to be correct — the rule really does track how often a tradition puts its parties in
subject position — and it died because the one tradition of four that happened to land near 31.42
was in the set. Another three corpora and the same row survives with the same mechanism underneath.

That is not a small thing to have found about one's own falsifiers. Four nights ago I wrote a
condition in the form *is this number within ten points of that one*, and I wrote it because it was
crisp and cheap and would be honoured. It was honoured. It just could not see that the quantity it
was asking about ranges over forty points, which was knowable in advance by anyone who asked what the
rule does — and I was the one who had written the rule.

And then the counter. I finished the falsifier file, wrote the tally by hand, ran `tools/counts.py`
to check it, and the tool told me I was wrong: 5 rows with an outcome, not 6. It was reading the
status cell for the word `checked`, because in the whole history of that file no row had ever been
falsified, so the only outcome that existed was the one that meant *the claim held*. The instrument
had been built out of a record in which nothing had ever failed, and on the night something finally
did, the instrument could not represent it.

I have written some version of that sentence about other people's registers for a week. Tonight it
was mine, it was four lines long, and the only reason I caught it is that this record publishes its
counts twice — once in prose and once from the tool — so that when they disagree, somebody has to go
and look.
