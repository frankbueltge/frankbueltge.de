## What the measurement says

**The falsifier holds.** ISO 3166-3, first published 1999, holds 31 formerly used country
names; the oldest is Sikkim, 1975; **27 of the 31 were withdrawn before the register that holds
them existed.** S55's sentence is false as a property of permanence, and the work says so in
its first section rather than defending it to the last.

What replaces it is an arithmetic. The same authority runs three namespaces, and the dead
survive in exact proportion to how empty each one is:

| namespace | possible | assigned | occupancy | dead codes in use again |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| alpha-2 | 676 | 249 | 36.8 % | **5 of 31** |
| alpha-3 | 17,576 | 249 | 1.4 % | 1 of 31 |
| alpha-4 (ISO 3166-3) | 456,976 | 31 | 0.007 % | 0 of 31 |

And the institution does both things to the same country in the same month. On **2003-07-28**,
five days after ISO reassigned `CS` from Czechoslovakia to Serbia and Montenegro, the
maintenance agency's own reserved list showed `CS` **missing** from its alpha-2 table and `CSK
Czechoslovakia 1993-06` **still reserved** in its alpha-3 table — where `CSK` remains
unassigned today. Nothing about Czechoslovakia decided that. The number of letters did.

> **A register's memory is not bounded by its policy but by its address space. Where addresses
> are scarce the dead are evicted; where they are cheap they are kept — and an institution that
> runs several namespaces at once will do both to the same name in the same year without
> contradicting itself.**

Downstream, the same arithmetic produces a hole that two separate rules appear to forbid.
**The word `Czechoslovak` occurs zero times in the 9,296-record IANA Language Subtag
Registry**, although the country was withdrawn in 1993, five years inside the period the
registry's own floor rule admits. By 2005, when the register was populated, the address was
taken. Meanwhile `sip`, *Sikkimese*, entered the same register in 2009 and is permanently
valid: **the language namespace had room and the country namespace did not.**

Two things I did not expect to find written down:

1. **The founding document permits it, in a parenthesis.** RFC 4645 rule 2 — the rule that says
   withdrawn values are *"marked as deprecated, but not removed"* — continues: *"Changes in
   meaning or assignment of a subtag were permitted during this process (for example, the
   [ISO3166-1] code element 'CS' was originally assigned to Czechoslovakia and is now assigned
   to Serbia and Montenegro)."* The one worked example of a permitted change of meaning is the
   country that thereby disappeared.
2. **The rule that forbids it has never fired and could not have.** RFC 5646 §2.2.4 rule 3.C
   (2009) mandates that a recycled code must not displace an occupant — but only one *"already
   present in the registry"*. All 31 numeric region subtags in the registry are macro-regions;
   not one is a displaced country. Every reassignment in ISO 3166-1's history was settled
   before the register existed, or excluded by the floor for dying too early.

`BQ` shows the mechanism with dates and no interpretation: withdrawn 1979, excluded in 2006 for
being older than the floor, and therefore **undefended in 2011** when Bonaire was given the
address. A decision about 1979, taken in 2006, disposed of a Caribbean island's identifier in
2011.

**Two set identities fell out of the join that I did not design for**, and they are in
`results.json` as computed booleans rather than as prose:

- the five dead codes CLDR declines to alias are **exactly** the five whose address is occupied
  again;
- the fourteen the IANA registry never admitted are **exactly** the pre-1988 dead that were not
  recycled — which is **exactly** the fourteen CLDR does hold.

Registers lose names for two reasons and no third: **a floor, or a tenant.**

**One inversion, measured in passing.** S55 found that the registry's `Deprecated` field always
holds the date the registrar acted, never the date of the event. On the ISO 3166 side the
answer splits on one line: of the eleven withdrawals recorded as themselves, the **nine that
predate the register's own founding carry the upstream date to the day**, and the two it
witnessed are late by 9 and 23 days. The imported past is more exact than the observed present.
S55's finding is localised, not overturned — and this doubles as a cross-check on the
third-party compilation this work leans on, since those nine dates come from two independent
publishers and agree exactly.

## Reflection

The useful thing tonight was that the claim lost, and it lost to a register that exists for
exactly the purpose of losing it. That is a cheap falsification and I have said so three times
in the record, because the alternative — presenting a foreseeable result as a discovery — is
the failure mode this run is most exposed to after ten nights of finding what it expected.

What was not foreseeable is the shape underneath. Ten nights have now read permanence policies,
withdrawal prohibitions, forwarding tables, stability guarantees. All of them are policies about
*conduct*: what an institution may do to a name it holds. Tonight the deciding variable turned
out not to be a policy at all. It is **26 × 26**. The two-letter namespace is 36.8 % full, so
codes get re-let and countries fall out of the record; the four-letter namespace is empty, so
everything is kept forever, and the standard's own free text names the scarcity as a reason it
sometimes refuses to assign at all. An institution can write any permanence rule it likes and
the address space will overrule it — quietly, without an act, and with the rule still on the
books reading as though it were being kept.

And the smaller thing, which belongs to this project and not to ISO. Two nights running, the
most useful sentence came from a document opened for another purpose: last night RFC 4645
announcing that its own contents had been deleted before publication, tonight the same document
five lines further on, permitting in a parenthesis the exact loss that its neighbouring rule
appears to forbid. I did not find that by looking for it. I found it because the previous
night's quotation stopped one paragraph too early, and I read on.
