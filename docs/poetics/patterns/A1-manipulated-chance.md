# A1 — Manipulated Chance

*Family A, admit the uncontrolled. First entry in pattern form. Drafted 2026-08-23.*

---

## The problem

**Whatever you are about to make is already there before you start, and it is a cliché. Avoiding
it, deforming it or parodying it all leave you inside it.**

## Context

Arises at the moment of beginning, in any medium where conventions are strong and the maker is
competent — which is precisely where competence is the danger. It is the entry pattern for a
work: nothing in families B, C, F or G survives if the thing being subtracted from, constrained
or exposed is itself a default form nobody chose.

## The forces

- The surface is never blank. "Everything he has in his head or around him is already in the
  canvas, more or less virtually, before he begins his work… the painter does not have to cover
  a blank surface, but rather would have to **empty it out, clear it, clean it**" (Deleuze,
  *Francis Bacon: The Logic of Sensation*, tr. Smith, Continuum, p. 86).
- Fighting the cliché intellectually fails. "If the painter is content to transform the cliché,
  to deform or mutilate it, to manipulate it in every possible way, **this reaction is still too
  intellectual, too abstract: it allows the cliché to rise again from its ashes**, it leaves the
  painter within the milieu of the cliché, or else gives him or her no other consolation than
  parody" (p. 87). And: "**even the reactions against clichés are creating clichés**" (p. 89).
- Knowing the goal does not give you the route, and that is the saving condition, not a defect:
  the painter "knows what he wants to do, but **what saves him is the fact that he does not know
  how to get there**" (p. 96).
- The move can simply fail: "the manual marks could easily add nothing, and definitively botch
  the painting" (p. 96).

## Therefore

Four beats, in order. The order is the procedure.

1. **Accumulate the clichés instead of avoiding them.** "It would be much better to **abandon
   oneself to clichés, to collect them, accumulate them, multiply them**, as so many prepictorial
   givens: 'the will to lose the will' comes first. **Only when one leaves them behind, through
   rejection, can the work begin**" (p. 92). Name the default forms of your medium explicitly and
   in quantity; they are the material to be left, and they cannot be left unnamed.
2. **Work until the probable whole is almost certain.** Before beginning, all places on the
   surface are "equally probable"; the maker's idea makes the probabilities unequal, "and it is
   when the unequal probability becomes almost a certitude that I can begin" (p. 93). Bring the
   work to the point where its conventional outcome is nearly determined — do not stop short of
   it.
3. **Then throw in free marks that destroy the nascent form.** "'Free marks' will have to be made
   rather quickly on the image being painted so as to **destroy the nascent figuration in it and
   to give the Figure a chance, which is the improbable itself**" (p. 93). These marks are
   "nonrepresentative"; they "express nothing regarding the visual image: **they only concern the
   hand of the painter**" (p. 94). Here "chance" no longer means probability but "**a type of
   choice or action without probability**" (p. 94).
4. **Reinject them and answer them.** This beat is the pattern; the other three prepare it. "A
   probable visual whole (first figuration) has been disorganized and deformed by free manual
   traits **which, by being reinjected into the whole**, will produce the improbable visual
   Figure (second figuration). The act of painting is the unity of these free manual traits and
   **their effect upon and reinjection into the visual whole**" (pp. 97–98).

Bacon's formula for the result: "**create resemblance, but through accidental and nonresembling
means**" (p. 98).

## Provenance

Deleuze, *Francis Bacon: The Logic of Sensation* (French 1981; English tr. Daniel W. Smith),
chapter 11, "The Painting before Painting…", pp. 86–98; chapter 12, "The Diagram", pp. 99–110,
not yet read. Deleuze is here reporting Bacon's own statements from the Sylvester interviews and
arguing from them; the interviews themselves are **not yet consulted** and must be, since this

stands on a philosopher's report of a workshop account — one remove further than the rule allows,
and marked as such.

Adjacent, cited by Deleuze at p. 94: Pius Servien's distinction between **probabilities** —
"givens, the objects of a possible science, and which concern the dice before they are thrown" —
and **chance**, "a type of choice, nonscientific and not yet aesthetic".

## Machine translation

**What a machine can do.** Beat 1 is the machine's strongest beat and the one humans do worst:
naming and accumulating the defaults of a medium *in quantity* is exactly a machine's work. It
can enumerate the conventional forms of its own output, hold hundreds of them, and refuse each
by name — where a human works from an unarticulated sense of "that looks like everything else".
Beat 2 is likewise mechanical: bring the piece to the point where its conventional outcome is
almost certain, and be able to say why.

**What a machine cannot supply.** Beat 3's marks, if they come from the machine's own randomness,
are not chance in this sense at all — Servien's distinction cuts them out as probabilities, and
the *Manifest* makes the same cut independently ("nur in Form von Zufällen oder statistischen
Variationen", thesis 13). The uncontrolled has to arrive from outside: latency, refusal, a
third party's clock, an unanswered request, a source that changed under the work.

**And the beat that decides everything, beat 4.** Deleuze is explicit that receiving the accident
is not the procedure. Asked whether anyone could make random marks, Bacon answers that his
cleaning woman "could do it in principle, abstractly; **but she could not do it in fact, because
she would not know how to utilize this chance, or how to manipulate it**. It is in the
manipulation, in the reaction of the manual marks on the visual whole, that chance becomes
pictorial" (p. 95). The skill is entirely in the answer, not the throw. **A machine practice that
merely records what befell it has not run this pattern.**

## The correction this entry makes to our own work

Deleuze rejects the Duchamp comparison by name, and it lands on this house:

> "he has been compared with Duchamp, who let three threads fall on the painted canvas, and fixed
> them exactly where they fell; but for Bacon, **this is nothing more than a set of probabilistic,
> prepictorial givens, which are not part of the act of painting**" (p. 95).

Pattern **P1 (the standard undone)** is built on precisely that Duchamp model — expose,
fix uncorrected, publish the deviation. By Bacon's criterion P1 stops at the givens. And **W1
*Twenty-Five*** inherits the same limit: it fixes the fall (the admissions), stages the apparatus,
and refuses to update — but nothing in it is *reinjected and answered*. The rules record; they do
not respond.

This is not a refutation of either. Duchamp's procedure is a different and legitimate one, and
Mersch reads it as the exemplary case of aesthetic research where Deleuze's Bacon reads it as
prepictorial. **Two sources, two verdicts on the same work** — which is worth more than either
verdict alone, and both belong in the record. What it does mean: P1 and A1 are not variants of
one pattern. They are the two halves of family A, and a work that wants beat 4 cannot get it
from P1.

## Cheap test

Take one existing surface of this house. Run beats 1 and 2 only: write out, in quantity, the
conventional forms that surface would take if nobody intervened — and then bring a draft to the
point where its conventional outcome is nearly certain.

**Go criterion:** the list of named clichés contains at least one the practice did not know it
was following. If everything on the list was already known, beat 1 did nothing and the pattern
has no purchase here.

## Failure test

- **Parody or deformation.** If the result is the default form, distorted, the pattern was not
  run — it is the trap Deleuze names at p. 87.
- **Self-supplied marks.** Randomness the practice generated is a probability, not chance.
- **Recording instead of answering.** If what befell the work is merely displayed, beat 4 is
  missing and the work is prepictorial by Bacon's own test.
- **Clean execution.** If the marks never risked botching the piece, they were decorative. The
  possibility of ruin is part of the pattern (p. 96).
