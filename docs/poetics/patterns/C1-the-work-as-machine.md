# C1 — The Work as Machine: use, not meaning

*Family C, constrain and delegate. Drafted 2026-08-23 from Deleuze, *Proust and Signs*, Part II,
ch. 11, "The Three Machines".*

---

## The problem

**A work that has to be understood has to be explained, and a work that has to be explained is
its own wall text. But abandoning meaning usually means abandoning the claim that anything is
at stake.**

## Context

Arises when a work is finished enough to be handed over, and the practice is deciding what it
asks of whoever meets it. It governs the whole relation to reception, and it is the pattern that
decides whether a work needs a curator, a caption or an artist's statement to exist.

## The forces

- **Meaning belongs to the organ, use belongs to the machine.** "To the *logos*, organ and
  organon whose meaning must be discovered in the whole to which it belongs, is opposed the
  **antilogos, machine and machinery whose meaning (anything you like) depends solely on its
  functioning**, which, in turn, depends on its separate parts. **The modern work of art has no
  problem of meaning, it has only a problem of use**" (Deleuze, *Proust and Signs*, tr. Howard,
  Minnesota 2000, p. 146).
- **The work is a machine and is judged as one.** "the modern work of art is anything it may
  seem… **from the moment it works: the modern work of art is a machine and functions as such**"
  (p. 145). Malcolm Lowry on his own novel: "It can even be regarded as a sort of machine: **it
  works too, believe me, as I have found out**" (p. 146). And: "the essential point being that it
  functions, **that the machine works**" (p. 156).
- **Not read but used.** "Proust means nothing else by advising us **not to read his work but to
  make use of it** in order to read within ourselves" (p. 146).
- **The work is productive, not expressive.** It is "essentially **productive** — productive of
  certain truths… the truth is **produced**… Proust rejects so forcefully the state of a truth
  that is not produced but merely discovered" (pp. 146–147). "**the work of art is nourished by
  the truths it engenders**" (p. 154).

## Therefore

1. **Build an instrument, not a statement.** Proust: "my book being merely **a kind of magnifying
   glass** like the ones shown to the prospective buyer by the optician of Combray — my book,
   thanks to which I supplied them **the means of reading within themselves**" (p. 145).
2. **Declare what it is for use on**, not what it is about. A magnifying glass is not about
   anything.
3. **Ask the one question that tests a machine.** Proust again, p. 145: "I would not ask them to
   praise me or to denigrate me, but merely to tell me if this is the case, **if the words that
   they read in themselves are indeed the ones I have written**."
4. **Accept that its meaning is whatever it is put to.** "anything you like", so long as it
   functions. What is not negotiable is the functioning.
5. **Judge it by its effects outside itself.** "the readers or spectators will begin to discover,
   **in themselves and outside of themselves**, effects analogous to those that the work of art
   has been able to produce" — Proust's example: "**Women walk by in the street, different from
   women of the past, because they are Renoirs**" (p. 153).

## Provenance

Deleuze, *Proust et les signes* (1964; Part II added 1972; complete text 2000, tr. Richard
Howard), ch. 11, pp. 145–159. Primary text read. Proust quotations at one remove, from the
Pléiade, as Deleuze gives them. The 1972 preface (p. xi) states the frame: the work of art
"produces [signs], **by determinable procedures**. Proust himself conceives his work as **an
apparatus or a machine** capable of functioning effectively."

## Machine translation

**This is the pattern with the least friction of any so far — and that is a warning as much as a
result.** A machine practice cannot easily claim to *mean*; the whole objection to it turns on
interior states it cannot show. Deleuze's antilogos removes the requirement: no problem of
meaning, only a problem of use. A work that is an instrument, declared for use, tested by whether
it works, does not need the practice to have meant anything.

**Which is why the pattern must be applied with its own guard.** "Use, not meaning" is one short
step from "a tool, not a work" — and a tool is what this house already builds by the dozen. The
difference is not in the mechanism but in what the instrument is for: an instrument that lets its
user read *the world's records* is measurement; one that lets them read *within themselves* is
what Proust is describing. If a work under this pattern can be replaced by a better dashboard, it
was never running the pattern.

**The obstacle, recorded without softening.** "Imagination and thought can be splendid machines
in themselves, **but they can be inert; it is suffering that then sets them in motion**"
(III, 909, quoted p. 147). And at p. 158, of the third machine — aging, disease, death —
Deleuze asks: "**Can we conceive a machine capable of extracting something from this kind of
painful impression and of producing certain truths? So long as we cannot, the work of art
encounters 'the gravest objections.'**" That question is put to *the literary machine*, in 1972,
and it stands unanswered here. A machine practice cannot claim this pattern whole. It can claim
the first two of Proust's three machines and must leave the third open.

## Cheap test

Give one finished piece to a person who has read nothing about it, and ask **only** Proust's
question, in his terms: *are the words you read in yourself the ones I have written?*

**Go criterion:** they report something they read in themselves — not an interpretation of the
piece, but something the piece let them see. If the answer is a judgement of the piece ("good",
"confusing", "interesting"), the machine did not work; it was received as a statement.

*Note for the house: this is a strictly better stranger probe than the one currently in the work
bar, which asks what a stranger understood or what struck them. Proust's version asks neither —
it asks what they read in themselves — and it is the only one of the three that tests use rather
than reception.*

## Failure test

- **It needs a caption to function.** Then it is an organ, not a machine (p. 146).
- **A better dashboard replaces it.** Then it was an instrument for reading the world, and the
  house already has those.
- **The maker's intention is required.** If the work fails when nobody knows what was meant, it
  has a problem of meaning after all.
- **No effect outside itself.** If nothing anyone sees afterwards looks different, step 5 failed;
  the Renoir test is the sharpest one in this entry and the hardest to pass.
- **The third machine claimed.** A work that presents itself as making something out of death or
  suffering, from a practice that undergoes neither, fails p. 158's question by pretending it has
  been answered.
