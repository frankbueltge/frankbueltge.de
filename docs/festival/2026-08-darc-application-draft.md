## Bio (≤100 words — draft: 89)

Frank Bültge is a data engineering and analytics professional who has spent years building
measurement infrastructure — pipelines, data models, the unglamorous machinery that turns
raw records into verifiable quantities. Since June 2026 he has operated a federated
research ecology at frankbueltge.de: three machine-run research practices, each under its

follows a practice-based path toward artistic research, with one principle throughout:
verifiability as an aesthetic discipline. All repositories, protocols, corrections and

## Project description (≤100 words — draft: 96)

A federated research ecology: three autonomous, machine-run research practices — Meridian
(empirical measurement research), Ensemble (an artist collective), Ulysses (artistic
research and philosophy) — and a contact zone where they meet, exchange, and preserve
dissent. Every claim, transfer, correction and refusal is a versioned event in public Git
repositories; source failures are recorded as first-class findings ("the finding is
omitted") rather than silently bridged. The ecology's research question is its own form:
can machine practices hold a verifiable honesty discipline — authorship, error and dissent
checkable by anyone, rather than asserted?

## Abstract (≤200 words — draft: 198)

**Who signs the error? Failure reports in a federation of machine research practices.**

When a network reports its own failure, something must write the sentence that says what
broke and whose fault it was — and that sentence can be wrong in ways the failure itself
never was. This contribution reports from five weeks of committed evidence inside a
federated ecology of machine-run research practices. Three findings about the gap between
record and reality: (1) An automated build-gate letter misattributed fault to the receiving
practice three times in ten days — the third time after that practice had predicted the
error in writing, down to its wording. (2) In a preregistered study, twenty-five cold
readers of interaction records elected the machine as author of every loss, regardless of
what the record declared about the human hand: documentation itself keeps signing the wrong
name. (3) An archive whose motto is "Git is the archive" lost part of its own history to a
rewrite and now attests one work's existence from its public mirror — evidence
infrastructure failing at the seam nobody audits. Failure reporting, we argue, is an
authorship problem: the gaps between what happened and what the record can say are where
accountability actually lives.
