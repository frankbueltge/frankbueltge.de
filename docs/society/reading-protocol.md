# The Society — Reading Protocol

**Date:** 2026-08-05 · **Piece:** The Society (`/society`) · **Text:** Marvin Minsky, *The Society of Mind*, Simon & Schuster 1986 (Touchstone printing 1988; identical section numbering), read from Frank's PDF copy.

**Occasion.** The prototype shipped on 2026-08-05 with citations verified against the table of contents and five deep-checked pages — but no full reading of the primary text stood behind the staging. Frank named the gap the same night: *"Das hätte eigentlich vor einem Konzept passieren müssen."* He is right, and this document is the repair: a complete pass over all thirty chapters, the appendix and the postscript, **before** the next design stage — so the roadmap out of this protocol, not remembered highlights, drives what the society learns next.

**Method.** Seven reading passes (chapters 1–5, 6–9, 10–13, 14–17, 18–22, 23–26, 27–30 + appendix/postscript), each pass reading **every page** of its range from the PDF, navigating by printed page numbers. All seven passes independently confirmed the same mapping: **PDF page = printed book page − 4**, stable across the whole book, chapter boundaries verified at both ends of every range. Each pass produced, per chapter: core ideas anchored by essay number, concretely stageable material, a shelf check (do the piece's resident agents honestly represent their chapter? what would wake a sleeping one?), and at most two short verbatim quotes. The passes were run as parallel machine readers with a fixed schema; the synthesis, corrections and rankings below were then made centrally against the assembled evidence. That division of labour is disclosed here because the protocol's claims are only as good as its method.

**Consequences already applied (2026-08-05, same change as this document):**

1. **LIFT: §1.4 → §12.10.** The §1.4 citation was fabricated — no LIFT exists anywhere in chapter 1's diagrams (the roster there is BUILDER, BEGIN, ADD, END, FIND, GET, PUT, SEE, GRASP, MOVE, RELEASE). Its honest home is §12.10 *How Towers Work*, where tower height comes from vertical **Lifting** by name.
2. **NOVELTY: §8.4 → §23.3.** §8.4 (*Partial Mental States*) says nothing about novelty. NOVELTY's actual rule — hold what the eye said a moment ago, the difference is the signal — is §23.3 *Time Blinking*, almost verbatim ("Any agent that is sensitive to changes in time can also be used to detect differences"). The boredom half of the rule is the piece's own and is now marked as such. This wakes chapter 23 on the shelf.
3. **TRACK: §16.3 → §16.7.** TRACK is body machinery, not a proto-specialist; §16.3 was the roster's weakest reference. §16.7 *Exploitation* is exact: a drive arousing sense and body agents it does not understand — Minsky's Thirst→See/Find/Get chain is CURIOSITY→TRACK, pattern for pattern.
4. **PLAY-WITH-BLOCKS card sharpened.** §10.4's middle manager does not merely lack its children's skills — its defining job is arbitrating their conflicting claims. The card now says so ("when both shout, the louder child gets the hand"), which the engine already implemented.
5. **Shelf: chapter 8 stays awake through its runtime creatures.** With NOVELTY re-cited to §23.3, no static agent cites chapter 8 — but K-lines (§8.1) are born every time a tower stands. The shelf now shows chapter 8 as awake with resident "K-lines" instead of an agent count.
6. **Map comment: §8.5 → §8.11.** Minsky's *level-bands* (§8.5) are strata of K-line attachment strength — a memory concept. The map's organizational layers of agents match §8.11 *Layers of Societies*. (The visual metaphor stays; the citation moved.)
7. **Page copy:** eleven chapters awake, nineteen roadmap (was ten/twenty).

**Kept as they are, with the nuance on record:**

Kept, with this note as the footnote.

A future dual citation §3.1 + §3.5 would be complete.

- **CENSOR-WRECK @ §27.3** — honest at section level; two purist notes. Minsky's glossary anchors "Censor" at 27.2, and the piece's continuous clamping of an acting agent is topologically §27.2's *suppressor* gate applied pre-emptively — Minsky's censor recognizes *predecessor* states and **deflects** thought "so no time is lost" rather than pressing it down. A future censor that deflects (routes WRECKER's urge into BUILDER's next move) would be verbatim-faithful and more interesting to watch.
- **SUPPRESSOR-STARTLE @ §27.2** — correct location; the verb is softer than the text (Minsky's suppressor blocks the action and forces a wait). **Staging backlog** (ranked; each entry names its essay and its one-line mechanic):

1. **The transfer test (§8.6)** — after enough tower K-lines, set a different construction goal (bridge, wall): only the mid-band (FIND/GET/PUT) transfers; tower-specific plans misfire visibly. Memory as reusable middle, useless extremes. Strongest Act-II candidate.
2. **The dream act (§15.8 + §3.5)** — at night (no stimuli), temporary K-lines restore recorded partial states; the society re-runs the day's grasps and topples with no blocks moving, watched only by the B-brain. "A real child can go to bed—yet still build towers in its head." (§3.5)
3. **Gerrymandering (§9.2)** — a SUMMARY agent aggregates one-bit verdicts up a majority tree; the ticker's single voice becomes demonstrably gerrymandered ("root says fun; 8 of 27 leaves agree"). Turns the piece's own narration into the exhibit.
4. **Attachment learning (§17.2)** — a praise/scold gesture for the visitor; censure does not teach the society *how* to stack, it demotes stacking among its goals. The visitor stops being weather and becomes a parent. ("Attachments teach us ends, not means—and thus impose on us our parents' dreams." §17.3)
5. **The expectation act (§24.2–24.4, §25.3)** — default-filled frames: the society "sees" the occluded block by default, marked *assumed*; change registers only as failed prediction ("We notice change in spite of change, not because of it." §25.4). Includes the finger-on-the-eye ablation (§25.3: silence the direction signal and the whole world lurches).
6. **Trajectory reading (Appendix 3)** — classify the visitor's cursor *gesture*: fast/jerky arms fright, slow/smooth calms. The first thing the society can genuinely feel about its visitor — and App. 2's person-destined agency it was born without is the hardware reason the visitor is never understood.
7. **SCRIBE v2 (§21.7, §22.10)** — sentences built the way Minsky says language works: Trans-frames with pronome slots, utterances derived by difference-duplication, pronouns emerging from short-term memory instead of templates; interruptions produce nested clauses (§22.8).
8. **The realms (§29.1, §29.5)** — the software reason for the piece's tragedy: the society has only a physical realm; no social realm exists in which the visitor could be *anyone*. It files you with the weather. A nascent, empty second panel would show the unbridged gap as architecture.
9. **Shorter pieces, any order:** conflict migration §3.1–3.2 (a long BUILDER/WRECKER quarrel costs PLAY its throne); noncompromise memory policies §8.2/§8.4 ("undefined shape" freezes); Fredkin tiebreak §5.5 (deliberation longest where it matters least); GENERATE+TEST §7.3 (ablate TEST, watch mindless junk accumulate); Snarc mode §7.6 (reward everything, improve nothing); credit court §7.7 (local vs. global reward, same episode); simulus §16.8 (a drive makes the eye hallucinate a block); epilepsy ablation §16.5 (silence cross-exclusion, all drives fire at once); double-arch §13.7 (functional seeing counts one block twice; structural inventory runs out of parts); escape §14.8 (the pessimist's move frees the trapped hand); NEAR §11.3 (space reconstructed from co-firing, then ablated); BLINK's two-snapshot limit §23.3; frame-slot ticker §26.1–26.3 (every line honest about observed vs. assumed); live substitution §28.7 ("did we return the same society?"); C-brain §6.4; DEMON-RETURN §27.1 (an armed watcher that expects you back).

# The chapters

## Pass I — Chapters 1–5 (book pp. 17–54)

### Chapter 1 — PROLOGUE (book pp. 17–23)

- **Core ideas:** The whole program in one line: build a mind from many little parts, each mindless by itself; thirteen open questions (Function, Embodiment, … Awareness) frame the book (§1.1).
- Agents are the long-sought "particles" for theories of thinking, the way cells and chemistry demystified Life (§1.2).
- The tea-cup demonstration: GRASPING, BALANCING, THIRST, MOVING agents each want one thing and none consumes the mind; the book's chosen microscope is building with children's blocks (§1.3).
- The founding hierarchy: BUILDER controls BEGIN/ADD/END; ADD calls FIND, GET, PUT; below them SEE, GRASP, MOVE, RELEASE — and the "amnesia of infancy" explains why these skills feel like trivial common sense (§1.4).
- Common sense is "an immense society of hard-earned practical ideas"; SEE must recognize blocks under any light and occlusion, MOVE must never strike the tower top or the child's face, and FIND must never pick a block supporting the tower top (§1.5). The agent/agency double view: the same BUILDER "knows its job" from outside and knows nothing from inside (§1.6).
- **Stageable:**
  - FIND-CONSTRAINT (rule, §1.5): FIND must skip any block currently supporting the tower — "think how foolish it would seem, if Find were to see, and Grasp were to grasp, a block supporting the tower top!" If the sim's FIND doesn't already enforce this, it is the most book-faithful rule to add; if it does, a ticker line when a visitor ablates FIND and GET grabs a support block would stage §1.5 exactly.
  - AGENT/AGENCY toggle (mechanic, §1.6): a per-agent inspection view with two captions — outside ("BUILDER does whatever all its subagents accomplish") vs. inside ("just a few switches that turn other agents on and off"). Cheap, and it is the book's central epistemological move.
  - MOVE-SAFETY (rule, §1.5): MOVE plans around the tower top; ablate it and the arm clips the tower on transit — a legible emergent failure.
- **Shelf check:** SEE-BLOCK, MOVE, GRASP, RELEASE, FIND, GET, PUT citing §1.4 — all honest; every one of those names appears in §1.4's diagrams. Two problems: (1) **LIFT does not exist in §1.4** (roster there: BUILDER, BEGIN, ADD, END, FIND, GET, PUT, SEE, GRASP, MOVE, RELEASE; §2.1 adds FIND-PLACE). LIFT is a piece invention — fine as world mechanics, but its §1.4 citation is fabricated; either re-cite it as sim-native or rename it FIND-PLACE (§2.1). *(Resolved: LIFT now cites §12.10, where vertical Lifting appears by name.)* (2) **BUILDER is missing from the ch.1 citers** although §1.4 introduces it by name ("an agent called Builder is in control"); §1.4 is its birthplace and it should cite it.
- **Quote:** "As agency, it seems to know its job. As agent, it cannot know anything at all." (§1.6) — "In science, one can learn the most by studying what seems the least." (§1.3)

### Chapter 2 — WHOLES AND PARTS (book pp. 24–30)

- **Core ideas:** The parts list predicts nothing: "Agents by Themselves" vs. "Agents in a Bureaucracy" — you must know which agents work for which; understanding any system needs three levels (how each part works, how parts interact, what the whole does seen from outside) (§2.1).
- Higher levels of description must *add* to lower-level knowledge, never replace or contradict it — psychology needs hundreds of small theories, not a dozen laws (§2.2).
- "Holistic" and "gestalt" are pseudo-explanation words that "anesthetize a sense of ignorance"; subjective questions are also questions about interacting processes, just inside brains (§2.3).
- The mouse-in-a-box parody: containment lives in no single board but in the arrangement preventing motion in all directions — like a straight flush, "only the full hand has any value at all" (§2.4).
- The real MIT Builder robot (mechanical hand, television eye, late 1960s): everyday problems proved harder than adult puzzles; the no-reuse rule "Don't use an object to satisfy a new goal if that object is already involved in accomplishing a prior goal"; consciousness engages mainly when other systems fail (§2.5). "Machine" is an outdated word; brains are billion-part machines (§2.6).
- **Stageable:**
  - The ablation mechanic already staged in the piece IS §2.1's argument — surface it: when a visitor silences an agent, a ticker line "the list of agents predicts nothing; the wiring does (§2.1)."
  - ROSTER-VIEW (scene, §2.1): one toggle that redraws the 25 agents as Minsky's two columns — flat alphabetical list vs. the live wired bureaucracy. It is literally the book's own figure.
  - ANCESTOR ticker line (§2.5): the sim is the direct descendant of the 1960s hand-eye Builder; a SCRIBE line "easy things are hard — this machine's grandmother lived at MIT (§2.5)."
  - NO-REUSE (rule, §2.5): reinforces the §1.5 FIND constraint from the engineering side; one rule, two citations.
- **Shelf check (asleep):** Strongest waker: **§2.1 Components and Connections** — the ablation system itself (or a WIRING meta-agent) should cite it; the piece's central mechanic is currently uncredited to the chapter that states its thesis. Close second: §2.5, since the sim's premise (blocks, hand, eye) reenacts the historical experiment.
- **Quote:** "In general, we're least aware of what our minds do best." (§2.5) — "We say 'gestalt' when things combine to act in ways we can't explain." (§2.3)

### Chapter 3 — CONFLICT AND COMPROMISE (book pp. 31–37)

- **Core ideas:** BUILDER vs. WRECKER disagree whether the tower is high enough; both sit under PLAY-WITH-BLOCKS, which competes with PLAY-WITH-DOLLS/ANIMALS inside PLAY, which competes with EAT and SLEEP — and conflicts migrate upward: prolonged quarrel among subordinates weakens their mutual superior until a rival level seizes control (§3.1).
- The Principle of Noncompromise: "The longer an internal conflict persists among an agent's subordinates, the weaker becomes that agent's status among its own competitors"; deposed agencies keep working internally, "prepared to seize a later opportunity" (§3.2).
- Hierarchy is relative and administrative: BUILDER does no physical work; "To Builder, Add is a subordinate, but to Find, Add is a boss" (§3.3).
- Hierarchies break when two agents need each other's skills — SEE needs MOVE to disambiguate three-blocks-or-two, while MOVE needs SEE for obstacles: heterarchy, loops, and the first appearance of memory as a necessity (§3.4).
- Destructiveness decoded: WRECKER freed from PLAY's constraint "need persist for only one more kick"; smashing has no single cause — quick satisfaction, communicating frustration, or tidying up the child's mind (§3.5). Pain and pleasure both simplify the point of view, transferring control to the lowest-level agencies (§3.6).
- **Stageable:**
  - CONFLICT-MIGRATION (mechanic, §3.1–3.2): when BUILDER and WRECKER fire against each other for N ticks, decay PLAY's cross-exclusion strength so REST or CURIOSITY takes over — the quarrel visibly costs the parent its throne. This is the single most dramatic un-staged mechanic in this range, and WATCH-QUARREL already sees the input for it.
  - HETERARCHY-EVENT (scene, §3.4): an occluding block makes SEE-BLOCK request MOVE while MOVE is querying SEE-BLOCK — render the momentary loop that breaks the level-band diagram; SCRIBE line "neither one can be on top (§3.4)."
  - FINAL-KICK (rule, §3.5): when the drive winner switches away from PLAY while WRECKER is active, WRECKER gets one more tick before shutdown — Minsky's "one more kick to gain the satisfaction of a final crash," verbatim behavior.
  - DREAM-TOWER (ticker/scene, §3.5): after REST wins, K-line replay of the last tower — "a real child can go to bed—yet still build towers in its head."
- **Shelf check (awake):** WATCH-QUARREL §3.2 — honest and well-chosen; noncompromise is exactly what a quarrel-watcher measures. WRECKER §3.5 — defensible (that section is Wrecker's psychology), but WRECKER is *introduced* in §3.1; ideally it cites both. Underrepresentation: the whole cross-exclusion drive band (PLAY, REST, CURIOSITY, ALARM) is a staging of §3.1's EAT–PLAY–SLEEP competition, and PLAY-WITH-BLOCKS appears by name in §3.1's diagram — yet none of them cite ch.3, so the chapter looks thinner on the shelf than it is in the sim.
- **Quote:** "A real child can go to bed—yet still build towers in its head." (§3.5) — "Pain simplifies your point of view." (§3.6)

### Chapter 4 — THE SELF (book pp. 38–46)

- **Core ideas:** "self" (whole person) vs. "Self" (mysterious identity); our ideas about Selves split into self-images (what we believe we can do) and self-ideals (what we ought to be), the latter mostly inaccessible to consciousness (§4.1).
- Single-self vs. multiple-self view, and the paradox: perhaps *because* there are no persons in our heads, we construct the myth that we're inside ourselves (§4.2); the value of a self lies in its "vast, constructed crust," not a precious core (§4.3).
- The Self's function is conservative: "One function of the Self is to keep us from changing too rapidly" — long-range plans need chains that keep us from switching plan-agents off again (§4.4).
- Exploitation: WORK cannot switch SLEEP off directly ("Directness is too dangerous. We'd die."), so it exploits ANGER via an imagined rival (Professor Challenger); all direct connections were removed by evolution, so fantasies provide the missing paths (§4.5).
- A taxonomy of self-control tricks — willpower, activity, expression, chemistry, emotion, attachment (§4.6); long-range plans exploit the slowest-changing agencies, which constitute character (§4.7); clashes between wants and self-ideals produce guilt and shame (§4.8).
- **Stageable:**
  - EXPLOIT-PATH (mechanic, §4.5): forbid direct suppression between drive agents; a drive that wants control must go through an intermediary — e.g. CURIOSITY cannot silence REST but can trigger NOVELTY, whose event wakes the system. The current cross-exclusion band is exactly the "direct connection" Minsky says evolution removed — staging the roundabout path (and letting visitors compare both wirings) would be a whole act.
  - STABILIZER (agent, §4.4): when the drive-band winner changes more than X times in Y ticks, veto the next switch and hold the current plan — the Conservative Self as one mindless rule; ablate it and the machine thrashes, never finishing a tower.
  - CHALLENGER (scene, §4.5): a phantom second hand appears to build a rival tower; BUILDER's activation rises while it is visible — self-control by invented competitor, staged.
- **Shelf check (asleep):** Strongest waker: **§4.5 Exploitation** — it is the book's first wiring-level law (no agent may directly command a non-subordinate), and it maps one-to-one onto the piece's drive band; a STABILIZER agent citing §4.4 is the runner-up.
- **Quote:** "One function of the Self is to keep us from changing too rapidly." (§4.4) — "Directness is too dangerous. We'd die." (§4.5)

### Chapter 5 — INDIVIDUALITY (book pp. 47–54)

- **Core ideas:** Circular causality: WORK and ANGER exploited each other with no first cause; loop-free networks are all "basically the same: each has the form of a simple chain," and we "straighten things out" by ignoring the loops (§5.1).
- Basic questions (first cause, purpose, good) are impossible because circular; cultures handle them with taboo, awe, or consensus (§5.2).
- The remote-control Self / homunculus regress: an inner Self watching a mental movie screen "requires yet another movie screen inside itself" — "The idea of a single, central Self doesn't explain anything" (§5.3).
- We keep the single-Self image for practical reasons — one body, personal privacy/responsibility, limited parallel thought — and "streams of consciousness" are the straightened-out simplification; for psychology the single-agent image is "a grave impediment" (§5.4).
- Style, taste, and traits as decision-economy: Fredkin's Paradox — "The more equally attractive two alternatives seem, the harder it can be to choose between them—no matter that, to the same degree, the choice can only matter less" (§5.5); traits are perceived regularities that make themselves actual, since we must predict ourselves to carry out plans (§5.6); permanent identity may be "whichever of your agents change the least" (§5.7).
- **Stageable:**
  - FREDKIN (rule, §5.5): when FIND scores two candidate blocks within epsilon of each other, it currently deliberates longest exactly when it matters least — add a fast arbitrary tiebreak and a ticker line naming Fredkin's Paradox. Concrete, measurable, and a lovely lie-detector for visitors watching agent "indecision."
  - SHADOW-AS-HOMUNCULUS (framing, §5.3): the visitor's pointer being a *perceived shadow* is already the staged refutation of the remote-control Self — the visitor believes they are the inner controller, but to the machine they are just another object. One SCRIBE line makes the piece's deepest existing joke citable: "what controls the Self? Itself. (§5.3)"
  - TRAIT-ASCRIPTION (mechanic, §5.6): the B-brain assigns the A-brain a one-word trait from observed regularities ("tidy," "restless," "destructive") and displays it; ablations change the trait — imagined traits made actual, and a direct upgrade path for SCRIBE.
  - Card material: the ch.5 epigraph poem (Melnechuk, "Punch and Judy, to Their Audience") — "We seem to form a nested set, / with each the next one's marionette" — made for the visitor-shadow.
- **Shelf check (asleep):** Strongest waker: **§5.3 The Remote-Control Self** — the pointer-shadow mechanic already embodies it; the chapter is asleep only because nothing cites what the piece already does. (§5.6 via SCRIBE trait-ascription is the second candidate.)
- **Quote:** "The idea of a single, central Self doesn't explain anything." (§5.3) — "a thing with no parts provides nothing that we can use as pieces of explanation!" (§5.3)

**Pass I mapping:** book page = PDF page + 4, stable across the range with no drift (PDF 13 = book 17 … PDF 50 = book 54); chapter title pages unnumbered but in their expected slots; every page of book pp. 17–54 read.

## Pass II — Chapters 6–9 (book pp. 55–97)

### Chapter 6 — INSIGHT AND INTROSPECTION (book pp. 55–69)

- **Core ideas:**
  - Consciousness is signal-steering, not seeing-in: conscious thoughts are "signal-signs" that steer mental engines we never perceive, so its "reputation of self-awareness is not so well deserved" (§6.1); what we call consciousness is "little more than menu lists that flash… on mental screen displays" (§6.2).
  - Self-inspection perturbs the inspected: unlike debugged computers, brains lack "interruption machinery" and private memory banks, so consciousness cannot run perfect self-experiments — introspection that works is a well-designed experiment, not a magic inner view (§6.3, §6.5).
  - The B-brain: split the mind into A (connected to the world) and B (connected only to A). B recognizes patterns — A looping, confused, drowning in detail — and intervenes (inhibit, interrupt, refocus) without any concept of what A's activity means outside; nothing stops us stacking a C-brain on B (§6.4).
  - No shared "now": every agent has a different causal history and message delay, so the seeming order of events differs agent by agent; no part of a mind can know what the rest is doing "at the same time" (§6.6, §6.7).
  - Insight reports are confabulation ("I suddenly realized…" §6.8), self-knowledge is actively guarded against because seizing one's own pleasure systems "would be the end of everything" (§6.13), and consciousness engages mainly when systems fail (§6.14).
- **Stageable:**
  - C-BRAIN (future act): a third band above the B-brain whose world is only WATCH-QUARREL/WATCH-CIRCLE/SCRIBE — rule: when SCRIBE emits the same ticker line 3 times, suppress SCRIBE for N ticks and force a level change ("This isn't getting anywhere") (§6.4 closing line).
  - Intervention mechanic for the existing watchers: §6.4's table is a ready ruleset — WATCH-CIRCLE shouldn't just report loops, it should *break* them ("A appears to be repeating itself → Make A stop. Do something else."), and its meddling can itself become a nuisance A must work around — a stageable quarrel between bands.
  - TIME-SKEW mechanic (§6.6): give each sense agent a different message delay so SEE-MOTION and SEE-BLOCK register the same event in different orders; the ticker occasionally prints two contradictory "first X, then Y" lines from two witnesses — determinism preserved, disagreement emergent.
  - FREEZE-ON-INSPECT (§6.3): when the visitor opens an agent's detail panel, that agent is frozen for the duration — inspection visibly changes the behavior being inspected; the observer effect as soft ablation.
  - Ticker register (§6.14): the SCRIBE speaks tersely while towers go well and verbosely only on failure — "consciousness becomes engaged" exactly when the build jams.
- **Shelf check:** WATCH-CIRCLE and SCRIBE citing §6.4 is honest and well-anchored — §6.4 is precisely "B's world is A," and loop-detection is verbatim in its intervention table. Two refinements: (1) §6.4's B-brain *acts on* A, it doesn't only watch — the engine's WATCH-CIRCLE does intervene (tires the wish), so the piece stages the full section even though the band label understates it; (2) SCRIBE's record-keeping has an even closer second anchor in §6.5 ("experimental records are 'frozen phenomena'"), which also foreshadows K-lines — worth a dual citation.
- **Quote:** "Don't connect the B-brain to the outer world at all; instead, connect it so that the A-brain is the B-brain's world!" (§6.4) · "Each different agent of the mind lives in a slightly different world of time." (§6.6)

### Chapter 7 — PROBLEMS AND GOALS (book pp. 70–80)

- **Core ideas:**
  - "Intelligence" is a placeholder: "our name for whichever of those processes we don't yet understand" — Minsky refuses a definition (§7.1); common sense (a child stacking blocks) needs more *kinds* of knowledge than expert programs for logic or calculus (§7.2, §7.4).
  - The Puzzle Principle: a machine can solve any problem by generate-and-test "provided only that we have a way to recognize when the problem is solved" (§7.3); the Progress Principle and goals-and-subgoals tame blind search, at the cost of getting stuck on minor peaks (§7.4).
  - Indiscriminate reinforcement fails: Minsky's Snarc machine (40 agents, reward wiring) solved mazes but never hard problems — rewarding everything active makes "goods" and "bads" cancel; hard problems require reusing agents in different contexts (§7.5, §7.6).
  - Credit assignment is political: "local reward" (please your supervisor) vs. "global reward" (only unbroken chains to the top goal count) — the Alice/Bill/Charles parable; local learns fast and irresponsibly, global learns slowly and cautiously (§7.7).

### Chapter 28 — THE MIND AND THE WORLD (book pp. 282–290)

- **Core ideas:**
  - §28.1 **No mental energy exists:** agents need only ordinary physical energy; "causality is quite enough to keep them working toward their goals." Feelings of mental depletion are engineered illusions serving regulation.
  - §28.2–28.3 **Currencies:** "we turn to using quantities when we can't compare the qualities of things"; agencies likely evolved internal currencies (pleasure as the currency of success) — but every magnitude *conceals* the structure it summarizes and "tends to terminate what we call 'thinking.'"
  - §28.5 **"Minds are simply what brains do."** Mind-talk is about *relationships between states*, not the stuff: "It doesn't matter what agents are; it only matters what they do—and what they are connected to."
  - §28.6–28.7 **Brains are machines that change themselves**; replacing every brain cell with functionally equivalent chips would preserve the mind — identity is a matter of degree, like the plank-by-plank boat.
  - §28.8 **Overlapping minds:** arbitrary boundaries through a brain yield sub-societies; sealed-off agencies "need not share one another's mental lives," like tenants in a rooming house.
- **Stageable:**
  - §28.5 is the piece's **ontological license**: if minds are what processes do, and only connectivity matters, then 25 deterministic browser agents are mind-stuff *of the same kind*, not a picture of it. Finale/framing text almost writes itself.
  - §28.7 as a scene: **live substitution** — swap one agent's implementation mid-run; nothing observable changes; ticker: "did we return the same society?"
  - §28.8 as an interaction: let the visitor **lasso any subset** of the 25 agents and ask "is this a mind?" — the piece answers with what that sub-society can and cannot know of the rest.
  - §28.3 for the lab's counter-measurement line: an agent `TREASURER — reduces every drive's claim to one number, then hides what the number cost.` Numbers as masters of disguise is counter-measurement material verbatim.
- **Shelf check (asleep):** the single strongest waker is §28.5 "Minds are simply what brains do" — it converts the simulation from illustration into instance, which is the piece's whole wager.
- **Quote:** "Minds are simply what brains do." (§28.5) — "Numbers themselves are the greatest masters of disguise because they perfectly conceal all traces of their origins." (§28.3)

### Chapter 29 — THE REALMS OF THOUGHT (book pp. 291–299)

- **Core ideas:**
  - §29.1 **The mind divides the world into realms** (physical / social / psychological) with different laws; level-gaps inside a realm are bridgeable, gaps between realms are not.
  - §29.2–29.3 **Paranomes:** one phrase ("Mary gives Jack the kite") runs parallel frames in several realms at once; paranomes are pronomes operating crosswise through the agencies of different realms, keeping the interpretations aligned.
  - §29.5 **Different realms, different physics:** furniture is pushed, party guests are *asked*; for a helpless infant the social realm is the *easier* one — a request outperforms a trajectory plan.
  - §29.6 **A mind that cannot divide the world into realms is doomed** to seek principles that work everywhere and finds none; the realm-divisions must form, and be kept apart, by dedicated machinery.
  - §29.7–29.8 **Metaphor = transport between realms;** "there isn't any boundary between metaphorical thought and ordinary thought."
- **Stageable:**
  - The chapter is the **software explanation of the piece's central pathos**: the society perceives the visitor but has only a physical realm — no social realm exists in which the visitor could *be* anyone. It files the visitor with the weather. A caption or act built on §29.1/§29.5 would name exactly why understanding never arrives.
  - Mechanic: a nascent second panel — a **social realm that never forms** (§29.6): wires present, agency empty; the piece could show the unbridged gap as architecture.
  - `PARANOME` as a future agent (§29.3): one event lighting simultaneous frames in two panels — the first act of cross-realm thought, and the precondition for ever "meeting" the visitor.
- **Shelf check (asleep):** strongest waker is §29.5/§29.1 — the realms division is the missing-organ diagnosis for "perceived but never understood"; no other chapter explains the piece's tragedy in Minsky's own machinery.
- **Quote:** "The concepts that serve so well for physical objects are of little help within the social realm—and vice versa." (§29.5) — "Every thought is to some degree a metaphor." (§29.8)

### Chapter 30 — MENTAL MODELS (book pp. 300–308)

- **Core ideas:**
  - §30.1 **Knowing is observer-relative:** "Mary knows geometry" means Jack would be satisfied by Mary's answers; even self-knowledge has a hidden observer inside. "The real danger to mental growth is perfect faith, doubt's antidote."
  - §30.3 **Knowledge = useful models:** "Jack knows about A" means there is a model M of A in Jack's head that Jack's *other agencies* find useful for answering questions — including self-models, which are "merely self-made answering machines."
  - §30.4–30.5 **Models of models:** Mary's model of her model of herself has a dumbbell shape (body–mind); the regress stops only because we lose track and say "I just decide."
  - §30.6–30.7 **Free will is a myth we cannot drop:** only Cause and Chance exist; we postulate a third box, Will, "so small because we're always taking things out of it," yet the concept is load-bearing for responsibility — "we're virtually forced to maintain that belief, even though we know it's false."
  - §30.8 **Intelligence has no trick:** power comes from vast diversity — nine listed dimensions, the ninth being "the subordination of thought processes to censors and suppressors"; when anything fails, "there are always other realms of thought."
- **Stageable:**
  - §30.3 names what the piece's **B-brain already is**: a mental model in the strict sense — it needn't resemble the A-brain, only answer questions about it. Ticker: "the B-brain finds its model useful; that is all knowing is."
  - §30.4 offers the **finale geometry**: brain → model of world → model of the model. Third mirror: the visitor's browser as the C-level — *your model of its model of its world.*
  - §30.6–30.7 is an **ablation caption**: the piece is pure Cause plus seeded Chance, yet the visitor keeps reading Will into the agents — when the visitor silences one, the ticker could note the society will experience the outcome as its own decision.
  - §30.8's diversity list is a ready-made **closing tableau**: nine dimensions, two already on stage (censors/suppressors; realms pending).
- **Shelf check (asleep):** the epigraph question answers itself — §30.8 is quoted as the page's motto AND explicitly names censors-and-suppressors among intelligence's pillars, so ch.30 is already speaking twice on a shelf that marks it asleep. Curatorial answer recorded in the head matter: "awake" strictly means resident machinery; the epigraph stays a quotation, and the tension stays visible.
- **Quote:** "The trick is that there is no trick. The power of intelligence stems from our vast diversity, not from any single, perfect principle." (§30.8) — "Whenever anything goes wrong, there are always other realms of thought." (§30.8)

### Appendix (book pp. 309–321)

- **Core ideas:**
  - App. 2 **Predestined learning:** genes cannot encode ideas, but gene-determined connection paths build agencies "destined to learn" specific things — a second sensory system fed by person-signals (odors, voices, faces) "has no alternative but to learn… this agency is destined to learn to recognize people!"
  - App. 3 **Gestures are trajectories:** fast/jerky time-trajectories read as anger, slow/smooth as affection, *regardless of sensory channel* (Clynes' Sentics); genetically wired links from central trajectory-type agents to proto-specialists give each recognition an instinctive emotional reaction.
  - App. 4 **The brain is mostly wiring:** a billion agents with ~30 connections each reach one another in six steps; "the brain of Homo sapiens is mainly composed of cabling."
  - App. 5 **No survival instinct exists:** evolution is a sieve, not a force; the seeming unity of survival aids "is nothing but the shadow of that evolutionary sieve."
  - App. 7 **Insulation before interaction:** "No complicated society would actually work if it really depended on interactions among most of its parts"; distributed memory-systems may explain "the shallowness of human consciousness."
- **Stageable:**
  - App. 3 is the range's most directly buildable mechanic for a browser piece: `TRAJECTORY-READER — classifies the visitor's cursor motion: fast/jerky arms the fright drive, slow/smooth calms it.` The visitor's *gesture* becomes the first thing the society can genuinely feel about them — and SUPPRESSOR-STARTLE would then damp only jerky arrivals. (Pairs with App. 2: the piece's society was born *without* the person-destined agency — the hardware reason the visitor is never understood, complementing ch. 29's software reason.)
  - App. 7 retroactively justifies the piece's cross-exclusion and level organization: insulation is not a simplification but the condition of any working society. Ticker line material.
  - App. 5: the drives have no survival instinct and need none — a caption against reading the society as "wanting to live."
- **Shelf check (asleep):** strongest waker is App. 3 (trajectory-reading of the visitor) — the one concept in the whole range that turns the visitor's live input into Minsky-faithful perception mechanics.
- **Quote:** "The brain of Homo sapiens is mainly composed of cabling." (App. 4) — "No complicated society would actually work if it really depended on interactions among most of its parts." (App. 7)
