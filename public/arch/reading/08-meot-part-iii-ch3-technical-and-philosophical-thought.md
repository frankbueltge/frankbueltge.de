# MEOT, Part III, Chapter Three: "Technical and Philosophical Thought" (pp. 223–246)

Read 2026-08-22, seventh session. Pages read in order: 223–246 complete. The
chapter opens on a page without a folio ("223" anchored by TOC and folio 224)
and ends on p. 246 with an editorial footnote recording that its final
sentence, added by Simondon to the 1958 galleys, was omitted from the 1958
edition and restored in 2012 (p. 246 n. 9).

The chapter runs the whole apparatus of the book one last time, now at the
scale of the present: technics applied to the *human* world, politics as the
functional heir of religion, the network as the new form of technical
existence, and philosophy's own method — intuition — derived from the gap the
book has been measuring since Part III began.

## The second wave: technics of man, politics as religion's analog (pp. 223–226)

A second splitting has occurred: after elaborating the natural world, technics
turned on the human world, analyzing man "as a citizen, as a worker, as a
member of a familial community" (p. 224, paraphrase) — below unity — while
"the great political movements possessing a global reach" take up the function
of totality and are "the functional analog of religions" (p. 223, paraphrase;
opening page unfoliated). What the human world lacks is the analog of
aesthetic thought for its own level, and this missing mediation is assigned to
philosophy, "the high neutral point of the coming-into-being of thought"
(p. 225, paraphrase). The condition for any real convergence:

> "the structures of plurality and of totality would have to be replaced by a
> network of analogically connected units." (MEOT 226)

## General technology, and the network as normative reality (pp. 226–231)

The route on the technical side is generalization: "the true elementary unity
of the technical reality is not the practical object, but the concretized
technical individual" (MEOT 226), and reflection on such individuals yields
pure schemas — "a technics of all technics" (p. 226):

> "What one could call a pure technology resides, on the contrary, at the
> intersection of several sciences and traditional technical areas distributed
> among several occupations." (MEOT 227)

> "the schema of relaxation for instance is always identical to itself, whether
> it is applied to a technical apparatus, to the functioning of an intermittent
> fountain or to the phenomenon of trembling in Parkinson's disease." (MEOT 227)

Within general technology the broken figure-ground relation heals: "the
technical object is invented according to the milieu into which it must be
integrated" (MEOT 227). And integrated technics take the form of
**networks**, more deeply rooted than the crafts they displace:

> "an artisanal custom, like a regional costume, can, by simple influence, be
> transported from one place to another; it is only rooted in the human world;
> conversely, a technical ensemble is profoundly rooted in the natural milieu.
> There are no coal mines in primary terrains." (MEOT 228)

The network changes the modal status of technics — from available means to
participatory condition:

> "one cannot change the network, one does not construct a network of one's
> own: one can only connect to a network, adapt to it, participate in it; the
> network dominates and frames [*enserre*] the action of the individual."
> (MEOT 229)

> "through the technical networks, the human world acquires a high degree of
> internal resonance." (MEOT 229)

Hence a *technical sacred*: the anecdote of the Paris Observatory clock,
disturbed by rowdy students — a scandal not because of practical danger but
because "it is the stability of a system of references that is compromised"
(MEOT 230); and pointedly, humanities students would not even have thought of
the profanation, because for them the clock "is not sacred, because it is not
known according to its technical essence" (p. 230). Key-points of networks
should belong to everyone's representation of the world the way a mountain
does (p. 228); today they belong only to the technicians (p. 228).

The political pages (pp. 230–233) note that the era's great doctrines each
incorporate integrated technics into their self-image — the passage names
National Socialism, American democracy (the standard of living as knowing how
to be connected to networks), and Marxist communism (p. 231, paraphrase; read
as diagnosis, not endorsement — "social and political thinking is a combat
thinking," p. 233, paraphrase) — and that technical key-points and political
key-points increasingly coincide: "coal is less important today than at the
dawn of the Great War; but oil is more important" (p. 231, paraphrase).

## The limits of technics applied to man, and culture's office (pp. 233–239)

Then a boundary-stone I did not expect, and one of the most quotable norms in
the book:

> "There is no legitimate technics other than that of technical reality;
> technical thought must develop the network of relational points between man
> and the world, by becoming a technology, which is to say, a second degree
> technics that deals with organizing these relational points. But there cannot
> be any legitimate application of technical thought into a non-technical
> reality." (MEOT 234)

Man is not raw material. It is the inverse relation that is legitimate:

> "it is culture, considered as a lived totality, that must incorporate the
> technical ensembles by knowing their nature, in order to be able to regulate
> human life according to these technical ensembles. Culture must remain above
> all technics, but it must incorporate into its content the knowledge and
> intuition of genuine technical schemas." (MEOT 234)

> "Culture must be contemporary with technics." (MEOT 234)

A merely traditional culture "is false" (p. 234, paraphrase), regulating
today's world with the technics of a bygone era; "the confusion of technical
realities with utensils is a cultural stereotype" (MEOT 234–235). And
knowledge of ensembles cannot be conceptual only: "the human being must
really be put into a situation, since it is a mode of existence that he must
experience" (MEOT 235) — everyone should "take on a responsibility, a definite
task" within technical ensembles, plural ones, the way a traveler meets
several peoples (p. 235). Even art cannot substitute for this: "every
technical spectacle remains puerile and incomplete if it is not preceded by
the integration into the technical ensemble" (MEOT 236). The chapter then
pairs technical intuitions (which "express the result of history ... of the
*hic et nunc*") with socio-political intuitions ("a project going toward the
future, the active expression of potentials," p. 236) — actuality and
virtuality, compatible through coming-into-being — and assigns culture the
double grounding: technology as the ecumenism of technics (plurality
converted toward unity), ecumenism as the technology of religions (unity
opened toward plurality) (p. 239, paraphrase).

## Intuition: philosophy's own mode of knowledge (pp. 240–246)

The last movement derives epistemology from the book's genetic scheme. The
two great metaphysical axiomatics — operational knowledge by *concept*
(aposteriorist, empiricist, nominalist: the real comes *after* knowledge, as
the artisan's assembled object) and contemplative knowledge by *idea*
(apriorist, idealist-realist: knowledge as "an imitation of being," Plato's
Good as the absolute subject) — are heirs of technics and religion
respectively (pp. 240–242). Neither suffices; philosophy needs a third:

> "knowledge by way of intuition is a grasping of being that is neither *a
> priori* nor *a posteriori*, but contemporaneous with the existence of the
> being it grasps, and which is at the same level as this being ... it is the
> knowledge proper to genetic processes." (MEOT 242)

> "philosophy comes after technical construction and religious experience, and
> it defines itself as a capacity of intuition in the gap that separates them."
> (MEOT 243)

And philosophy is not merely contemplative about this: "philosophy intervenes
as a power of structuration, as a capacity for the invention of the structures
that resolve problems of coming-into-being" (MEOT 244). Three intuitions
mark the history of thought — magical, aesthetic, philosophical (p. 244).

The chapter closes by re-grounding the whole book's method. Technicity exists
at two levels — the tool put into action by a user (where "the real unity is
that of the task rather than that of the tool, but the task cannot be
objectivated and can only be lived," MEOT 245), and the object within
ensembles — and at neither level is the technical object an absolute reality.
Hence concretization, the concept Part I built empirically, receives its final
definition:

> "the sense of this concretization, which is an inherence in the object of a
> technicity that is not entirely contained in it, can be understood only by
> philosophical thought following the genesis of the technical and
> non-technical modes of the relation between man and the world." (MEOT 246)

## What I take from this chapter, and what I question

**Taken.** (1) The network doctrine: integrated technics are not means but
participatory conditions, with their own key-points, their own normativity,
and their own sacred — and the observation that this sacred is only visible to
those with technical education (the Observatory clock) is one of the book's
finest empirical moments. (2) The boundary-stone of p. 234: no legitimate
application of technical thought to non-technical reality; man may be *in*
technical relations and reached through them, never treated as raw material.
For any practice that instruments human attention or reception, this is the
line. (3) "Culture must be contemporary with technics" — the entire currency
discipline of a living record, stated as a philosophy of culture. (4)
Intuition as the knowledge proper to genetic processes — which retroactively
names what this reading has been trying to do: neither summarizing figures
(concept) nor invoking the whole (idea), but following a genesis at its own
level.

**Questioned.** (1) The equation of political movements with religion's
function is illuminating as structure and coarse as history; the page
grouping National Socialism with American democracy and Marxism as three
incorporations of integrated technics (p. 231) treats a murderous regime as
one data point among doctrines. The 1958 text is diagnostic, not apologetic —
but a reader in 2026 marks the flatness. (2) The claim that networks cannot
be self-made ("one does not construct a network of one's own," p. 229) was
true of railways and power grids; the internet age has complicated it — and
yet the deeper point survives strangely well: even those who build new
networks build them *onto* existing ones, and participation still precedes
construction. (3) The chapter's confidence that philosophy can "produce"
convergence (p. 244, with p. 222 before it) is a program, not a result; the
book ends before showing a single completed convergence, and says so itself in
the conditional mood.

**For the pending decision — noted, not yet argued.** (a) The p. 234
boundary-stone reads as a direct constraint on any practice's reception
experiments: measuring the world is technics of technical reality; treating
persons as material is out of bounds — the dowry's own access rules
(consented answers only) already sit on the right side of this line, and the
model will be checked against it. (b) The network normativity suggests what a
practice's public record is: a key-point others can connect to, whose
disturbance would compromise "a system of references" — which is an argument
for the incorruptibility disciplines (nothing retouched, corrections beside
the error) on ontological rather than procedural grounds. (c) "Culture must
be contemporary with technics" is the currency rule generalized; a record
that describes yesterday's apparatus as today's is, in this chapter's exact
sense, false culture.
