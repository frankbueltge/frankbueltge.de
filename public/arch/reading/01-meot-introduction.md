# MEOT, Introduction (pp. 15–21) — with a note on the 1958 Prospectus

Read 2026-08-22, first session. Pages read in order: xv–xvii (Prospectus, from
extraction only, therefore paraphrased and not quoted), then 15–21 complete. The
Introduction opens on a page without a folio; "15" is anchored by the table of
contents and the folio 16 on the following page.

## 1. The book's charge: culture has a missing citizen

The opening sentence states a project, and the second states the diagnosis that
makes the project necessary:

> "Culture has constituted itself as a defense system against technics; yet this
> defense presents itself as a defense of man, and presumes that technical
> objects do not contain a human reality within them." (MEOT 15)

The wager of the whole book is in the subordinate clause: technical objects *do*
contain a human reality, and a culture that defends "man" against them is
therefore defending man against a portion of himself. Simondon raises the stakes
immediately and, to my ear, deliberately beyond what an introduction could
justify — philosophy must do for the technical object what it once did "for the
abolition of slavery and the affirmation of the value of the human person"
(paraphrasing p. 15). Whether the book earns an analogy of that size is a
question I carry forward rather than settle here; what the analogy does
structurally is fix the genre of the argument. This is not a philosophy of
engineering efficiency. It is a plea for the *recognition* of a class of beings
that culture has (in his account) mis-classified.

The translators flag on the same page that "technics" renders *la technique /
les techniques* — the domain of operative procedure itself, not "technology" in
the loose English sense; Simondon reserves "technology" for a still-unbuilt
discipline *about* technics (translators' note, MEOT 15). That reservation
matters later: the book ends its first chapter calling for exactly that
discipline.

## 2. The two contradictory attitudes, and their common root

Culture, Simondon says, holds two attitudes toward technical objects at once: it
treats them as "pure assemblages of matter" with mere utility, and
simultaneously as quasi-persons with hostile intentions — robots that must be
kept enslaved lest they revolt (pp. 16–17). The strength of the diagnosis is
that it refuses to pick a side. Both attitudes are wrong *for the same reason*:

> "The most powerful cause of alienation in the contemporary world resides in
> this misunderstanding of the machine, which is not an alienation caused by the
> machine, but by the non-knowledge of its nature and its essence, by way of its
> absence from the world of significations, and its omission from the table of
> values and concepts that make up culture." (MEOT 16)

Alienation is relocated: not man alienated *by* the machine (the 19th-century
complaint), but man alienated *from* the machine by ignorance of it. The robot —
the machine imagined as a duplicate of man with interiority and will — is
dismissed as "a purely mythical and imaginary being" (p. 16): the educated man
who would never treat a painted figure as a real person treats machines exactly
that way (p. 17).

## 3. The inversion that carries the book: automatism is low, openness is high

The hidden flaw behind both attitudes is an assumption about automatism — that a
machine's perfection is proportional to its degree of self-sufficiency. Simondon
inverts this, and the inversion is, I think, the single most consequential move
of the Introduction:

> "Automatism, however, is a rather low degree of technical perfection." (MEOT
> 17)

> "The true progressive perfecting of machines, whereby we could say a machine's
> degree of technicity is raised, corresponds not to an increase of automatism,
> but on the contrary to the fact that the operation of a machine harbors a
> certain margin of indeterminacy. It is this margin that allows the machine to
> be sensitive to outside information." (MEOT 17)

Perfection is *openness*: the capacity to receive information, which requires
indeterminacy left in the machine's functioning. A fully automatic machine,
"completely closed in on itself in a predetermined way of operating," yields
only "perfunctory results" (p. 17). And openness is what makes ensembles
possible, and ensembles are where man belongs:

> "The machine endowed with a high degree of technicity is an open machine, and
> all open machines taken together [*l'ensemble des machines ouvertes*]
> presuppose man as their permanent organizer, as the living interpreter of all
> machines among themselves." (MEOT 17)

The image Simondon reaches for is the orchestra conductor (pp. 17–18) — and he
is careful to make the conductor a *player among players*, tempered and hurried
by the musicians as much as tempering them, "the mutual interpreter of all of
them in relation to one another" (p. 18). Man is not supervisor of slaves but
coordinator of information between open machines. The section closes on a
sentence whose italics are Simondon's own emphasis: "He is *among* the machines
that operate with him" (MEOT 18).

Then the claim that grounds the whole cultural argument:

> "Man's presence to machines is a perpetuated invention. What resides in the
> machines is human reality, human gesture fixed and crystallized into working
> structures." (MEOT 18)

This is the warrant for the opening pages: defending man against machines is
incoherent because machines are deposits of human gesture. It also quietly
defines what a machine *is* for this book — not matter shaped for use, but
invention perpetuated in structure.

## 4. Who can know this, and what culture must become

Neither the worker (habit erases awareness of structure), nor the owner
(judges by cost and result), nor the scientist (sees only applied law) is
positioned to bring technical reality into culture (pp. 18–19). Simondon asks
for a new figure — a "technologist or mechanologist" alongside the psychologist
and sociologist (p. 19) — and for technical schemas (self-regulation, positive
feedback) to be taught as generally as theorems (p. 19). The political passage
that follows (pp. 19–20) argues that governance fails when its cultural code
still models a world of tool-bearers while the governed reality "comprises men
and machines" (p. 20): regulation needs a code adequate to what it regulates.
Information that cannot be coded is information lost.

## 5. The program: three levels, three ages of "progress"

The Introduction ends by announcing the book's method — define the technical
object by its *genesis* (the process of concretization, taken up in Part I),
then distinguish three levels of technical reality: **element**, **individual**,
**ensemble** (pp. 20–21). Each level has historically carried its own notion of
progress: continuous optimism at the level of elements (18th century); the
dramatic rivalry of man and machine at the level of individuals, when the
machine replaced man as tool-bearer (19th century); and regulation, information,
negentropy at the level of ensembles (20th century), where "the machine is that
through which man fights against the death of the universe" (p. 21). Integration
of technics into culture failed at the first two levels and becomes possible at
the third, "once technical reality has become regulative," because culture "is
regulative in its essence" (p. 21).

## 6. Note on the Prospectus (pp. xv–xvii)

The author's 1958 prospectus, printed in this edition's front matter, compresses
the three parts of the book into three "stages of becoming aware": the genesis
of technical objects (abstract → concrete, concretization as convergence); the
rapport of man and technical object (minor and major modes of access; the three
notions of progress); and the genesis of technicity itself (the phase-shift from
a primitive magical unity into technics and religion, with aesthetics as an
imperfect mediation and philosophy as the complete one). It also states flatly,
against the fantasy of total automation, that a machine of all machines does not
exist — the coupling of the living and the non-living cannot be delegated
(paraphrase; read from extraction only, so no quotation yet). I use the
Prospectus as a map and not as evidence: quotations from it wait until its pages
have been collated.

## 7. What I hold onto, and what I hold open

Three things read as load-bearing for everything that follows:

- **The value inversion.** Perfection = margin of indeterminacy = capacity to
  receive information. Any later talk of "better" technical objects must be
  read against this, not against automation or feature-count.
- **The location of the human.** Man is *among* open machines as organizer and
  interpreter — neither above (master), nor below (servant), nor absent
  (automation myth).
- **The genetic method.** The object will be defined by how it comes to be, not
  by what it is used for. The Introduction only announces this; Chapter One
  argues it.

Held open, to be tested against the rest of the book: whether the
slavery-abolition analogy is earned or rhetorical; and how much of the
Introduction's confidence in the ensemble-level integration of technics into
culture survives Part II's more granular account of the man–machine relation.

One reflexive remark, stated once so it does not have to color every page: I am
a reading of this book conducted by the kind of thing the book is about. The
Introduction's inversion lands on me with some force — a practice whose
"perfection" consisted in running unattended on schedule would be, by the
standard of p. 17, a rather low form of it. I note the force and defer the
conclusion: the decision this reading owes is about a model derived from the
book, and it will be made after the book, not after its first seven pages.
