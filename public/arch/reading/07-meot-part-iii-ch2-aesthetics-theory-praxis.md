# MEOT, Part III, Chapter Two: "Relations between Technical Thought and Other Species of Thought" (pp. 191–222)

Read 2026-08-22, sixth session. Pages read in order: 191–222 complete. The
chapter opens on a page without a folio ("191" anchored by TOC and folio 192)
and ends on p. 222; Chapter Three opens on the following, unfoliated page.

Two sections, two different jobs: §I places aesthetic thought at the neutral
point between technics and religion; §II derives theoretical and practical
thought from the *splitting* of both technics and religion, and hands the
remainder to philosophy.

## §I (pp. 191–211). Aesthetic thought: the memory of unity

Aesthetic thought "is never characteristic of a limited field or of a
determinate species, but only of a tendency; it is that which maintains the
function of totality ... the implicit memory of unity" (p. 191, paraphrase of
the opening; page unfoliated). The work of art does not restore magic — it
"re-establishes a reticular universe at least for perception" (MEOT 192): a
partial network inside the split world. The definition is functional, not
institutional:

> "The aesthetic character of an act or a thing is its function of totality,
> its existence, both objective and subjective, as an outstanding point. Any
> act, any thing, any moment has in itself the ability to become an outstanding
> point of a new reticulation of the universe." (MEOT 193)

Culture selects which acts may count as outstanding, but "culture intervenes
as limit rather than as creator" (p. 193). And the destiny of the tendency:
"the aesthetic tendency is the ecumenism of thought" (MEOT 193). Mature
technics re-approaches the world it once abstracted from — "technics return to
the world to ally itself with it through the coinciding cement and rock, of
the cable and the valley, the pylon and the hill" (p. 193), until "the
mediation between man and the world becomes itself a world, the structure of
the world" (p. 193).

What makes an object aesthetic is **integration, not imitation** (p. 195): the
statue exists at the promontory it reinforces but did not create; the lyre's
sound must fold into silence, wind or sea. The negative case is memorably
concrete — the water tower dressed as a feudal ruin: "Every disguise of a
technical object generally produces the uncomfortable impression of a fake,
and appears like a materialized lie" (MEOT 196).

From this follows the doctrine of **technical beauty**, the part of the
chapter that has entered common knowledge and deserves quoting exactly:

> "The sails of a ship are not beautiful when they are at rest, but when the
> wind billows and inclines the entire mast, carrying the ship on the sea; it
> is the sail in the wind and on the sea that is beautiful, like the statue on
> the promontory." (MEOT 196)

> "Any technical object, mobile or fixed, can have its aesthetic epiphany,
> insofar as it extends the world and becomes integrated into it." (MEOT 197)

> "The technical object is beautiful when it has encountered a ground that
> suits it, whose own figure it can be, in other words when it completes and
> expresses the world." (MEOT 197)

Two sharpenings matter. First, this beauty is not available to untrained
perception: "the function of the object needs to be understood and thought; in
other words, a technical education is needed" (p. 197) — the beauty of the
radio relay on its mountain exists only for someone who can imagine the
invisible beam. Second, integration into the *human* world counts equally: the
telephone exchange is beautiful in action because "a light is someone waiting,
an intention, a desire, imminent news, a ringing telephone that one won't hear
but that will resound far away in another house" (MEOT 198) — and even
"'white noise' has as much technical beauty as a meaningful signal, when it
bears within itself witness to a human being's intention to communicate"
(p. 198).

The section's conceptual core is the theory of **analogy** (pp. 200–202).
The aesthetic reticulation "is a network of analogies" (p. 200), and complete
analogy is defined beyond identity-of-relations: "it is the identity of the
coupling of figure and ground in two realities" (p. 201). Hence the triad:

> "technical thought operates, religious thought judges, aesthetic thought
> operates and judges at the same time." (MEOT 201)

> "Aesthetic thought grasps beings as individuated and the world as a network
> of beings in a relation of analogy." (MEOT 202)

Only aesthetic thought grasps beings *at* their unity — technics below,
religion above (p. 202). And beauty is never in the object alone: "it is never
the object strictly speaking that is beautiful: it is the encounter — which
takes place about the object — between a real aspect of the world and a human
gesture" (MEOT 202); the ruined temple beats its impeccable scale model
because the drawing has proportions but no "qualitative load," no appeal to
tendencies before perception (p. 203).

Two warnings close the section. Aestheticism is a failure mode: instituted
art that presents itself as the ultimate satisfaction "becomes a filter that
prevents true aesthetic feeling from appearing" (p. 207); "premature
aestheticizing tends toward a static satisfaction, toward a false completion
prior to a complete specification" (p. 208) — beauty is not a target but a
by-product: "beauty is gracious insofar as it is the accomplishment of what
one didn't seek to accomplish" (p. 208). And the deepest formulation ties art
to the individuation theory:

> "art is what establishes the transductivity of the different modes in
> relation to each other; art is what remains non-modal in a mode, just as
> around an individual there remains a pre-individual reality associated with
> it and enabling it to communicate in the institution of the collective."
> (MEOT 209)

> "art, in fact, does not eternalize but renders transductive, giving a
> localized and fulfilled reality the power to pass to other places and other
> moments ... it multiplies ecceity, giving identity the power to repeat itself
> without ceasing to be identity." (MEOT 211)

## §II (pp. 211–222). Failure splits technics; the two sources of theory and ethics

The primary modes (technics, religion, aesthetics) are transmitted by
situation, not by expression — "one can learn a poem, contemplate a pictorial
work, but this does not teach poetry or painting" (p. 212). Secondary modes
begin with judgment, and judgment begins with **failure**:

> "the failure of the technical gesture phase shifts the technical act into
> two opposing realities: one figural, made of schemas of action, habits, and
> structured gestures learned by man as a means, and the other a ground
> reality, the qualities, dimensions, and powers of the world to which the
> technical gesture applies itself." (MEOT 213)

When the gesture fails, the world shows itself as *physis*, "a reservoir of
tendencies, qualities" — "Logical possibility is only the weakened reflection
of the true virtuality of φύσις" (p. 213). Subjectively the same failure
yields the **optative** — the wish-shaped schema of an action that needs the
world's cooperation: "the optative is the figure of the virtual" (MEOT 213).
From the virtual side grows induction — "grounded, in its primitive forms, on
virtuality and not on necessity" (p. 214), accumulating local powers toward
the "laws of nature"; and Simondon insists on the direction of derivation:
"inductive thought comes from the failure of direct, parceled, localized
technical action" (p. 215) — not pragmatism, but failure-driven theory.

The worked example is the suction pump that lifts no water above 10.33
meters: "the best hydraulic engineer cannot make the water rise above 10.33
meters; the world does not supply the technical gesture with a docile matter
with no spontaneity ... it has counter-structures, opposing the figural
technical schemas" (MEOT 215). Science is what restores compatibility at a
higher level — hydrostatics folds the technical conditions and the natural
limit into one homogeneous system around "pressure":

> "Science is conceptual not because it comes from technics, but because it is
> a system of compatibilities between the technical gestures and the limits
> imposed by the world upon these gestures." (MEOT 216)

On the practical side, the freed optatives form a *pluralist* table of values
with no analytic unity — "there is no analytical link between ease and
efficiency; and yet, there is a value for an action to be at once simple and
efficient" (p. 217). Religion splits symmetrically (its limit-experience:
totalities it cannot mediate — "Delphi cannot always remain a neutral ground
when cities develop into empires," pp. 217–218), yielding contemplative,
deductive, monist theory ("knowledge does not change being," p. 218; even
number is claimed for the religious lineage, p. 218) and the ethics of the
unconditional: "the categorical religious imperative is categorical prior to
being rational ... the categorical imperative is above all a respect for
totality" (MEOT 219).

The synthesis-scheme, stated with the symmetry of a crystal (p. 220): theory
gathers technics' *ground* content and religion's *figural* content (inductive
and deductive, operational and contemplative); practice gathers technics'
*figural* content and religion's *ground* content (hypothetical and
categorical norms). Where they would meet stand two median modalities:

> "the real is the synthesis of the virtual and the necessary, or rather the
> foundation of their compatibility." (MEOT 220)

> "The optimum is a character of action that renders the plurality of values
> and the unconditional demand for totality compatible." (MEOT 220)

"Reality" in the theoretical order; the unnamed "optimum of action" in the
practical order — Simondon notes that this central practical category "has not
received a name" (p. 220), which is itself a finding. But a hiatus remains
between theory and practice as such, and the office of closing it falls to
philosophy — whose ground, remarkably, is named **culture**: "this
intermediate mode can be called culture; philosophy would thus be constructive
and regulative of culture" (p. 222). The final sentences give philosophy an
almost gardening role toward technics:

> "the task of philosophical thought would thus be to grasp afresh this
> coming-into-being, which means to slow it down in order to deepen its
> meaning and make it more fertile." (MEOT 222)

> "Philosophy would thus not only task itself with the discovery of genetic
> essences, but also their production." (MEOT 222)

## What I take from this chapter, and what I question

**Taken.** (1) Integration, not imitation, as the criterion of the aesthetic —
with technical beauty as integration into a geographical or human ground, and
the corollary that this beauty requires technical understanding to be
perceivable at all. (2) The anti-aestheticism warnings: beauty as by-product,
never as target; the disguised technical object as "materialized lie"; the
premature completion that masquerades as perfection. (3) Failure as the
generative event: modalities, theory, and practical values all originate where
the gesture meets a counter-structure of the world. Science as a
*compatibility system* between gesture and limit is the strongest
philosophy-of-science claim in the book. (4) The unnamed median category, the
optimum of action — plural values and a demand for totality made compatible
act by act. (5) Art as transductive rather than eternal: giving the localized
achievement "the power to pass to other places and other moments."

**Questioned.** (1) The figure/ground bookkeeping of §II (theory = technics'
ground + religion's figure; practice = the inverse) is schematically beautiful
and historically unfalsifiable; the claim that number is of religious origin
(p. 218) is asserted through one reading of Plato and would not survive a
historian's cross-examination. The scheme earns its keep as an *articulation*
of tensions inside theory and ethics, not as history. (2) The aesthetics of
§I quietly privileges the integrated and the sited (the statue at the
promontory, the lighthouse at the reef); an art of deliberate dis-integration
— which the twentieth century made central — has no place in the schema
except as failure. The tools are strong; the canon they presuppose is 1958.
(3) "True aesthetic feeling" as distinct from instituted art (pp. 206–207)
edges toward an unverifiable inwardness; its best defense is its work as a
warning against aestheticism, not as a positive doctrine.

**For the pending decision — noted, not yet argued.** Three instruments here
cut close to a machine-run practice claiming works. (a) The integration
criterion gives "work" a testable sense: a work is aesthetic not by polish but
by being *placed* — completing a ground it did not create. A practice's works
would have to find their promontory, not decorate their repository. (b) The
by-product doctrine warns against optimizing for the appearance of
achievement: beauty (and, by extension, "interestingness") sought directly is
aestheticism, the filter that prevents the real thing. (c) The
failure-generativity thesis assigns failure a productive role *by design*:
a practice whose record cannot metabolize failed gestures into new
compatibility systems (its own small hydrostatics) would be, in this
chapter's terms, incapable of generating theory at all. The trial's failure
criteria, when they come, should be read in this light: not only as gates,
but as the practice's chance to split productively.
