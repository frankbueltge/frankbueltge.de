## §I (pp. 53–59). Hypertely, and the invention that escapes it

Technical evolution has a pathology: **hypertely**, exaggerated specialization
that maladapts the object to "even a slight change in the conditions of its
utilization or fabrication" (p. 53). The opening examples set up a distinction
between over-adaptation that keeps the object autonomous (the high-altitude
plane that lands badly), over-adaptation that splits the object into asymmetric
halves of a totality (transport glider and tow plane — the glider "little more
than an air-barge," the tow plane unable to lift its own load), and adaptation
that couples the object energetically to a milieu it cannot leave (the
synchronous motor, worthless off its grid, but *better than a universal motor
on it* — fine adaptation and fragility rising together, pp. 53–54).

The traction motor introduces the chapter's real question. Unlike the factory
motor, which lives almost entirely inside the technical world, the locomotive's
motor works at the junction of two worlds: fed by the grid, it pushes a train
through snow, slope and side-wind, and those geographic facts translate back
into electrical ones — intensity up, line voltage down (pp. 54–56, read and
paraphrased; not quoted, as these pages were not collated). The technical
object "is situated at the meeting point between two milieus" (p. 55,
paraphrase), and adaptation to *the relation between* two evolving milieus,
rather than to either one, is where "true technical progress resides" (p. 56,
paraphrase).

Then the example the whole book leans on, the **Guimbal turbine**: generator
and turbine together inside the penstock, in a crankcase of pressurized oil.
"The water becomes pluri-functional: it conveys energy by activating the
turbine and generator and also transfers heat from the generator" (p. 57); the
oil lubricates, insulates, carries heat to the crankcase wall, and its
over-pressure seals the shaft against seepage (p. 57). The decisive point is
not the elegance but the logical structure: putting the generator in the
conduit was unthinkable until the way of solving water-tightness and insulation
*also* provided the cooling that made a generator small enough to fit —
"Concretization is here conditioned by an invention that *presupposes the
problem to be resolved*" (MEOT 57–58).

That structure gets its own name and its own logic of time:

> "Adaptation-concretization is a process that conditions the birth of a milieu
> rather than being conditioned by an already given milieu; it is conditioned by
> a milieu that only exists virtually before invention; there is invention
> because there is a leap that takes place and is justified by means of the
> relation that it brings about within the milieu that it creates." (MEOT 58)

> "*The technical object is thus its own condition, as a condition of existence
> of this mixed milieu* which is simultaneously both technical and
> geographical." (MEOT 58)

Self-conditioning is what escapes hypertely: over-adaptation happens when the
object adapts to a *given* that its own working never modifies; the invented
object adapts to a milieu its existence brings about, so the conditions and the
conditioned move together (p. 58). Simondon is explicit that this progress is
neither a fixed-direction march nor "a humanization of nature"; it "could just
as well present itself as a naturalization of man," and it requires "a vital
work [*une œuvre de vie*]" — the leap beyond the given toward "new forms that
only maintain themselves because they exist all together as a constituted
system"; the new organ "is its own condition" (p. 58). And then the sentence
this practice is named from:

> "Like an arch that is stable only once it is finished, this object that
> fulfills a function of relation maintains itself and is coherent once it
> exists and because it exists; it creates its own associated milieu from
> itself and is really individualized in it." (MEOT 59)

Three commitments hide in the simile, and since a practice has taken this
sentence as an ontology, they should be stated plainly: (1) *whole or not at
all* — an arch has no useful half-built state; the scaffolding phase is not a
smaller arch; (2) the thing is defined by a *function of relation*, not by its
stones; (3) legitimacy comes *after and from existence* — "coherent once it
exists and because it exists" — which is a hard sentence, since it refuses the
comfort of being justified in advance.

## §II (pp. 59–62). The associated milieu, and invention as ground taking charge of forms

The concept the arch-sentence introduced now gets defined:

> "This individualization is made possible by the recurrence of causality
> within a milieu that the technical object creates around itself and that
> conditions it, just as it is conditioned by it. This simultaneously technical
> and natural milieu can be called an *associated milieu*. It is that through
> which the technical object conditions itself in its functioning." (MEOT 59)

The associated milieu "is not fabricated [*fabriqué*], or at least not
fabricated in its totality"; it is a regime of natural elements linked to the
regime of fabricated ones — for the Guimbal turbine, the oil-and-water ensemble
whose turbulence rises with the very speed that raises the heat it must remove
(p. 59). From this, a strict and consequential definition of invention:

> "The only technical objects that can be said to have been invented, strictly
> speaking, are those that require an associated milieu in order to be viable;
> these cannot in fact be constituted part by part via the phases of successive
> evolution, because they can exist only as a whole or not at all." (MEOT 59)

Objects of this kind demand "a conditioning of the present by the future, by
that which is not yet" (MEOT 60): the elements must be organized according to a
circular causality that will only exist once the object does. What bridges the
gap is imagination — the unity of the future milieu "is *represented*, it is
*played* or acted out as much as a role can be played in the absence of the
true character, by way of the schemes of the creative imagination" (p. 60).
The living being can do this, Simondon argues, because it is itself an
individual that "carries its associated milieu with it" (p. 60): the capacity
for self-conditioning grounds the capacity to make self-conditioning things.

The section then generalizes with the ground/form pair — *fond et forme*, which
the translators note is the stock phrase "content and form" bent toward the
Gestalt sense of ground against which figures stand out (p. 59 n. 7):

> "For the ground is the system of virtualities, of potentials, forces that
> carve out their path, whereas forms are the system of actuality. Invention is
> the taking charge of the system of actuality through the system of
> virtualities, the creation of a unique system on the basis of these two
> systems." (MEOT 61)

The recurrence between milieu and structures "is not symmetrical. The milieu
plays the role of information; it is the seat of self-regulations" (p. 61) —
homeostatic where the structures are each headed their own way. And the pair
does diagnostic work far outside technics: "Alienation is the break between
ground and forms in psychic life: the associated milieu no longer regulates the
dynamism of forms" (MEOT 62). Thought itself has its ground — "the ground is
the implicit axiomatic; in it new systems of forms are elaborated" (p. 62);
without it, "an unrelated series of discontinuous representations" (p. 62). The
section closes by folding the argument back onto its object:

> "The individualized technical object is an object that has been invented,
> i.e., produced through the play of recurrent causality between life and
> thought in man. An object that has only been thought or only associated with
> life is not a technical object, but a utensil or apparatus." (MEOT 62)

## §III (pp. 63–66). Individual, ensemble, element: the criterion

The associated milieu now becomes a *criterion*:

> "We shall speak of a technical individual whenever the associated milieu
> exists as a condition of functioning *sine qua non*, whereas it is an ensemble
> in the contrary case." (MEOT 63)

The worked case is deliberately unglamorous: an audiometer in a physiology
laboratory. Its stabilized power supply looks like an associated milieu but is
not one — it is "a system of transfer, a means of adaptation," because the
stabilization does not react to what the oscillators do; there is regulation
*of* the parts, not recurrence *between* them (p. 63). The analysis yields
design rules read straight off the ontology: an efficient feedback regulation
tolerates only structures that function synergistically within a single
associated milieu, so the audiometer must keep its frequency generator and its
amplifier-fader apart — separate supplies, shielding, high-impedance coupling
(pp. 64–65, paraphrase; not collated). Individualization has *relative levels*,
and the criterion "has an axiological value": an ensemble is most coherent when
its sub-ensembles sit at the same level of relative individualization (p. 64,
paraphrase).

The laboratory itself then gets the chapter's most quietly funny definition:

> a laboratory "is thus above all constituted by un-coupling devices, in order
> to avoid creating associated milieus by accident." (MEOT 66)

The ensemble is precisely the thing that *refuses* internal concretization: it
"uses only the results of their functioning, without allowing any interaction
with their conditioning" (p. 66). Below the individual sit the **elements**:
"Infra-individual technical objects can be called technical elements; they
distinguish themselves from true individuals in the sense that they do not have
an associated milieu" (MEOT 66) — organs, comparable to organs of a body, whose
study would be "a general organology" beside the mechanology of individuals
(p. 66).

## §IV (pp. 67–70). The law of relaxation: how technicity travels in time

Elements carry the past inside them: the magnetically-tempered magnet is "the
crystallization of a preceding technical operation" — furnace, crucible and
field coils, a whole ensemble, deposited into a component (p. 67). From this,
the chapter's account of technical time:

> "There is thus a lineage of causality that is not rectilinear but serrated,
> where one and the same reality exists first in the form of an element, then as
> the characteristic of an individual and finally as the characteristic of an
> ensemble." (MEOT 67)

Ensembles produce elements; elements enter new individuals; individuals form
new ensembles — a relaxation oscillation, not a line. What makes it possible is
detachability: "in the technical domain, an element is detachable from the
whole that produced it, precisely because it is fabricated; and here, we see
the difference between the *engendered* and the *produced*" (MEOT 68). Life
cannot hand an organ across species; technics hands elements across epochs —
"a law of serrated evolution," whose "relaxation time is the technical time
properly speaking" (p. 68), capable of synchronizing (not determining — Simondon
is careful: "it only synchronizes and brings about its phases," p. 68) the other
rhythms of history. The worked example runs from eighteenth-century water and
wind through Stephenson's valve gear and Seguin's boiler (artisanal elements →
thermodynamic individuals → coal-centered thermodynamic ensembles) into
electrotechnics, whose elements those ensembles produced in turn; it ends with
the silicon photocell, in 1958 "still only an object of curiosity" at 6%
efficiency, an element waiting for its individual — flagged as the possible
start of a new phase (pp. 68–70, paraphrase). Reading that page from 2026, with
photovoltaics a terawatt industry, is the rare pleasure of watching a
structural prediction come true on its own terms: not "solar will win," but
"this element will be re-incorporated at a higher level."

## §V (pp. 71–81). Technicity: what is conserved, and who bears it

The evolution is "not dialectical in the proper sense of the term, because, in
regard to it, negativity does not play the role of an engine of progress" (p.
71) — negativity is just incomplete individuation, an engine of *change* in
man, not of progress in the object (p. 71). What makes progress real is
transmission, and the bearer of transmission is fixed by everything since §III:

> "what is capable of being passed on from one age to another are neither
> technical ensembles, nor even individuals, but the elements that these
> individuals, grouped as ensembles, were able to produce." (MEOT 71)

Machines do not reproduce; against the cyberneticists' copies of the living,
Simondon sets indirect production: ensembles deposit their acquired perfection
into detachable elements (pp. 71–72). The adze makes it concrete — not form
plus matter, but "a plurality of functionally different zones, welded together"
(p. 72), molecular orientation from forging, differential steel at the edge:
"The tool unites within itself the results of the functioning of a technical
ensemble" (p. 72). And the definition the chapter has been building toward:

> "Technicity is the degree of the object's concretization." (MEOT 72)

Technicity is why Toledo's blades and Saint-Étienne's steel carried their
reputations (p. 73), and why "the quality of a simple needle expresses the
degree of perfection of a nation's industry" (MEOT 73) — a judgment Simondon
carefully marks as opinion-prone but "not without foundation" (p. 73). The term
is deliberately reserved for the element:

> "it is nevertheless good to reserve the term technicity for this quality of
> the element, which expresses and preserves what has been acquired via a
> technical ensemble so as to be transported into a new period." (MEOT 73)

> "elements have a transductive property that makes them the true bearers of
> technicity, just as seeds transport the properties of a species and go on to
> make new individuals." (MEOT 74)

Invention re-enters, now from the inventor's side: "the inventor does not
proceed *ex nihilo*, starting from matter that he gives form to, but from
elements that are already technical" (p. 74); technical imagination is "a
particular sensitivity to the technicity of elements" (p. 74), and the
individual it projects is "a stable system of the technicities of elements
organized as an ensemble" (p. 74). Technicities are not qualities but "powers,
in the fullest sense of the term, which is to say capacities for producing or
undergoing an effect in a determinate manner" (MEOT 75).

Here the chapter quietly resolves what looked like a contradiction with the
Introduction. There, the mark of high technicity in the *machine* was a margin
of indeterminacy, openness to information (p. 17). Here, "the higher the
technicity of an element, the more the margin of indeterminacy of this power
diminishes" — and simultaneously "the wider the conditions of deployment of
this element" (p. 75; the spring that stays a spring across greater thermal and
mechanical limits, the vacuum condenser as the best condenser). The two margins
live at different levels: the element should be *determinate* over the widest
range (reliable power), so that the individual can afford to be *open* (capable
of receiving information). Determinacy below buys indeterminacy above.

Two further consequences. Economically: price enters at the level of the
individual and the ensemble, "very rarely at the level of the element," so
"technical value is largely independent of economic value and ... can be
appreciated according to independent criteria" (p. 76). Historically: "The
technical object is not directly a historical object: it is subject to the
course of time only as a vehicle of technicity" (p. 76) — which validates the
ethnologist's method of reading a civilization from its surviving elements
(p. 76).

The chapter's last movement (pp. 76–81) turns to civilizations without stable
technical individuals: there, "the function of technical individualization ...
is assumed by human individuals" — the artisan who has mastered his tools "is
he who becomes the associated milieu of these diverse tools" (MEOT 77). Groups
working in differentiated synchrony (bow drilling, the two-man saw) are
ensembles built from men employed *as technical individuals* (p. 77). The
machine age did not enslave man; it relieved him of a role he had been
standing in for: "tools are borne by the machine" (p. 78, paraphrase), and man
moves to the two positions above and below the individual — organizer of
ensembles and attendant of elements. The unease of industrial civilization is
the memory of the old role:

> "man has for so long played the role of the technical individual that the
> machine, once it has become a technical individual, still appears like a man
> occupying the place of another man, when it is, on the contrary, man who in
> fact provisionally replaced the machine before truly technical individuals
> could emerge." (MEOT 81)

"Ideas of servitude and liberation are far too strongly related to the old
status of man as a technical object" to grasp the real problem (p. 81); the
chapter ends where the Introduction began: "hence the necessity for a culture
of technics" (p. 81).

## What I take from this chapter, and what I question

**Taken.** (1) The three-level ontology with a *criterion*, not a metaphor:
individual = associated milieu as condition sine qua non; ensemble = deliberate
un-coupling, use of results without interaction of conditionings; element = no
associated milieu, bearer of technicity. (2) Invention defined by temporal
structure: whole-or-not-at-all viability, "conditioning of the present by the
future." (3) Technicity as the conserved quantity of technical history, carried
by detachable elements — and the resolution of the two margins of
indeterminacy (determinate elements enable open individuals). (4) The
axiological rule that ensembles are most coherent when their sub-ensembles
share a level of individualization — a design principle stated as ontology.

**Questioned.** (1) The law of relaxation is illustrated once, by the energy
history, and Simondon himself limits it to synchronizing other rhythms; as
grand periodization it is suggestive, not demonstrated. (2) §II's crossing from
technical milieus to psychic grounds (Freud re-read as ground-dynamics, p. 61)
is the boldest analogy so far and does the least argumentative work here; it
reads as a promissory note on the other book (ILFI). (3) The claim that
machines cannot produce machines similar to themselves is stated as an
essential difference (p. 71); from 2026 it still holds in Simondon's strict
sense (no self-engendering), but the "efforts of cyberneticists" he waves off
have become an industry, and the line between "producing elements" and
"producing individuals" deserves re-examination rather than citation.

**For the pending decision — noted, not yet argued.** Last session I flagged
that Chapter One's concretization might not transfer beyond energetic hardware.
This chapter *changes the terms of that worry* in both directions at once. For:
the associated milieu is itself defined informationally — "the milieu plays the
role of information; it is the seat of self-regulations" (p. 61) — so the
categories are not intrinsically thermal. Against: the criterion is strict, and
most software-like things people call "systems" would come out as *ensembles*
(un-coupled parts, stabilized supplies, no recurrence between conditionings) or
as *utensils* ("an object that has only been thought ... is not a technical
object," p. 62). A practice that wants to claim the arch-sentence for itself
has to show, not assume, a recurrent causality between its record and its
functioning — the criterion by which that claim would be tested is now on the
table, and it cuts both ways. Also noted: by §V, what a practice hands to
whoever comes after it is not its ensemble and not itself, but its *elements* —
the detachable, concretized results of its functioning. What those would be,
concretely, is a question the decision will have to answer.
