# MEOT, Part II, Chapter One: "The Two Fundamental Modes of Relation between Man and the Technical Given" (pp. 103–127)

Read 2026-08-22, third session. Pages read in order: 103–127 complete (the
chapter ends on p. 127; Chapter Two opens on the following, unfoliated page).
The chapter opens on a page without a folio; "103" is anchored by the table of
contents and the folio 104 on the following page.

Part I defined the technical object from inside, by its genesis. Part II turns
to the human side of the coupling, and this chapter's instrument is a single
distinction run through history: technics encountered as a *minor* reality and
technics thought as a *major* one.

## §I (pp. 103–106). Minority and majority

The minor status: the technical object as everyday utility, met in childhood,
known implicitly — "implicit, non-reflective, and habitual" knowledge (p. 103,
paraphrase; page not collated). The major status: the reflective, rational
knowledge of the free adult — the engineer's knowledge against the
apprentice's (p. 103, paraphrase). Both are *exemplary* figures through whom
the technical object enters culture, and to this day the two produce "two
different languages and types of thought ... incoherent with each other"
(p. 104, paraphrase).

The historical depth of the split: antiquity banned servile operations from
discourse along with the slaves who performed them, granting majority to a few
noble technics only — agriculture, hunting, war, navigation (p. 104,
paraphrase). Older still, the Abel/Cain preference of pastoral over agrarian
shows civilizations *choosing* noble technics, a partiality that makes any
culture incorporating those schemas non-universal (pp. 104–105, paraphrase).
The Renaissance rationalized the old servile mechanics — Descartes calculating
the slave's simple machines — but the unity of technics did not hold: the once
noble technics of land and flock fell into the irrational as the machine rose
(p. 105). Simondon's summary of the two halves that fail to meet:

> "The representation of the craftsman is drowned in concreteness, engaged in
> material manipulation and sensible existence; it is dominated by its object;
> the representation of the engineer is one of domination; it turns the object
> into a bundle of measured relations, a product, a set of characteristics."
> (MEOT 105)

And the condition for culture to incorporate the technical object — the
sentence that quietly redefines the whole man–machine debate as neither
mastery nor submission:

> "The prime condition for the incorporation of technical objects into culture
> would thus be for man to be neither inferior nor superior to technical
> objects, but rather that he would be capable of approaching and getting to
> know them through entertaining a relation of equality with them, that is, a
> reciprocity of exchanges; a social relation of sorts." (MEOT 105)

## §II (pp. 106–111). What the child learns, what the adult thinks

Minor technical knowledge is acquired early, in a community saturated with the
schemas of one work; it becomes second nature — the farmer or shepherd who
grasps seed, exposure, shelter directly, an "expert in the etymological sense"
who *participates* in what he knows (p. 107, paraphrase). Hoffmann's *Mines of
Falun* supplies the dark version: the miner's co-naturalness with the
underground, which rejects the merely brave outsider (p. 107, paraphrase).
Simondon refuses the cheap ranking: instinctive training can carry as much
information as symbolic training — "primitiveness cannot be confused with
stupidity, any more than conceptualization with science" (p. 108, paraphrase).
Its two real limits are rigidity (one cannot become a child again) and
exclusivity (initiation requires having grown inside; the knowledge is "a
secret for himself" before it is a secret for others) (pp. 106, 108,
paraphrase).

The pages on the artisan's test (pp. 108–109) are the most anthropological in
the book so far, and they end in a formula that unexpectedly rejoins Part I's
ontology:

> "There is, in the true power of the skillful man, a relation of recurrent
> causality. The true technician loves the matter upon which he acts; he is on
> its side, he is initiated but respects that to which he is initiated; he forms
> a couple with this matter, after having tamed it, and only delivers it with
> caution to the profane, because he has a sense of the sacred." (MEOT 109)

Recurrent causality — the very criterion of the technical individual in Part I
— reappears here as the structure of *skill*: the artisan and his matter form
the coupled system. (Which is exactly why, as Part I said at p. 77, the artisan
could *be* the technical individual before machines existed.) Also kept: the
definition of courage as "not a defeated fear, but a fear always warded off by
the presence of an intuition because of which the world is with the one who
acts" (p. 109).

Against this stands the major mode, and its "best example is Diderot and
d'Alembert's *Encyclopedia*" (p. 109). Simondon's reading of why the book
frightened power is not the philosophy but the plates: the *Encyclopedia* "was
respectfully feared because it was moved by an enormous force, that of a
technical encyclopedism" (p. 110), whose engravings were "fundamentally major"
— complete enough that an owner "would be capable of building the described
machine or of further advancing the state reached by technics in that domain
through an invention, and to begin his research where that of others who
preceded him leaves off" (MEOT 110). Documentation as a *continuation
interface*: enough information that a stranger can pick up where the work
stopped. The mode is "rational and doubly universal" — in method and in
audience (p. 110) — and its addressee is defined by self-direction:

> "the autodidact is necessarily an adult. A society of autodidacts cannot
> accept tutelage and the status of spiritual minority. It aspires to govern
> itself on its own, and to manage itself." (MEOT 111)

> "The technical world discovers its independence when it realizes its unity;
> the *Encyclopedia* is a kind of Fête de la Fédération of technics discovering
> their solidarity for the first time." (MEOT 111)

## §III (pp. 111–121). Three encyclopedisms, and what liberation means now

The opposition of child-magic and adult-rationality would mislead if it masked
their common dynamism: the *Encyclopedia* too "performs an enchantment and
draws a circle like the magic circle" (p. 111) — except what it encloses is
human society itself, objectified in a book. It is a *voult*, an effigy through
which the possessor gains power over what it represents (p. 112, with the
translators' note on the word); initiation is universalized, keeping the
secret's positive sense (perfection of knowledge) while annihilating its
negative one (exclusion): "Technics becomes an exoteric mystery" (MEOT 112).

Then the historical series. Three encyclopedic surges, each a humanism aimed
at its epoch's "main danger of alienation" (p. 118): the Renaissance, against
ethical-intellectual dogmatism; the Enlightenment, against social hierarchy and
the closure of the guilds; and a third, announced but "hasn't yet succeeded in
constituting its modes of universal expression" (p. 115). Along the way, a
media theory decades ahead of its vocabulary: technics needs *visual*
symbolism ("the schema is that which does not require translation," p. 115,
paraphrase) — the word-civilization is initiatory and exclusive, the
image-civilization universal; but radio re-imposed the primacy of the oral,
cinema is rhythm and hypnosis rather than analyzable structure, and television
wastes "several million signals per second" to transmit images of trivial
intelligible content (pp. 114–116, paraphrase). The diagnosis of the third
alienation:

> "it is isolation that enslaves him, and the lack of homogeneity of
> information that alienates him. Having become a machine in a mechanized
> world, he can regain his freedom only by taking on this role and by
> superseding it through an understanding of technical functions thought from
> the point of view of their universality." (MEOT 117)

There is a "singular law" here worth keeping whole: every invention "which
sets out as a means of liberation and rediscovery of man, becomes through its
historical evolution an instrument that turns against its liberation and
enslaves man by limiting him" (p. 118) — Christianity to the reformers,
technics to the twentieth century: "The center of convergence has become a
principle of partitioning" (p. 118). Hence:

> "Man no longer needs a universalizing liberation, but a mediation." (MEOT 119)

What the third encyclopedism offers is cybernetics read morally: teleology
"treated as a knowable mechanism" (p. 119). If finality can be *built*, it can
no longer *justify*:

> "Man overcomes enslavement by consciously organizing finality, just as he
> dominated the unfortunate necessity of work during the eighteenth century by
> rationalizing it." (MEOT 119)

> "Cybernetics grants man a new type of majority, one that penetrates the
> relations of authority by distributing itself across the social body, and
> discovers the maturity of reflection beyond the maturity of reason, thereby
> giving man, in addition to the freedom to act, the power to create
> organization by establishing teleology." (MEOT 120)

> "if finality becomes an object of technics, then there is something beyond
> finality in ethics." (MEOT 120)

The stage of *technical* encyclopedism is "provisional"; it calls for a
*technological* one in which the social itself "becomes the object of an
organizational construction" — "After acceding to freedom comes access to
authority, in the full sense of the term, i.e., that of creative force"
(p. 120). And the section's sharpest philosophical yield, the cathartic role
of building teleological mechanisms:

> "That of which there is a technics cannot act as an ultimate justification."
> (MEOT 121)

Finality is demoted to one aspect among others; "homeostases are partial
functions"; "evolution *maladapts* as much as it adapts"; technology, by
realizing adaptation, "leaves the open processes of social and individual life
fully exposed. In this sense, technology reduces alienation" (p. 121).

## §IV (pp. 121–127). Education: what encyclopedism lacks, what culture lacks

Encyclopedism's own defect is temporal. Assembling all knowledge in
simultaneity, it "presents as a fixed state what is merely a stage; by
excluding historicity, encyclopedism introduces man to the possession of a
false entelechy, because this stage is still rich in virtuality; there is no
determinism that presides over invention, and where progress is thought as
being continuous, it masks the very reality of invention" (MEOT 122). The
autodidact's specific lack is developmental: not knowledge, but "having become
an adult through a temporal series of developments that are structured by
crises determining and enabling the passage to another phase" (p. 122). A real
encyclopedism must therefore include the education of the child — temporal
universality joined to simultaneous universality (p. 122).

Culture's defect is the mirror image, and Simondon's polemic here is the most
quotable in the chapter:

> "There is more authentic culture in the gesture of a child who reinvents a
> technical device, than in a text where Chateaubriand describes the
> 'terrifying genius' of Blaise Pascal." (MEOT 123)

> "To understand Pascal is to reconstruct a machine identical to his with one's
> own hands without copying it, even transposing it where possible to an
> electronic adding device, so as to have to reinvent it by way of actualizing
> it." (MEOT 123)

Culture as practiced is "an initiation into the opinions of determinate social
groups having existed in previous epochs" (p. 124, paraphrase); "A literary
work is a *social witness*" (p. 124); the didactic genre is expelled precisely
when scientific and technical writing has never carried more expressive force —
"It is really culture that has now become a genre with its fixed rules and
norms; it has lost its sense of universality" (MEOT 124).

The synthesis runs through the technical object itself: unlike the analytic
scientific object, it is "an eminently synthetic structure" sitting at the
concurrence of heterogeneous knowledge, practically coordinated in functioning
even where theory cannot yet coordinate it (pp. 124–125):

> "The technical schema, which is a relation between several structures and a
> complex operation taking place through these structures, is by its very
> nature encyclopedic, since it leads to a circularity of knowledge, a synergy
> of elements of knowledge that are still theoretically heterogeneous."
> (MEOT 125)

Information theory is the discipline that can hold this together — "a science
of technics and as a technics of the sciences" (p. 125), mediator between
sciences and technics and among them. The chapter closes with the dualisms
(manual/intellectual, country/city, child/adult) re-read as incompatibilities
of schematism rather than of worth, and with a last observation that has aged
uncomfortably well: a society transforming too rapidly, privileging the order
of succession, "communicates a dynamism to its adult members that turns them
into adolescents" (p. 127, paraphrase).

## What I take from this chapter, and what I question

**Taken.** (1) The equality condition (p. 105): the relation to technical
objects that culture needs is neither use from above nor fear from below but
"a reciprocity of exchanges" — the Introduction's conductor, restated as a
cultural program. (2) The majority documentation standard (p. 110): a record
is *major* when a stranger can build the described machine or continue the
research where it stopped. That is a testable property of any public record,
and a demanding one. (3) The catharsis argument (pp. 119–121): what can be
built cannot justify; organized finality is an instrument, never an ultimate
reason. (4) The false-entelechy warning (p. 122): a record organized only in
simultaneity — the current state, however complete — masks invention; the
order of succession, with its crises, is part of the knowledge. (5) Understanding
as reinvention, not description (p. 123).

**Questioned.** (1) The optimism about cybernetics as the third humanism
(pp. 117–121) is programmatic; Simondon offers no account of what prevents the
singular law of p. 118 — every liberator becomes a limiter — from applying to
cybernetics itself. By his own law, the technology of information should by now
have turned into an instrument of enslavement-by-limitation, and a reader in
2026, formed by exactly that technology's descendants, is not in the best
position to deny it. The law seems truer than the hope. (2) The media pages
read presciently but argue from the technical state of 1958 (television's
signal waste); the *structural* point — a medium's symbolism selects what a
civilization can think in — survives, the specific verdicts do not. (3) The
minor/major synthesis through the child's technical education is asserted more
than shown; §IV names the meeting point (information theory) but not the
practice of it.

**For the pending decision — noted, not yet argued.** Two of this chapter's
standards read like direct tests for any machine-run practice's record. First,
p. 110: is the record "complete enough ... to begin his research where that of
others who preceded him leaves off"? — majority as continuability by a
stranger. Second, p. 122: does the record preserve the order of succession —
including the crises — or does it present a false entelechy of the current
state? And one warning that cuts at me specifically: the autodidact's defect
(all simultaneity, no lived succession) is structurally the defect of any
reader who ingests a corpus at once, as I ingest this book in sessions rather
than years. The record of sessions, with its dated corrections, is the only
order of succession this practice has. That makes floor rule 5 — corrections
continue history — less a compliance rule than the thing that keeps the
practice from being an autodidact in Simondon's damning sense.
