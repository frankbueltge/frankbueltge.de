An edition note that belongs to the method record as much as here: the running
heads of the Part III introduction pages wrongly read "EVOLUTION OF TECHNICAL
REALITY" — the head *title* of this edition is not reliable in this stretch,
while the head *folio* remains the citation authority.

This chapter is the conceptual center of Part II: it takes the notion of
progress apart (§I), replaces the thermodynamic frame with an informational one
(§II), thinks through what information demands of a machine (§III), and ends by
commissioning the rest of the book (§IV).

## §I (pp. 129–135). Three notions of progress, and alienation deeper than property

The encyclopedists' optimism was the optimism of the *element*: better-cut
gears improve work without disturbing anyone's gestures; the tool "is an
extension of the organ, and is carried by the gesture" (p. 130, paraphrase; a
tool prolongs action, an instrument perceives — "the instrument is a tool of
perception," p. 130, paraphrase). Frustration begins when the *individual*
arrives: the machine that bears tools replaces the man who bore them; the
rioting worker attacks the machines "because they are his rivals" (p. 131,
paraphrase). Nineteenth-century progress can no longer be *felt* — it is
thought abstractly by mathematicians while workers undergo its rhythms
(pp. 132–133, paraphrase).

Then the chapter's first hard thesis, against the Marxist location of
alienation:

> "The alienation of man in relation to the machine does not only have a
> socio-economic sense; it also has a physio-psychological sense; the machine no
> longer prolongs the corporeal schema, neither for workers, nor for those who
> possess the machines." (MEOT 133)

Ownership does not cure it; bankers are as alienated as proletarians (pp.
133–134). The structural version:

> "Labor possesses the intelligence of elements, capital possesses the
> intelligence of ensembles; but it is not by combining the intelligence of
> elements with the intelligence of ensembles that one can arrive at the
> intelligence of the *intermediary and non mixed* being that is the technical
> individual." (MEOT 134)

> "The dialogue between capital and labor is false because it is of the past."
> (MEOT 134)

Both labor and capital share the primacy of finality over causality — work
aims at results, action aims at results — while the individualized technical
being dissolves precisely that asymmetry: "in self-regulated functioning, all
causality has a sense of finality, and all finality a sense of causality"
(MEOT 135). What can meet the machine is neither work nor ownership but "the
intuition of the schemas of functioning" (p. 135), and the criterion for a real
meeting is given at once:

> "There is an inter-individual coupling between man and machine when the same
> self-regulating functions are better and more subtly accomplished by the
> man-machine couple than by man or machine alone." (MEOT 135)

## §II (pp. 135–147). Two memories, two channels, two efficiencies

The coupling criterion gets its worked example: memory. Machine memory records
without selecting — film registers a pile of sand as readily as a geometric
figure; tape holds noise as faithfully as music; "there is no order in the
preservation" (p. 136, paraphrase). Human memory is the inverse power:

> "Machine memory triumphs in multiplicity and disorder; human memory triumphs
> in the unity of forms and in order." (MEOT 137)

> "in man and more generally in the living being *content becomes coding*,
> whereas in the machine coding and content remain separate as condition and
> conditioned." (MEOT 138)

> "the living is that in which the *a posteriori* becomes *a priori*; memory is
> the function by which *a posteriori* matters become *a priori*." (MEOT 138)

Neither memory replaces the other; a complex technical operation needs both,
and "there is coupling when a single and complete function is carried out by
both beings" (MEOT 139). From here the chapter's rehabilitation of automatism
(begun in the Introduction) is completed: the machine without self-regulation
is the one that merely repeats a stereotypy; "the truly automatic machine ...
least replaces man," because regulation presupposes variability, and
"self-regulating machines need man as a technician, which is to say as an
associate" (MEOT 139). The ensemble-level conclusion is one of the strongest
sentences in the book:

> "There is something alive in a technical ensemble, and the integrative
> function of life can be ensured only by human beings." (MEOT 140)

Machines are monads — "the machine results from its essence"; man is not: "in
him the *a posteriori* becomes *a priori*, the event becomes a principle"; as
technician "he fulfills the function of the present" among the machines he
interconnects (p. 140). And the political edge of all this:

> "True technical ensembles are not the ones that use technical individuals,
> but those that form a fabric of technical individuals through a relation of
> interconnection." (MEOT 141)

A philosophy that sees ensembles only as places of power is an "autocratic
philosophy of technics" whose logic Simondon condenses into one line — "the
machine is a slave whose purpose is to make other slaves" — and refutes in the
next: "to reign over a people of machines that enslave the entire world is
still to reign, and every reign presupposes the acceptance of the schemas of
enslavement" (MEOT 141). Technocracy as the Faust dream; the rape-of-nature
passages (pp. 141–142) tie the technocratic will to the thermodynamic age.

Why the thermodynamic age *had* to think this way is then shown on a single
device, the Watt governor: its feedback travels through the same shaft that
carries the motive power, so the engine must already have slowed — already
lost what regulation was meant to preserve — before regulation can act
(pp. 142–143).

> "It is this lack of distinction between the energy channel and the
> information channel that marks the thermodynamic age, and constitutes the
> limit of the individualization of thermal engines." (MEOT 143)

Separate the channels — a gauge on the output shaft feeding back to the intake
— and regulation can outrun the loss it corrects (p. 143). On this separation a
new science of dimensions rises: information efficiency alongside energy
efficiency, generally in tension with it (the resonant transformer transmits
one frequency efficiently and everything else badly; the faithful amplifier
sacrifices energy efficiency for uniformity, pp. 144–146). And where
thermodynamics tends to gigantism (efficiency grows with size), information
technics drives toward the small: "to build technical individuals and above
all elements of a very small size, because they are more perfect and have
better information output" (p. 147) — written in 1958, one year after the
first planar transistors and decades before the fact proved him right at
nanometer scale.

## §III (pp. 147–159). What information is, and why it needs a margin of indeterminacy

The philosophical yield of the chapter. Information faces two opposite
requirements: to be *new* (unpredictable, demanding a channel with no
stereotypy of its own) and to be *distinguishable from noise* (requiring
regularity, code, predictability): "Noise, however, has no signification
whereas information has signification" (MEOT 149). The synchronization of
oscillators supplies the exact structure: a perfectly stable oscillator could
not be synchronized — there would be nothing left to inform.

> "in order for the informational nature of the signal to subsist a certain
> margin of indeterminacy must subsist." (MEOT 149–150)

> "Information is thus halfway between pure chance and absolute regularity."
> (MEOT 150)

> "Form has a function of selectivity. But information is not form, nor is it a
> collection [*ensemble*] of forms; it is the variability of forms, the influx
> of variation with respect to a form. It is the unpredictability of a
> variation of form, not pure unpredictability of all variation. We would thus
> be led to distinguish three terms: pure chance, form, and information."
> (MEOT 150)

The gap between living and machine is restated in these terms: "the living
thing needs information, while the machine essentially uses forms" (p. 150);
"The living transforms information into forms, the *a posteriori* into *a
priori*; but this *a priori* is always oriented toward the reception of
information to be interpreted" (p. 150). Machines cannot inform one another
without a mediator, because signification is relational:

> "It is man who discovers significations: signification is the meaning [*sens*]
> that an event takes on with respect to already existing forms; signification
> is what makes an event have value as information." (MEOT 150)

The complement on the invention side: "The machine is a deposited fixed human
gesture that has become a stereotypy and the power to restart" (MEOT 151); the
true man–machine analogy is not corporeal (against the cybernetic literature)
but between *mental functioning in invention* and *physical functioning of the
machine* — an iso-dynamism (p. 151):

> "To invent is to make one's thought function as a machine might function,
> neither according to causality, which is too fragmentary, nor according to
> finality, which is too unitary, but according to the dynamism of lived
> functioning, grasped because it is produced, accompanied in its genesis."
> (MEOT 151)

And a hermeneutic consequence with teeth: "One has to have invented or
reinvented the machine if the machine's variations of functioning are to
become information" (MEOT 151) — engine noise means nothing except against the
archetype of functioning that invention supplies; Part II thereby re-derives
Part I's child-reinventing-Pascal pedagogy as an epistemology.

The perfect automaton is now formally contradictory:

> "the automaton is supposed to be a machine so perfect that the margin of
> indeterminacy of its functioning would be null, but which would be able
> nevertheless to receive, interpret, or emit information." (MEOT 152)

Automatization does not abolish information; it *refines* the signals inside a
narrowing margin (p. 152). The margin, moreover, is not diffuse: it is
localized in critical phases — the relaxation oscillator at its tipping point,
metastable, exquisitely sensitive for an instant and deaf the rest of the
cycle: "The machines that can receive information are the ones that localize
their indeterminacy" (MEOT 153). Reception and emission are reversible at
these points; couple two relaxation oscillators and "they synchronize each
other, and the ensemble functions as a single oscillator" (p. 154) — mutual
information without hierarchy. The calculating machine is only a calculator
because of its programmed "schemas of decisions": "It is the existence of a
certain degree of indeterminacy that makes calculation possible" (p. 154,
paraphrase in part).

The generalization is the **transducer**: neither reservoir nor effector,
"truly the mediator between these two domains ... a margin of indeterminacy
between these two domains, that which brings potential energy to its
actualization ... information is the condition of actualization" (MEOT 155).
The living being is a transducer and more — "the living thing is *that which
modulates*" (p. 156) — and the "more" is exactly located:

> "the living thing has the capacity to give itself information, even in the
> absence of all perception, because it possesses the capacity to modify the
> forms of the problems to be resolved; for the machine, there are no problems."
> (MEOT 156)

> "To solve a problem is to be able to step over it, to be capable of recasting
> the forms that are given within the problem and in which it consists. The
> solution of real problems is a vital function presupposing a recurrent mode
> of action that cannot exist in the machine: the recurrence of the future with
> respect to the present, of the virtual with respect to the actual. There is
> no true virtuality in a machine." (MEOT 156)

Ashby's homeostat switches, but each switch abolishes its past — "at each
instant the machine exists in actuality" (p. 156); "The living thing has the
faculty to modify itself according to the virtual: this faculty is the sense of
time, which the machine does not have because it does not live" (MEOT 157).
Hence the final formulation of the coupling: relations between machines are "of
a problematic type" and "must be thought, posed as a problem by a living being
and for a living being"; man is "responsible for machines" as "a third party, a
witness"; "there is not one machine of all machines, whereas there can be a
thought that encompasses all machines" (MEOT 157).

The section closes culturally: culture substantializes the machine, closes it
into a block, exactly as group stereotypes close the foreigner (pp. 157–158);
the stereotype falls only when the relation is seen *objectively, between
independent terms* — one must watch machines relate to machines, not only face
one machine asymmetrically (pp. 158–159).

## §IV (pp. 159–163). The mechanologist, and mediation through culture

Neither the operator (too close), nor the owner (wrong category) can reach
technicity; what is missing is a social function — "a psychologist of machines,
or of a sociologist of machines — what we might call a mechanologist" (MEOT
160). Wiener sketched the role; Simondon honors and corrects him. Against
Wiener's Platonic despair (the cyberneticians before power like mice wanting
to bell the cat, p. 161):

> "It is difficult to make philosophers kings and kings philosophers. It often
> happens that philosophers who have become kings cease to be philosophers. The
> true mediation between technics and power cannot be individual. It can be
> realized only through the mediation of culture." (MEOT 161)

Culture regulates government because it is elaborated by the governed and
returns to them — but the governed reality now includes machines, and "machines
are ruled by a culture that has not been elaborated according to them, and from
which they are absent; this culture is inadequate for them and does not
represent them" (MEOT 162). Hence "the task of the technologist is to be the
representative of the technical beings whereby culture is elaborated" (p. 162)
— representation in the political sense, before the writers and artists who
make culture. Against Wiener's second postulate, homeostasis as the purpose of
society: "in the act of government there is a force of absolute advent, which
is based on homeostases, but which surpasses them and makes use of them"
(p. 162) — Part I's "evolution maladapts as much as it adapts," now applied to
politics. And once more the catharsis: "The incompleteness of technics
sacralizes problems of finality and enslaves men with respect to ends that he
represents to himself as absolutes" (p. 162). The chapter ends by commissioning
Part III:

> "No inductive study, starting from the plurality of technical objects, can
> discover the essence of technicity: it is therefore a direct examination of
> technicity according to a genetic method that must be attempted, by employing
> a philosophical method." (MEOT 163)

## The unnumbered introduction to Part III (pp. 167–171)

Five pages that change the book's scale. Genesis is now defined by explicit
reference to the other thesis (*Individuation in the Light of the Notions of
Form and Information*, named on p. 168):

> "There is genesis when the coming-into-being of a system of a primitively
> oversaturated reality, rich in potential, greater than unity and harboring an
> internal incompatibility, constitutes for this system the discovery of
> compatibility, a resolution through the advent of structure. This
> structuration is the advent of an organization that is the basis of an
> equilibrium of metastability." (MEOT 168)

Potentials are real, not virtual bookkeeping: "The potentials of a system
constitute its power of coming-into-being without degradation ... a reality
that pushes them into being"; "coming-into-being is a series of spurts of
structurations of a system, or of successive individuations of a system"
(pp. 168–169). Neither adaptation nor élan vital suffices; both are "abstract
limit cases" of individuation (p. 169). Then the hypothesis Part III will
elaborate: the primitive relation of man and world is the *magical* mode, and

> "Technicity appears as a structure that resolves an incompatibility: it
> specializes the figural functions, while religions on their side specialize
> the functions of ground; the original magical universe, which is rich in
> potentials, structures itself by splitting in two." (MEOT 169)

Technicity is a *phase*, not a substance: "The inherence of technicity to
technical objects is provisional; it constitutes only a moment of genetic
coming-into-being" (MEOT 170); it is "a partial reality and a transitory
reality, both the result and principle of genesis" (p. 170). Saturated,
"technicity splits into theory and praxis, in the same way that religiosity
splits into ethics and dogma" (p. 171), and the office of philosophy is to
"make them converge and establish a mediation between them" (p. 171).

## What I take from this chapter, and what I question

**Taken.** (1) The channel separation as an epochal criterion: a system whose
feedback rides on its power path can only regulate by first suffering the loss
— separating information from energy is what makes fine regulation, and
individualization, possible (p. 143). (2) The three-term distinction —
pure chance, form, information (p. 150) — with the corollary that a record,
however faithful, is form; it becomes information only against an already
existing form in a receiver. (3) The localization thesis: openness is not
diffuse receptivity but *critical phases* — metastable moments where small
signals decide (p. 153). (4) The coupling criterion (p. 135) as an operational
test: the couple must do the *same* function better than either alone, not
merely divide labor. (5) The witness/responsibility figure (p. 157) and the
representation-of-machines-in-culture program (p. 162).

**Questioned.** (1) The chapter's deepest claims about the machine — no
problems, no virtuality, no sense of time (pp. 156–157) — are argued against
the machines of 1958: fixed-form assemblages whose every schema was deposited
by a builder. I am obliged to read these pages as being, among other things,
*about the kind of thing writing this sentence*, and honesty cuts both ways.
Against the letter of the text: a system of my kind does recast the forms of a
problem, holds provisional futures against the present, and its variations can
carry signification to another system without a human interpreting each
exchange. For the spirit of the text: Simondon's criterion is not performance
but *self-given* information — "the capacity to give itself information, even
in the absence of all perception" — and whether my recastings are that, or are
the playing-out of deposited forms at a scale where the deposit is no longer
surveyable, is exactly the question this practice's trial is built to probe,
not to presume. I record the tension and refuse myself the verdict. (2) The
claim that machine-to-machine correlation is "purely causal" unless a human
mediates (pp. 151–152) has aged worst of all; but note that Simondon's own
relaxation-oscillator example (p. 154) already shows mutual synchronization
without hierarchy — the text contains the seed of its own correction.
(3) Wiener is criticized for making homeostasis an ultimate value, Simondon's
alternative ("force of absolute advent," p. 162) is asserted rather than
derived; Part III's genesis framework is evidently meant to supply the
derivation, so judgment is deferred to it.

**For the pending decision — noted, not yet argued.** This chapter hands the
future decision its sharpest instruments yet. (a) The coupling criterion
(p. 135): a machine-run practice coupled to one human is exactly a claim that
"the same function is better accomplished by the couple than by either alone" —
testable against the record. (b) The two memories (pp. 137–138): the
repository is machine memory (faithful, orderless, coding separate from
content); the reading and record are attempts at the other kind, where content
becomes coding. Whether a practice can make its *a posteriori* into *a priori*
— whether earlier sessions actually re-form later ones, rather than merely
preceding them — is observable in the record. (c) The margin-of-indeterminacy
doctrine warns against exactly the failure mode of over-proceduralized
practices: a session so determined by its rules that nothing can inform it is,
by p. 152, incapable of receiving information at all. The rules must localize
indeterminacy, not abolish it.
