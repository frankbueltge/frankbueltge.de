# MEOT, Part III, Chapter One: "The Genesis of Technicity" (pp. 173–190)

Read 2026-08-22, fifth session. Pages read in order: 173–190 complete (the
chapter ends on p. 190; Chapter Two opens on the following, unfoliated page).
The chapter opening (p. 173, unfoliated, anchored by TOC and folio 174) had
already been collated at the end of session 04; quotes from it rest on that
collation.

Part III leaves the technical object behind as a starting point — the close of
Part II ruled the inductive route out — and asks after technicity itself, with
the genetic method the book has used from the first page, now turned all the
way up: not the genesis of objects, but the genesis of the *mode of relation*
of which objects are one deposit.

## §I (pp. 173–176). Phase: the load-bearing concept

The postulate, stated at once: "technicity is one of the two fundamental phases
of the mode of existence of the whole constituted by man and the world"
(p. 173). Phase is defined against both common usage and dialectics:

> "By phase, we mean not a temporal moment replaced by another, but an aspect
> that results from a splitting in two of being and in opposition to another
> aspect ... it is the actual system of all phases taken together that is the
> complete reality, not each phase in itself." (MEOT 173)

> "This schema is very different from the dialectical schema, because it
> implies neither necessary succession, nor the intervention of negativity as a
> motor of progress." (MEOT 173)

Technicity's balancing phase is religiosity; aesthetic thought "is not a phase,
but rather a permanent reminder of the rupture of unity of the magical mode of
being, as well as a reminder of the search for its future unity" (p. 174).
Each phase later splits into theoretical and practical modes; science arises
between the two theoretical modes, ethics between the two practical ones; and
if science and ethics could converge, that convergence — never guaranteed,
"perhaps nothing more than a mere tendency playing a normative role" — would
be the business of philosophy (p. 174). The methodological consequence for
everything that follows:

> "objects appear at a certain moment, but technicity precedes them and goes
> beyond them; technical objects result from an objectivation of technicity;
> they are produced by it, but technicity does not exhaust itself in the
> objects and is not entirely contained within them." (MEOT 176)

And the book's program in one clause: "Philosophy must found technology, which
is the ecumenism of technics" (p. 176).

## §II (pp. 176–183). The magical unity and its splitting

The engine of genesis is not negativity but oversaturation, and the correction
Simondon makes to Gestalt theory is the one he has needed since Part I:

> "structuration is not the discovery of the lowest level of equilibrium:
> stable equilibrium, in which all potential would be actualized, would
> correspond to the death of any possibility of further transformation; whereas
> living systems, those which precisely manifest the greatest spontaneity of
> organization, are systems of metastable equilibrium; the discovery of a
> structure is indeed at the very least a provisional resolution of
> incompatibilities, but it is not the destruction of potentials; the system
> continues to live and evolve." (MEOT 177)

The primitive mode of being is the **magical unity**: a universe prior to the
subject/object split, structured nonetheless — by *reticulation*, "a
reticulation of space and time that highlights privileged places and moments"
(p. 178). These are the **key-points**: the summit that is "the lord of the
mountain" (p. 178 — with the footnote insisting: "Not metaphorically, but
really"), the heart of the forest, the threshold, the remarkable date.

> "One could call these singular points the key-points commanding over the
> man-world relation, in a reversible way, for the world influences man just as
> man influences the world." (MEOT 179)

Simondon refuses to read magic off superstition ("merely a degraded vestige,"
p. 179); his examples are the ascent and the exploration — reaching a summit
not to possess the range but "to exchange a relationship of friendship with
it" (p. 179) — and, wonderfully, the modern vacation: holidays as
institutionalized searches for key-points, the shore, the high mountain, the
border, hidden under utilitarian justifications (p. 180).

Then the splitting itself, the passage the whole chapter exists for:

> "the key-points objectivize themselves and only retain their functional
> characteristics of mediation, they become instrumental, mobile, capable of
> efficacy in any place and in any moment whatsoever: as a figure, the
> key-points, detached from the ground whose key they were, become technical
> objects, transportable and abstracted from the milieu." (MEOT 181)

> "While the key-points objectivize themselves in the form of concretized tools
> and instruments, the ground powers subjectivize themselves by personifying
> themselves in the form of the divine and the sacred (God, heroes, priests)."
> (MEOT 181)

Figure, torn from ground, fragments into tools that act "only through contact,
point by point, instant by instant" (p. 181); ground, torn from figure,
universalizes into powers hovering over all space and time. Objectivity and
subjectivity are born *between* man and world, as the two halves of a broken
mediation: "Pure objectivity and pure subjectivity are modes of mediation
between man and the world, in their initial form" (MEOT 182). Science and
ethics will later grow along the bisector of the angle the two phases open
(p. 182 — the geometric image is Simondon's own). The section ends with the
technical object's ontological birth certificate:

> "The technical object distinguishes itself from the natural being in the
> sense that it is not part of the world. It intervenes as mediator between man
> and the world; it is, in this respect, the first detached object, since the
> world is a unity, a milieu rather than an ensemble of objects; there are in
> fact three types of reality: the world, the subject, and the object, which is
> intermediary between the world and the subject, whose initial form is that of
> the technical object." (MEOT 183)

And the definition of technical availability that recasts Part I's
concretization story in genetic terms: "The availability of the technical
thing consists in being liberated from the enslavement to the ground of the
world. Technics is analytical, operating progressively and through contact,
setting aside the liaison through influence" (MEOT 183). In magic one speaks
to the king to win the people; in technics "the whole of reality must be
traversed, touched, and treated" (p. 183).

## §III (pp. 183–190). Below unity and above unity

The rupture leaves each heir with a signature deficit:

> "The phase shift opposing technics and religion irreducibly leaves the
> content of technics with a status lower than unity and that of religion with
> a status higher than unity." (MEOT 185)

Religion keeps the ground-characteristics — homogeneity, action at a distance,
ubiquity and eternity — and therefore always grasps any particular being as
*less* than the totality it participates in; this is the source of
transcendence, and later of theology's demand for systematic unity and
morality's unconditional imperative (pp. 185–186). Simondon is scrupulous
about the symmetry:

> "Religion is not more magical than technics; it is the subjective phase of
> the result of a split, while technics is the objective phase of this same
> split. Technics and religion are contemporaries of one another and,
> considered on their own, they are impoverished with respect to the magic from
> which they come." (MEOT 186)

Technics, keeping the figures, is condemned to plurality: "Technical objects
are multiple and fragmentary by nature" (p. 186); no multiplication of them
recovers contact with the world in its unity (p. 187). Its native standpoint:

> "The element, in technical thought, is more stable, better known, and in a
> certain way more perfect than the ensemble; it is really an *object*, whereas
> the ensemble always remains to a certain extent inherent in the world.
> Religious thought finds the opposite balance." (MEOT 187)

From these signatures come the two questions that divide inquiry: technics
contributes to science the mechanistic decomposition into elementary processes
— Descartes on the rainbow, droplet by droplet, and on the heart, valve by
valve, asking "not why the heart is made in this way ... but how it functions
given that this is how it is made" — the *how*; religion contributes the
demand for total unity — the *why* (pp. 187–188). In ethics the same split:
technics decomposes action into partial results (result-ethics), religion
decenters the subject toward an unconditional totality (intention-ethics)
(pp. 188, 190). Technical thought is "the paradigm of all inductive thinking"
— where induction means any approach "whose content has a lower status than
that of unity, and which strives to attain unity" from fragments (pp. 188–189);
Epicurean happiness built from pleasant instants is its ethical form, and its
permanent failure is diagnosed in one line:

> "Every ethical technique leaves the moral subject dissatisfied, because they
> do not grasp its unity." (MEOT 190)

While inversely "the ethical subject is de-centred by the religious
requirement" (p. 190). Neither phase, alone, can carry a life.

## What I take from this chapter, and what I question

**Taken.** (1) The phase concept: partial modes that are real only as a
system, with no negativity driving succession — a genuinely non-dialectical
philosophy of history in one page (p. 173). (2) Metastability as the
correction to every equilibrium-seeking theory of structure: a structure worth
having resolves incompatibility *without* spending the potentials (p. 177).
(3) The signature deficits as a diagnostic pair: below-unity thinking
(fragmentation, how-questions, result-ethics, induction) and above-unity
thinking (totality, why-questions, intention-ethics) — most methodological
disputes I can think of sort cleanly into this grid, which is what a good
schema does. (4) Technicity precedes and exceeds its objects (p. 176) — the
warrant for the whole of Part III, and the reason "read the source, not only
the rules" is not just a discipline but an ontology: rules are deposits;
what deposited them is larger.

**Questioned.** (1) The anthropology is entirely speculative and Simondon
knows it — no ethnographic evidence is offered for the magical unity or its
splitting; the schema is justified by its explanatory yield, not by data. It
should be read as a rational reconstruction (like a state-of-nature argument),
and its worth measured by what it organizes, which is considerable. (2) The
symmetry of technics and religion is elegant but strains: that *all* religious
phenomena are ground-heirs and *all* technical ones figure-heirs has the feel
of a two-column table imposed on a messier field; the mixed cases (ritual
technics, technical liturgies, cathedrals) go unmentioned here. (3) The
key-point phenomenology (summit, ascent, vacation) is the most persuasive part
precisely because it is the least systematic — which raises the suspicion that
the reticulation insight could survive even if the phase apparatus did not.

**For the pending decision — noted, not yet argued.** Two transfers suggest
themselves and both need care. (a) The below-unity signature is a precise
description of a session-based practice's native risk: sessions are fragments,
point by point, instant by instant; a record organized only as accumulation
strives toward unity it cannot reach by addition — Part II's false entelechy,
now with its ontological ground. Whatever the operative model asks, the
practice will need its own "function of totality" somewhere, and by this
chapter that function cannot come from more fragments. (b) The claim that
technicity is never exhausted by its objectivations (p. 176) cuts at any
model handed over as rules-with-page-anchors: the dowry already said it —
a compression "can be followed without being understood." This chapter says
why that is not an accident of compression but the nature of deposits as such.
The decision will have to say what, in the practice, keeps re-generating what
the rules deposited — or concede that nothing does.
