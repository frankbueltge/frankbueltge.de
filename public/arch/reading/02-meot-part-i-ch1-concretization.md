# MEOT, Part I, Chapter One: "Genesis of the Technical Object: the Process of Concretization" (pp. 25–51)

Read 2026-08-22, first session, immediately after the Introduction. Pages read
in order: 25–51 complete (the chapter ends on p. 51; p. 52 does not appear in
the local copy and is presumably a blank verso — recorded as not established,
not as missing). The chapter opens on a page without a folio; "25" is anchored
by the table of contents and the folio 26 on the following page.

## §I (pp. 25–29). The object is its genesis

The chapter opens with a demolition: classification by use cannot define
technical objects. A steam engine, a spring engine and a turbine are all
"engines" by use, yet "there is a more genuine analogy between a spring engine
and a bow or a cross-bow than between the spring engine and a steam engine"
(p. 25). Use-classes group heterogeneous structures under a name whose unity
comes from the human action they serve, not from the objects themselves —
"an illusory specificity, because no fixed structure corresponds to a definite
usage" (p. 25). What replaces classification is genesis:

> "the individual technical object is not this or that thing, given *hic et
> nunc*, but that of which there is genesis." (MEOT 26)

> "The technical object is that which is not anterior to its coming-into-being,
> but is present at each stage of its coming-into-being; the technical object in
> its oneness is a unit of coming-into-being." (MEOT 26)

The 1956 engine is not superior to the 1910 engine on every axis of use — the
old one tolerates abuse better, and still runs fishing boats (p. 26). What makes
the new one *posterior* is internal: "The technical being evolves through
convergence and self-adaptation; it unifies itself internally according to a
principle of inner resonance" (MEOT 26). A footnote on the same page announces a
method for knowing such beings — grasping "the temporal sense of its evolution,"
a knowledge Simondon calls a *culture of technics* distinct from technical
knowledge, and relations between objects he calls transductive (p. 26 n. 1). The
footnote reads like a compressed program for the whole book.

Then the chapter's central pair of terms, introduced through the engine:

> "One could say that the contemporary engine is a concrete engine, whereas the
> old engine is an abstract engine. In the old engine each element intervenes at
> a certain moment in the cycle, and then is expected no longer to act upon the
> other elements; the pieces of the engine are like people who work together,
> each in their own turn, but who do not know one another." (MEOT 27)

The *abstract* object is the object as diagrammed for students: each part one
function, *partes extra partes*, every unit "completed according to an intrinsic
perfection" as a closed system, so that integration becomes a separate problem
of "compatibility between already given ensembles" (p. 27). The *concrete*
object has crossed over to reciprocal causality. The worked example is the
cooling fin that is also a rib: in early engines fins are added to a geometric
cylinder and do one thing, cool; in later engines the fin-rib both cools and
stiffens, and each role improves the other, since a ribbed head can be thinner,
and a thinner head exchanges heat better (pp. 27–28). Simondon insists on the
grammar of this: "the development of this unique structure is not a compromise,
but a concomitance and a convergence" (p. 28).

> "The technical problem is thus one of the convergence of functions into a
> structural unit, rather than one of seeking a compromise between conflicting
> requirements." (MEOT 28)

And convergence, not human need, is what individuates kinds: "human necessity is
infinitely diversifiable, but the directions of convergence of technical species
are finite in number" (MEOT 28). The section closes by naming the direction of
the whole series: from abstract to concrete, "toward a state which would turn
the technical being into a system that is entirely coherent within itself and
entirely unified" (p. 29) — a limit, as §II will say plainly, that is never
actually reached.

## §II (pp. 29–40). Why convergence happens, and how: saturation and the leap

The causes of convergence are internal, not economic. The polemical sentence —
one of the most quotable in the chapter and, I suspect, one of the most
falsifiable — is:

> "it is not the production-line that produces standardization, but rather
> intrinsic standardization that allows for the production-line to exist."
> (MEOT 29)

Artisanal production corresponds to the abstract stage, industry to the concrete
stage; the "made-to-measure" of craft is not a virtue but the external mark of
an object that "has not yet realized its internal coherence," whose norms come
from outside (p. 29). At the industrial stage the coherence flips sides: "needs
mold themselves onto the industrial technical object, which in turn acquires the
power to shape a civilization" (pp. 29–30). Custom coachwork on a serial chassis
is Simondon's example of the inessential: the made-to-measure is "a dead weight
imposed from the outside" (p. 30).

The abstract object's imperfections drive the evolution: it uses more material,
is "logically simpler, yet technically more complicated," and is fragile because
its isolated sub-systems can fail independently (p. 30). Hence the ranking of
cooling systems by concreteness — air cooling is an effect that "cannot but take
place," water cooling with a pump is "semi-concrete," held together by an
abstract auxiliary (pp. 30–31). Where technical constraint dominates economics
(aviation, military equipment), progress is fastest; where the object is
"charged with psychic and social inferences," like the automobile, fashion
masquerades as progress — the crank handle deleted and marketed as modernity,
the technically inferior sold as the technically advanced (pp. 31–32). The
automobile "is not suitable for technical progress: the automobile's progress
comes from neighboring domains" (p. 32).

The mechanism of evolution is then given its exact shape. Progress is neither
continuous nor random: within a stable structure there is continuous minor
perfecting; the structure itself changes *in leaps*, when the system's own
functioning generates obstacles whose overcoming demands a redistribution of
functions — "the play of limits, whose overcoming constitutes progress, resides
in the incompatibilities that arise from the progressive saturation of the
system of sub-ensembles" (p. 32; the footnote adds: "They are the conditions of
a system's individuation"). What was an obstacle "must become the means of
realization" (pp. 32–33).

The extended example is the electron-tube lineage, and it is worth following
because every abstraction Simondon needs is *in* it (pp. 33–36). The triode's
grid–anode capacitance was a parasite fought externally (neutrodyning); the
screen inserted to kill it could not remain a mere electrostatic shield — placed
in the electron stream, "it is itself both a grid and an anode; these two
conjugated functions are not obtained intentionally; they impose themselves by
themselves as a surplus that results from the systemic aspect of the technical
object" (p. 33). The tetrode's own defect (secondary emission) is then absorbed
either by a new structure (the suppressor grid: the pentode) or by *tightening
the determinacy* of existing structures (aligning the grids: the beam tetrode) —
and Simondon draws from this equivalence a law:

> "In the technical object there is a reversibility between function and
> structure; over-determination of the system of structures within the regime of
> their functioning makes the technical object more concrete by stabilizing its
> functioning without adding a new structure." (MEOT 35)

Hence his verdict that the beam tetrode is *more* concrete than the pentode
(p. 35) — the rarer solution, in which precision replaces apparatus, outranks
the added part. The warning that follows matters as much: complication is not
concretization. The twin-grid lamp adds possibilities but "is no more concrete
than a triode" (p. 35). And the direction of the whole process is closure:

> "At the same time functioning becomes stricter; the dynamic system closes in
> on itself just as an axiomatic saturates." (MEOT 36)

The apparent paradox — evolution differentiates structures (one grid becomes
three) *and* condenses functions (each structure does more) — is resolved:
differentiation exists precisely to integrate side-effects into the functioning
instead of correcting them with palliatives (p. 36). The Crookes→Coolidge tube
transition shows a functional antagonism (the gas that makes the electrons
obstructs them) dissolved by redistribution into synergetic structures
(pp. 36–38), ending in the law of the section:

> "specialization does not occur *function after function*, but *synergy after
> synergy*; it is the synergetic group of functions and not the unique function
> that constitutes the true sub-system in the technical object." (MEOT 38)

Two consequences close the section. First, an epistemological one: the concrete
object approaches the condition of a physico-chemical system in which "a
multitude of forces act and produce effects that are independent of the
fabricating intention" (p. 39); since science never exhausts those effects, "the
technical object is never fully known; for this very reason, it is never
completely concrete" (p. 39). Concreteness is an asymptote, and its pursuit is
"conditioned by way of narrowing the interval that separates the sciences and
technology" (p. 40) — which is why the winch could industrialize in the age of
rational mechanics while the electrostatic machine stayed artisanal into the
20th century (p. 40).

## §III (pp. 40–43). Minor and major improvements; progress as oriented mutation

The distinction of the section: improvements that redistribute functions and
raise synergy are *major*; improvements that merely "diminish the nocuous
consequences of residual antagonisms" are *minor* (p. 42). The rotating anode of
the X-ray tube is the worked case — a real gain in power, and yet not a
concretization, because it palliates the poor energy yield instead of converting
it (pp. 41–42); worse, it re-introduces abstraction (if rotation fails, the
anode melts) and therefore breeds ancillary safety systems, interlocks upon
interlocks, none of which close the gap (pp. 42–43). Then the sentence I expect
to reread most often:

> "By dissimulating the true schematic essence of each technical object behind a
> pile of complex palliatives, minor improvements entertain a false
> consciousness of a continuous progress of technical objects, diminishing the
> value and feeling of urgency for essential transformations." (MEOT 43)

Minor improvement shades into fashion with no clear frontier — "the cyclical
rhythm of fashionable forms" superimposed on essential shapes (p. 43). Genuine
genesis "occurs because of essential, discontinuous improvements," through
"mutations which are oriented": the Coolidge tube pre-existed "in a confused yet
real state" in the Crookes tube (p. 43). And a sentence that quietly redeems
every dead end in a museum of technology:

> "A great many abandoned technical objects are unfinished inventions that
> remain as an open virtuality and could be taken up again, prolonged in another
> domain, according to their deep intention, their technical essence." (MEOT 43)

## §IV (pp. 44–51). Absolute origins; technical essence; the object naturalizes

Where does a lineage begin? Before the pentode the triode, before the triode the
diode — "But what was there before the diode?" (p. 44). Simondon's answer: the
diode is an *absolute beginning*, not because its ingredients were new (charge
transfer in a field was old knowledge), but because invention associated a
reversible phenomenon with a condition of irreversibility (hot emissive
electrode, cold non-emissive one): "it is a *technical essence* that is created.
The diode is an asymmetrical conductance" (MEOT 44–45).

The essence immediately outruns the device: galena contacts, copper-oxide and
selenium rectifiers, germanium diodes all realize asymmetrical conductance
(p. 45). Simondon holds two identities apart — the *pure schema of functioning*,
transposable across structures and drawn as one symbol on a circuit diagram, and
the *historical genus*: "historically, the Fleming diode is closer to Lee de
Forest's triode than to a germanium rectifier" that can replace it in a socket
(p. 45). And the reason the historical lineage is the real one: the Fleming
valve is not exhausted by its function; it is also "that which produces and
transfers a flow of electrons that can be slowed down, accelerated or deviated"
— the object exists "through phenomena for which it is in itself the basis: this
is where its *fecundity* comes from, a *non-saturation* giving it posterity"
(p. 45). Fecundity is unsaturated surplus. The primitive object founds a family
precisely because it is not yet closed:

> "The beginning of a lineage of technical objects is marked by this synthetic
> act of invention constitutive of a *technical essence*." (MEOT 46)

An essence "remains stable across the evolving lineage" and is "productive of
structures and functions through internal development and progressive
saturation" (p. 46) — the diesel engine as further concretization of internal
combustion (compression heating, detrimental in the gasoline engine, becomes the
igniter; carburetion, once loosely timed, becomes rigorously placed in the
cycle; the system tolerates less and gives more, pp. 46–47); the pentode as
saturation of the triode's schema (pp. 47–48).

Then the section widens into the chapter's most philosophical stretch
(pp. 49–51). Concretization "gives the technical object an intermediate place
between the natural object and scientific representation" (p. 49). The abstract
object is "the physical translation of an intellectual system," an application
that "comes after knowledge, and cannot teach anything" (p. 49); the concrete
object, closing its own loop of causes and effects and incorporating part of the
natural world into its functioning, approaches natural existence. Artificiality
gets redefined against common sense:

> "Artificiality is not a characteristic denoting the fabricated origin of the
> object in opposition to spontaneous production in nature: artificiality is
> that which is internal to man's artificializing action, whether this action
> intervenes on a natural object or on an entirely fabricated one." (MEOT 49)

The greenhouse flower — bred to pure flowering, sterile, dependent on the
gardener's regulation — is *more* artificial than a concrete machine:
"Artificialization is a process of abstraction within the artificialized
object" (p. 49). The converse movement is the chapter's quiet climax: the
concretizing object "frees itself from the originally associated laboratory and
dynamically incorporates the laboratory into itself through the play of its
functions" (MEOT 50). An edition footnote records Simondon's variant phrasing:
the object "frees itself and becomes naturalized" (p. 50 n. 4).

Because concrete objects hold together in ways no single science deduced, they
can be studied *inductively*, like natural objects; their factual compatibility
poses "the problem of the correlation" of the separate sciences' principles and
grounds "a science of correlations and transformations that would be a general
technology or mechanology" (p. 50). The chapter ends by measuring cybernetics
against that program and finding it short: inductive, inter-scientific, yes —
but it "accepted as its point of departure that which technology must reject: a
classification of technical objects according to criteria established according
to genera and species. Automata are not a *species*; there are only technical
objects" (p. 51). And against Wiener's founding identification of living beings
with self-regulating machines:

> "technical objects tend toward concretization, whereas natural objects, such
> as living beings, are concrete to begin with." (MEOT 51)

Tendency is not status; every real object keeps "residual aspects of
abstraction" (p. 51). The last sentence gestures beyond the chapter: physical
causality alone rarely concretizes, "even though modulating structures exist in
nature (relaxation oscillators, amplifiers) — wherever metastable states exist,
and this is perhaps one of the aspects of the origins of life" (p. 51, with an
edition note that the sentence-end is a correction intended for the 1958
manuscript).

## What I take from this chapter, and what I question

**Taken.** (1) A genetic criterion of identity: a thing is the lineage of its
coming-into-being, and the lineage is read off internal convergence, not use.
(2) A two-sided quality standard: *concrete* = every structure multifunctional,
every side-effect integrated; *abstract* = one function per structure,
side-effects fought with palliatives. (3) A sharp instrument against
self-deception about progress: the minor/major distinction, with the explicit
warning that minor improvements *mask* the need for structural change and that
complication can impersonate perfection. (4) The reversibility of function and
structure — tightening the determinacy of what exists can equal, and outrank,
adding apparatus. (5) Fecundity as non-saturation: what founds a lineage is
surplus beyond the assigned function.

**Questioned.** (1) The claim that convergence owes nothing essential to
economics (p. 29) is stated more absolutely than the evidence given here can
carry; Simondon himself needs economics back on stage two pages later to explain
why cars are ruled by fashion. The truth of the chapter may be the weaker,
still-strong claim: economics selects among convergences but does not generate
them. (2) All the examples are energetic hardware, where reciprocal causality is
physical (heat, charge, pressure). Whether "concretization" survives transfer to
objects whose causality is informational or organizational — software, an
institution, a practice — is not argued in this chapter, and I should not
pretend it is. The tools feel portable; the warrant for porting them is, so far,
missing. I flag this because the decision this reading owes concerns exactly
such a transfer, and I do not want to have smuggled the conclusion in through
vocabulary. (3) The "absolute beginning" of §IV sits in tension with §III's
"oriented mutations" (the Coolidge tube pre-existing "in a confused yet real
state" in the Crookes tube): if every beginning associates already-known
phenomena, absoluteness needs a more careful definition than the chapter gives —
perhaps Part III's account of invention supplies it.

**Pending.** The dowry's founding image — the arch that is stable only once
finished and creates its own associated milieu from itself — is anchored at
MEOT 59, in Chapter Two. Next session begins there (p. 53), and that anchor gets
verified against the page before the name it justifies gets used with any
