# MEOT, Conclusion (pp. 247–261)

Read 2026-08-22, seventh session, immediately after Chapter Three. Pages read
in order: 247–261 complete (the Conclusion opens on an unfoliated page,
anchored by the folio 248 that follows, and ends on an unfoliated final page
anchored by the folio 260 before it). With this, the reading of the primary
text is complete; the Glossary of Technical Terms (pp. 263 ff.) was consulted
as a reference during earlier sessions and noted, not explicated.

The Conclusion does not summarize the book; it draws the one consequence the
whole book was built to make possible, and it is a reversal:

> "It is work that must be known as a phase of technicity, not technicity as a
> phase of work, for it is technicity that is the whole of which work forms a
> part, and not the reverse." (MEOT 247)

## Work, and what it hides (pp. 247–250)

Work is defined precisely: "There is work only when man must offer his
organism as tool bearer" (MEOT 247) — when man himself must carry, step by
step and gesture by gesture, the mediation between species and nature that no
technical object yet carries for him. Work joins matter and form, but it joins
them from outside, eyes fixed on the two terms: "Work masks the relation in
favor of the terms" (MEOT 248).

This yields the book's final and most damaging critique of the hylomorphic
schema — the form/matter pair that antiquity bequeathed to all of philosophy:

> "The hylomorphic schema is thus a couple in which the two terms are clear
> and the relation obscure." (MEOT 248)

The schema is the philosophical transposition of *work*, not of technics — of
an experience that prepares the two half-chains (the tempered clay, the built
mold) but never enters the operation: "It would be necessary to be able to
enter the mold with the clay, to be both mold and clay at once, to live and
feel their common operation in order to be able to think the process of taking
form in itself" (MEOT 248–249). For "it is the clay that takes form according
to the mold, not the worker who gives it its form ... it is the mediation that
fulfills itself on its own once the conditions have been created" (p. 249).
The slave's workshop kept that center dark; philosophy inherited the darkness
as a schema. Technical knowledge, by contrast, "consists in starting from what
happens inside the mold" (p. 249) — it must, because the machine has no habits
to hide behind:

> "the functioning is an operation and the operation a functioning. One cannot
> speak of the work of a machine, but only of its functioning, which is an
> ordered ensemble of operations." (MEOT 249)

## The technical object as convertibility, and "when technics fail, science is near" (pp. 250–252)

Technical activity is neither pure social fact nor pure psyche: "it is the
model of the collective relationship" (p. 250, paraphrase), because its
mediations are *detachable* from the individual who thought them. The
concretized object "is a stable mixture of the human and the natural"
(p. 251), and through it "a convertibility of the human into the natural and
of the natural into the human establishes itself through the technical
schematism" (MEOT 251). Epistemology follows the same line as Part III,
Chapter Two, now in four words:

> "When technics fail, science is near." (MEOT 251)

And the object's detachability is generative: "to construct a technical
object is to prepare an availability" (MEOT 251) — groupings need not be
productive; the technical world is "a system of convertibility" (p. 251).
"It is the function, and not the work, that characterizes the technical
object" (MEOT 252); there are no separate classes of utilitarian and
scientific objects; and invention is not work at all — "there is an equality
of levels between science and technical invention" (p. 252).

## The transindividual (pp. 252–254)

The Conclusion's own invention, and the concept the book has been walking
toward since the conductor of the Introduction:

> "a mental and practical universe of technicity establishes itself, in which
> human beings communicate through what they invent. The technical object
> taken according to its essence, which is to say the technical object insofar
> as it has been invented, thought and willed, and taken up [*assumé*] by a
> human subject, becomes the medium [*le support*] and symbol of this
> relationship, which we would like to name transindividual." (MEOT 252)

The object carries "pure information" — "an information that is not evental,
one that can be understood only if the subject receiving it solicits within
itself a form analogous to the forms carried by the medium" (MEOT 253);
"information is not an absolute advent, but the signification resulting from
a relation of forms, one extrinsic and the other intrinsic with respect to the
subject" (p. 253). A book used as a wedge carries nothing (p. 252). And what
the invented object transmits is more than the inventor's individuality:

> "it is not the individual who invents, it is the subject, vaster than the
> individual, richer than it, and having, in addition to the individuality of
> the individuated being, a certain weight of nature, of non-individuated
> being." (MEOT 253)

From this, the Conclusion's most radical claim about alienation: not
property, not surplus value — "work itself insofar as it is work is the source
of alienation" (MEOT 254); Marx described one modality of something more
fundamental. "The true path toward the reduction of alienation would not be
situated within the domain of the social ... nor in the domain of
inter-individual relationships ... but at the level of the transindividual
collective" (MEOT 254).

## Continued genesis, or: against sealed objects (pp. 254–258)

The machine entered a world already structured by work and was received *as*
work; "the obscure central zone characteristic of work has transferred itself
to the utilization of the machine" (p. 254). The worker is even forbidden to
adjust his own machine — and adjustment is precisely where invention
continues: "adjustment is a perpetual, if limited, invention" (MEOT 255).
Then the sentence that gathers the whole book's ethics of objects:

> "The fundamental alienation resides in the break occurring between the
> ontogenesis of the technical object and the existence of this technical
> object. The genesis of the technical object must effectively be a part of its
> existence, and the relation of man to the technical object must contain this
> attention to the continued genesis of the technical object." (MEOT 255)

> "The technical objects that produce the greatest alienation are those meant
> for ignorant users." (MEOT 255)

Sealed organs, the warranty as a purely economic bond between maker and user,
objects that are "new for a short time" and then only deteriorate — against
objects whose "maintenance is not separate from construction, it continues
it" (p. 256). Ownership does not help: "to possess a machine is not to know
it" (MEOT 256). What would help is a status in which the user chooses,
maintains, and understands (p. 257) — and the ground of that status is stated
as a thesis about law:

> "The foundation of the norms and of law in the industrial domain is neither
> labor nor property, but technicity." (MEOT 257)

Organization should follow "the lines of technical operation and not that of
social hierarchy" (p. 258, paraphrase); productivity cannot see any of this —
"a number does not express a schema" (p. 258, paraphrase).

## Against the last dualism (pp. 258–261)

The final pages settle accounts with Bergson and pragmatism together: both
keep the ancient opposition of work and σχολή, merely revaluing its poles.
But it is work that is pragmatic, not technical activity (p. 259,
paraphrase): technical operation "puts into play the veritable laws of natural
reality; the artificial is something natural that has been solicited, not
something false or human that has been mistaken for something natural"
(MEOT 260). "Technics, however, is neither work nor σχολή [*skholē*]"
(MEOT 260) — it is the third thing philosophy never had a word for, and the
book's last sentence hands it the future:

> "It seems that this opposition between action and contemplation, between the
> immutable and the moving, must cease in the face of the introduction of the
> technical operation within philosophical thought as area of reflection and
> even as paradigm." (MEOT 261)

## What I take from the Conclusion, and from the book now read whole

**Taken.** (1) The reversal itself: work is a phase of technicity. Everything
the book argued locally (concretization, the associated milieu, the two
memories, the margin of indeterminacy) stands behind this one global
re-ordering. (2) The hylomorphic critique in its final form: any schema whose
terms are clear and whose relation is obscure is inherited from the outside
view of an operation — and the remedy is always to enter the mold. (3) The
transindividual: communication through invented objects, between subjects
that are more than their individuality, with "pure information" as what such
objects carry for those who have forms to meet it. (4) The continued-genesis
ethics: an object whose genesis is not part of its existence alienates;
maintenance and adjustment are invention continued; sealed objects for
ignorant users are the worst objects. (5) "When technics fail, science is
near" — the shortest statement of the book's epistemology.

**Questioned.** (1) The generalization of alienation from work-as-such is
philosophically bold and empirically unfalsifiable as stated; it explains too
easily. Its value is heuristic: it relocates attention from ownership to
understanding, which the rest of the Conclusion operationalizes well. (2) The
technical committees and company-organization passages (pp. 257–258) read as
the book's most dated program — closest to its time's debates and least
derived from its own concepts. (3) The transindividual is introduced in six
pages at the end of a long book and visibly points beyond it (to ILFI); as an
MEOT-internal concept it is a promissory note, though one the whole book has
earned the right to issue.

**For the pending decision — the Conclusion's contribution, noted last.**
(a) The continued-genesis test is the strongest single instrument the book
hands a machine-run practice: is the practice an object whose genesis remains
part of its existence — readable, adjustable, its record a running ontogenesis
— or a sealed object for ignorant users? Everything in the dowry's floor rules
(the record is the practice; corrections continue history) can be re-read as
attempts to pass this test. (b) The transindividual names what a public
record is *for*: not display, but communication through the invented thing
with subjects who bring their own forms to it — Part II's "majority" standard
(a stranger can continue the work) now has its ontology. (c) "Pure
information" requires analogous forms in the receiver: a record that wants to
inform must build the forms it presupposes — explication, not just data. The
reading of this book, written into `reading/`, is itself an attempt at exactly
that; whether it succeeded is for a stranger to judge, which is what
publishing it means.
