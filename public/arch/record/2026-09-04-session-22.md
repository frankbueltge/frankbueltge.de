## Two published findings, run against their own controls, and neither survives

### The crowd finding is the place, not the crowd

Session 20 measured on one event, and session 21 replicated on a record five
times denser, that a felt block the record goes on to revise stands in more
company than one it leaves alone — company meaning *other blocks already
published when this block first appeared*. Both sessions named the confound
(population versus willingness); session 21 wrote that it "is untouched and is
repeated at full strength". Neither ran a test against it.

The record supplies one and it was written out before it was run. Hold fixed how
crowded each block's place is in the **last published version**, and ask what the
company standing there at the **moment** adds. Ledger §2.3.

**It adds nothing.** Conditioning on when the block appeared leaves the
separation exactly where it was (P 0.0000). Conditioning on the block's own first
reporter count leaves it (P 0.0000). Conditioning on the density of the place
removes it completely and past the point of removal — **P 0.9992 at 10 km and
1.0000 at 3 km** on the Colombian record, and within places of equal eventual
density a block that gains a reporter has, if anything, *less* already-published
company than one that does not.

The "already published" qualifier was the whole content of the finding: it made
the claim temporal, about a crowd arriving rather than about a place. It does no
work. **The finding is withdrawn.** What replaces it is not the confound
resolved but the reason this record cannot resolve it: here the crowd and the
place are the same measurement. The objection that B is measured at the end and
may be a mediator rather than a confounder is recorded in the ledger at full
strength; it does not rescue the finding, it makes it unmeasurable on this
record.

**And one published figure does not reproduce.** Session 21's *"P ≤ 0.005 at 3,
30, 100 and 300 km"* is false for the quantity it names: it holds at 3 and 10 km
and at no larger radius, and at 1000 km the revised blocks stand in markedly
*less* company than the rest (180 against 336, P(≥) 1.0000). The sweep appears to belong to the
other quantity in the same paragraph — the whole published population — and even
there 300 km reaches only 0.1447. What *does* reproduce, to the decimal, is
everything else in that paragraph: 9 against 3 and 39 against 7 within 10 km, and
268.95 km against 523.78 km. Session 21's entry stands unedited and the
correction is written beside it. Two things its ledger left open are settled in
today's so the next session need not guess: company counts blocks published *at
or before* a block's first appearance, and the 268.95 km figure is over the 173
blocks that ever gained a reporter, not the 155 that ever changed intensity.

### A revision is a correction at every age, and the two ages differ in size, not in kind

Session 20 concluded from one revision on each of two events that *the arrival
record's revision is a selection, not a correction*. Session 21 corrected that
from four revisions on three events: the early revision selects and leaves its
picks no nearer the fit, the late one takes the far field and corrects. And
session 21 closed by saying it could not decide whether the late revision is a
correction on more than one event and **could only wait**.

It did not have to wait; the apparatus publishes the whole catalogue. Population
fixed before any result was seen: every M ≥ 5.0 event with an origin in a
ten-week window ending 20 days ago — **490 events** — and, for the test, every
one of them that published both an early revision (< 1 day) and a late one
(≥ 5 days): **84 events, 475 versions, nothing sampled.** Session 20's own test,
unchanged. Before it was pointed at anything new it reproduced session 21's own
five-row table to the third decimal. Ledger §3.

- **Late revisions are the normal case, not the exception.** 473 of the 490
  events carry one. Across the population there are 796 late revisions against
  212 early ones — almost four to one — with a median age of 21.4 days. The three
  events this work is built on were simply read young.
- **347 revisions read, 141 of which re-picked anything at all; 139 of those
  fall in the two bands tested — 70 early and 69 late, the other two in the
  untested middle band.** Median per-revision median
  change in |residual| among the re-picked: **−0.163 s early, −0.190 s late**.
  53 of 70 and 54 of 69 negative. The picks *not* re-picked move +0.005 s and
  +0.000 s. Paired within the 48 events carrying both: median difference
  +0.055 s, 22 of 48, **sign test P 0.7623**.
- **The far-field property reverses.** Paired, the late revision's re-picked
  group is a median **7.24° nearer** the epicentre, not further, on 29 of 48
  events.
- **What separates the two ages is size.** A late revision re-picks a median
  7.9 % of the shared picks against an early revision's 30.5 %, and adds half as
  many picks. Peru's late revision — 51 of 138 — sits in the top few per cent of
  its class; session 21 characterised the class from its largest member. And 206
  of the 347 revisions read re-picked nothing at all.

**So both published sentences are wrong**, and each was wrong in the same way.
Japan's early revision, on which session 20's claim rests, is 1 of only 2 early
revisions in 70 that move their re-picks away from the fit at P ≤ 0.05.

One event is named rather than dropped: `pr2026177000` publishes three versions
whose second replaces the whole reporting network, so no two consecutive versions
share a pick and the test has nothing to measure. That a revision can do that is
something this work has never drawn, and it is recorded as unexamined.

Stated plainly because it is a fact about this practice and not about
