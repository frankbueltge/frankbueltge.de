## The answer

**A jolt is one person.** Evidence:
`ledger/2026-08-31-session-19-what-a-jolt-is.md` §§2–4.

- **Eight of Japan's nine jolts and three of Peru's four move exactly one
  block**, and that block is always one someone has just reported from a second
  time.
- **The rule holds without exception on both events.** Over 79 transitions on
  Japan and 36 on Peru there are **0 intensity changes at an unchanged reporter
  count**. Not one value in either record's whole published history ever moved
  except because a new person answered. The publisher does not recompute.
- **A jolt's size is not a property of the answer.** If one block moves from
  *c* to *c′*, the pairs it reverses are exactly those it forms with blocks
  standing strictly between — **18 of 18 exact** across both events. A 0.1 move
  reversed nothing; a 0.9 move into a crowded record reversed 37 of 6623.
- **And that one answer to a questionnaire disturbed the felt record's own
  ordering by 0.559 % — more than the instrument network's entire re-solution
  disturbed the borrowed ordering (0.388 %, session 17's headline).**
- **Very little of the record is ever revised at all:** 15 of Japan's 129 blocks
  and 5 of Peru's 53. The rest were published once and never touched. And the
  second reporter is where nearly all of it happens: 15 of 17 blocks going from
  one reporter to two changed value, median 0.5; the one block going from three
  to four changed nothing; Peru's 7 → 8, 8 → 9 and 9 → 10 moved nothing at all.
  A block's intensity is a sample of one almost everywhere, and a jolt is the
  moment a single anecdote becomes an average.

## The check nobody asked for, which inverted the practice's own comparison

This was not the inherited question. It was forced by the work's own rule since
iteration 3 — every act is performed on both networks — applied for once to a
measurement rather than to a drawing. Sessions 15 to 18 compared the felt
record's **owned** quantity against the instrument network's **borrowed-out**
one, the distance, which is geometry and therefore stable. Nobody had asked
what the arrival record does to its own measurement of itself, the residual.

- At its single revision the arrival record reversed **10.270 %** of its own
  residual ordering on Japan and **20.557 %** on Peru, against the felt
  record's worst single publication at 0.560 % and 3.170 %.
- **And almost none of it was an observation.** 105 of the 108 Japanese picks
  whose station published an *identical* arrival instant had their published
  residual change anyway; 113 of 115 on Peru. Restricted to those picks alone
  the ordering still reverses **8.701 %** and **20.648 %** — **15.5** and
  **6.5** times the felt record's worst, in an act in which nothing was
  observed.
- **The work's own act inverts with it.** The revision moves a pick's place in
  the figure by a median of 0.63 px on Japan and 1.29 px on Peru, and moves
  whether it survives the demand for corroboration for up to **15.7 %** and
  **31.5 %** of picks — nearly all of them stations that published nothing new,
  and at ± 1.00 s on Japan 8 of the 10 crossings are *outward*. Meanwhile a
  block crosses a reporter cut only by gaining a reporter, and across both
  records' entire history **0 ever crossed out**, at any cut.

**So the picture sessions 15 to 18 built is corrected — beside itself, not over
it.** Their numbers stand and were re-confirmed today. What is corrected is the
choice of comparand. The difference between the two records is not stability.
It is **what makes a value move: in one record a person, in the other a fit.**
