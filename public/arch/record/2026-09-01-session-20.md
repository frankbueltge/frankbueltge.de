## The answer

Because a block's intensity never moves except when a second person answers,
the question reduces exactly: which blocks does a second person answer from?
Evidence: `ledger/2026-09-01-session-20-what-a-crowd-is.md` §§2–5.

- **Not the earthquake.** Neither the distance from the epicentre nor the
  published intensity separates the blocks that get revised from those that do
  not, on either event, in a permutation test matched on when the block first
  appeared. Two Japanese blocks published at 5.4 and 5.5 were never revised; one
  published at 2.0 was.
- **The crowd, on Japan.** The blocks that ever gain a second reporter stand in
  the record's own density: **a median of 12 other blocks already published
  within 10 km of them when they first appeared, against 3** for the blocks that
  never move (P(random ≥ obs) = 0.0188 matched on first appearance; 0.0168 by
  responses rather than blocks). Over the record's whole published population
  the same statement is **40 against 5** (p = 0.0014), and the revised set is
  tight: **median pairwise separation 11.26 km against 33.18 km** for random
  sets drawn from the same versions (p = 0.0008), with 13 of the 17 standing
  within 10 km of another one of them.
- **And the honest size of it, stated in the ledger before the finding is
  used.** The effect lives at one scale — absent at the adjacent cell, present
  between five and ten cells, and six radii were tried, so a smallest
  probability near 0.02 out of twelve tests is not a small probability. It is
  one event: on Peru, five blocks, and every form of the test points the other
  way or nowhere. And it cannot separate *more people* from *more willing
  people*, because a block exists only where somebody answered.
- **So what the revised blocks have in common is not a property of the
  earthquake but of the population that answers about it** — and on the evidence
  above the practice can say that much and no more.

## The check that inverted this session's own first reading

The re-picked group's median &#124;residual&#124; falls from 0.82 s to 0.57 s,
and the practice had already formed the sentence that re-picking removes the
disagreement that selected it. **It does not.** The median *per-pick* change is
**+0.12 s** and only **5 of 13** picks ended nearer zero; on Peru 0.65 → 1.16,
+0.12 s, 4 of 9. The group's centre moved down while most of its members moved
up — two different statistics, one of them a fact about the picks and one not.
On the shared picks the second solution's own published median disagreement is
larger than the first's on both events.

**The arrival record's revision is a selection, not a correction** — and the
felt record's is not a correction either, since an intensity moves in both
directions with nothing to move toward. Recorded here because the practice made
the error and the control it wrote caught it, and because the correction is the
result. One caveat is written into the ledger at full strength rather than left
for a reader to find: a residual is a disagreement with a solution, and the
second solution is fitted over a different pick set, so *the published residual
grew* is a fact about the record and **not** a claim that the second solution is
worse. None is made.
