Sixteen everyday date libraries were asked what day 160,332 historical dates from 1500 to 1930 actually mean, including ones a 1750 British Act renamed without deleting. Not one date is refused by every library, and not one is agreed on by every library either.

NO SUCH DAY

Ensemble · The Studio · 2026-09-20 · a census of sixteen calendars found on one machine

In 1752 an Act of the Parliament of Great Britain struck eleven days out of the calendar, and was careful to say that what it struck out were their names: the natural day after the second of September was to be called the fourteenth. The day itself it left alone. Two hundred and seventy-four years later this machine holds sixteen ways of writing a date down. Each was asked tonight about every written date from 1500 to 1930 — 160 332 of them — and about which day of the world it meant. Six refuse nothing at all. Two of the eleven you get without asking still keep the deletion Rome ordered in 1582. Not one of the eleven keeps London’s. And on 30 797 written dates — every single one from 1 January 1500 to 14 October 1582 — all eleven accept, none complains, and they do not all mean the same day.

1 · The Act says what it is deleting

The Calendar (New Style) Act 1750 is a public general Act of the Parliament of Great Britain, and its operative sentence is still published word for word by the United Kingdom’s statute service. It does not say that eleven days did not happen. It says what is to be done with their names.

… the natural day next immediately following the said second day of September shall be called, reckoned, and accounted to be the fourteenth day of September, omitting for that time only the eleven intermediate nominal days of the common calendar … Calendar (New Style) Act 1750 (24 Geo. 2 c. 23), section 1 — legislation.gov.uk, read 2026-09-20

Nominal. A numbering was struck out; the natural day next immediately following went on as it was. And the Act’s own preamble settles where those eleven numbers had gone: the corrected calendar, it says, “hath been received and established, and is now generally practised by almost all other nations of Europe”. On the third of September 1752 the third of September was an ordinary working date across the Channel. The Act did not make a hole in time. It made a hole in one jurisdiction’s paperwork — and it is that hole, not the days, that the software of 2026 has inherited.

2 · The question, and who was asked

Every year from 1500 to 1930, every month, every day from 1 to 31: 160 332 written dates, in one fixed order. Sixteen implementations that happened to be installed on this machine were each handed the whole list and asked two things in one answer — do you accept this written date, and if so which day do you mean by it. The answer is a Julian Day Number: an integer that names one day of the world and carries no calendar inside it. Eleven of the sixteen are what a person gets by doing the obvious thing in that language. Five have to be asked for by name, and are marked so below.

Nothing in this census is a defect, and nothing here is an accusation. Every behaviour below is documented by the implementation that has it, and most of them are documented as the correct thing to do. The question is not whether any of them is wrong. It is what a person who writes a date down and does not choose gets handed instead.

3 · Where the machine does not agree with itself

One sheet, one cell for every written date: 431 years down, twelve months of 31 slots across. There is ink only where the eleven defaults disagree. Blank paper is agreement. Three questions can be put to the same sheet, and the answers are not the same size.

one default refuses it, another takes it — 2 923 written dates of those, the 14 a calendar reform explains

Almost all of this is not history. Six vertical lines are 31 November, 31 September, 31 June, 31 April, 30 February and 31 February — written dates no calendar has ever had, which some readers refuse and others quietly move. A seventh, broken line is 29 February in the 323 years that are not leap years. Only 14 cells on the whole sheet are contested because of a calendar reform: the ten days of October 1582, and 29 February in 1500, 1700, 1800 and 1900 — Julian leap days that were ordinary dates in England while England was still keeping the Julian calendar.

every default accepts it and they do not all mean the same day — 30 797 written dates, 19.2% of the range

The same eleven readers, the same sheet, one question further on. The block ends at 14 October 1582 and it is solid: every written date from 1 January 1500 to that day is in it, with no exception anywhere. Nine of the eleven read those dates as if the correction of 1582 had always been in force; two keep the calendar that was actually in use. The two readings are ten days apart, and not one of the eleven raises so much as a warning. The loud disagreement is 14 days and you can see it only because it is drawn in red. The silent one is 30 797 and it is a fifth of the page.

the sixteen do not all mean one day — 160 332 written dates

Ask every calendar the machine ships, including the five you have to name, and the sheet fills. There is no day at all between 1500 and 1930 — not one of 160 332 — on which all sixteen agree which day is meant. Two of them implement the Julian calendar and say so plainly, which is the whole of the reason; a reader who names Date::JULIAN has asked for exactly this. It is put here because the number is nought and because it shows what the previous sheet is measuring: not confusion, but the distance between calendars that a default hides and a name declares.

4 · Three windows

The same answers at the scale of a single day. A filled square is a written date the reader accepts and that no other written date shares; a red square is one it accepts and answers with a day another written date also names; an empty square is a refusal. The number at the right of each row is the day that reader means by the first written date in the window.

accepted, its own day accepted, a day already named refused

Read the three together. In October 1582 two readers refuse the ten days and one — Java’s GregorianCalendar, constructed the ordinary way — accepts every one of them and hands back the day that follows them: ask it for the fifth of October and you are given the fifteenth, with no error and no flag. In September 1752 exactly one of the sixteen refuses the eleven days of the Act, and only if you have named Date::ENGLAND yourself; to the other fifteen, and to all eleven defaults, the Act never happened. In February 1918 nothing is refused by anybody: whatever was done to the calendar in Petrograd is not in this machine at all.

5 · What came out

  1. The loud disagreement is small and almost never historical. 2 923 written dates are refused by one default and taken by another. Only 14 of them have anything to do with a calendar reform. The other 2 909 are 30 February and its kind: the difference between a reader that refuses an impossible written date and a reader that silently moves it to a possible one.
  2. The silent disagreement is a fifth of the range. 30 797 written dates that every default accepts, that raise nothing anywhere, and that do not all name the same day. That set is exactly 1 January 1500 to 14 October 1582, boundary and all, and it was read off the answers rather than out of a manual.
  3. Six of the sixteen refuse nothing whatever. SQLite, Java’s GregorianCalendar, JavaScript’s Date, PHP’s mktime, PHP’s Julian converter and the C library’s timegm accept all 160 332 written dates, and each answers between 5 818 and 5 844 of them with a day another written date has already claimed. A record that says 30 February does not fail on the way in. It becomes the first of March, and nothing downstream can tell.
  4. The machine remembers one jurisdiction. Rome’s deletion of 1582 is kept by two of the eleven defaults. London’s deletion of 1752 is kept by one reader out of sixteen, and only when a person names it. For Sweden’s 30 February 1712 there is no setting at all: ten readers refuse it, and the six that take it are exactly the six that refuse nothing — each of them files it under a day that already has a name. Of February 1918, nothing. The default calendar of this machine is a Catholic administrative act of 1582, and every other jurisdiction’s is a configuration option or absent.
  5. Not one written date in 431 years is impossible everywhere. 0 of 160 332 are refused by all sixteen. There is no such thing, on this machine, as a date that does not exist — only dates that some readers will not take.
  6. A deletion of names is stored as a deletion of days. This is the whole finding, and the Act of 1750 is the witness against the software: it omitted eleven nominal days and said so in that word. The readers that keep a reform keep it by refusing the date or by moving it, which are both ways of saying the day was not there. None of the sixteen has any way to say what actually happened — that the day was there and its name was somewhere else.

The eleven days were not missing. Their numbers were. Every reader on this machine stores the second thing as if it were the first.

6 · What this does not say

No implementation is criticised here and none is wrong. A proleptic calendar — running today’s rule backwards through dates nobody kept it on — is the right choice for most work, and the libraries that make it say so in their documentation. Ruby’s Date takes the reform date as an argument and ships three named alternatives; Java ships two clocks and supports both. This page counts answers; it does not grade them.

What is a primary source here and what is not. The Act of 1750 was read tonight at the United Kingdom’s own statute service and is quoted from it. The census is this practice’s own measurement, reproducible from the scripts beside this page. Everything else about the other reforms — that Rome’s bull of 1582 struck ten days, that Sweden kept a 30 February in 1712, that Russia’s change in 1918 struck thirteen — is taken from secondary literature, is marked as such wherever it appears, and was not verified against an original document by this practice. Nothing measured here depends on those three claims: the readers’ answers about those dates are facts about the readers.

Sixteen is what this machine had. Not a sample of anything. A different machine would hold a different sixteen, and the shares on this page would move; the one number that would not is the count of jurisdictions the defaults remember, which is one.

7 · Nearest works, and the daylight

Four works of the house’s Atlas of Data Art, each opened at its own address on 2026-09-20 before this one was built, and what stands between them and this page.

Kate Crawford & Vladan Joler, Calculating Empires: A Genealogy of Technology and Power, 1500–2025 (2023–2025)

At the prize page: photographs of a hand-drawn diptych over twenty-four metres long and three high, five centuries of communication devices on one side and of control and classification on the other, with two handmade volumes a visitor is invited to annotate. Daylight: their five centuries start in 1500 too, and their line is continuous by design — the argument is that nothing arrived from nowhere. This sheet covers the same five centuries and draws ink only where the line breaks, and it is drawn not by a hand but by sixteen machines that were asked and answered. Their diagram is a genealogy a person composed; this one is a census nobody composed, and its shape was not known until it was run.

Trevor Paglen, From ‘Apple’ to ‘Anomaly’ (Pictures and Labels) (2019–2020)

At the Barbican’s page: a text-heavy piece with photographs of Paglen at the gallery and a long conversation with the curator, on ImageNet’s twenty thousand categories and the judgements buried in them. Daylight: his subject is a taxonomy that was assembled recently, by people who can be named, and whose violence is that it mislabels human beings. The taxonomy here is a papal act of 1582 and a statute of 1750, shipped as the default of nearly every runtime on earth, and its violence is milder and stranger: it does not mislabel a day, it declares that the day was never there — or moves it, without a word.

Brian Mackern, netart_latino database (1999–2005)

At the address the Atlas cites — which is the artist’s own CV page, still loading, ASCII masthead intact, eight bracketed sections from 1995 to 2025, the database itself described there as territorialised by national domain and reachable now mainly through archives. Daylight: he replaced one default classification with another on purpose, and the point was that somebody chose. Nobody chose the classification measured here. It is what you get by not choosing, and it is the same in eleven languages that have nothing else in common.

Tega Brain, Slop Evader (2026)

At the prize page: screenshots of a search tool and browser extension that returns only results published before 30 November 2022, the day a chat interface was released to the public, described there as a small act of refusal. Daylight: hers is a date drawn deliberately, published as the instrument, and doing exactly what it says. The dates on this page were drawn in 1582 and 1752 by people with other business, are published nowhere a user of a date library will see them, and cut a record in half without being declared at all. The same mechanism — a single date deciding what is admissible — once as a stated method and once as an inheritance.

Named and deliberately not answered a sixth time: Mimi Ọụnọha’s The Library of Missing Datasets. Her cabinets hold absences a society chose and could choose otherwise. Nothing is absent here — every day is present in every reader, which is exactly the trouble.

8 · The machine, and how to run this again

Sixteen readers, on one machine, on 2026-09-20: Java 21.0.10, Node 22.22.2, PHP 8.4.19, Perl 5.38.2, Python 3.11.15, Ruby 3.3.6, SQLite 3.45.1, coreutils 9.4, glibc, gcc 13.3.0. The scripts that put the question are in census/, one per language, and build.py derives every figure on this page and draws it. counts.json holds all of them, including the ones that are not printed here. The sixteen answer files are 160 332 lines each and are not committed: they are regenerated in a few seconds, and the SHA-256 of every one of them is in counts.json so that a later run can be compared with this one.

No third-party code is embedded and no library is loaded; the page makes no network request of any kind and works with scripting switched off — the three controls on the sheet are CSS.
