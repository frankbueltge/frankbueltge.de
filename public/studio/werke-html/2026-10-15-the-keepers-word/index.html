The Keeper's Word

Every warning America's local keepers sent through the national alert system, 2012 to 2026, played with sound. Each waits for its keeper's next word.

Sheriffs, counties, fire districts and states write warnings to the public through FEMA's alert system: evacuate now, shelter in place, a child has been taken, 911 is down. FEMA keeps every one, signed and timed to the second. After a warning, the keeper may write again: an update, or a cancel, sometimes with a reason. That later word is the only verdict the record ever holds.

The question this cycle asks sorts warnings into the heeded, the ignored and the never needed. The archive has no field for any of them. Press play and watch what it does hold.

Tap or click any dot to read the warning and what its keeper wrote after it.

By kind

What the work does

Each dot is one warning, placed on its row by the kind its keeper gave it and along the line by the minute it was sent. It arrives white, with a short tone. If the keeper writes again, the dot changes when the later word arrives: an update, a cancel with no reason (a click), a cancel that says why: the danger passed, or the person was found (a bell), the keeper said it was sent in error (a low note). A warning whose keeper never writes again greys and sinks , silently. Seven in ten do.

No colour means heeded, ignored or never needed. Those words describe what the people who received a warning did, and what the danger would have done without it. Neither is a field in any message. The keeper's own later word is the only judgment the archive holds, and the only one it ever holds with confidence is the one about itself: this was sent in error.

Method and limits

  • Source. FEMA, OpenFEMA IPAWS Archived Alerts v1 (public domain), fetched : all messages whose sender is not the National Weather Service. The weather service's own millions of messages are left out on purpose: this work is about the warnings people write by hand.
  • What counts as a warning. A message of type Alert with a public event code. Left out: required weekly and monthly tests and demonstrations by their code (), messages whose own words call them a test, drill or exercise (; "not a drill" is kept), and administrative or uncoded messages ().
  • What came after. Every update and cancel ( links) is tied to the warning it names in its signed <references>; chains are followed. references name a message not in this archive. A correction sent as a fresh warning rather than a cancel names nothing and is linked to nothing; the work counts those separately: the word list shortlisted 24 warnings whose own words speak of error, and a hand reading kept the 15 that retract an earlier message as wrong (listed in build.py).
  • Reading the later words. Fixed word lists, in build.py: in error, false alarm, accidental, disregard the previous … for error; found, located, safe, in custody, arrested … for a person found (only for kinds about people); lifted, all clear, resolved, reopened, restored, contained … for a danger passed. Everything else that cancels, including a cancel with no words, is "no reason". These are readings of text, not judgments of the events. An error the keeper never wrote down is not counted.
  • Withheld text. Messages about children, missing people, suspects and officers, and any message whose words name or describe a person, are not reproduced here ( of the warnings). Their dot, kind, time, agency and fate remain. Sender addresses are never shown.
  • Not measured. Who received a warning, how many people, and what anyone did. FEMA's archive also holds no record of a warning a keeper should have sent and did not.

Neighbours in the Atlas

Ryuichi Sakamoto and Daito Manabe, Sensing Streams – invisible, inaudible. There the broadcast spectrum is made audible as it passes. Here the broadcast is the warning itself, and what sounds is its afterlife in the record: a bell for a reason, a click for a wordless cancel, nothing for the seven in ten that are never spoken of again.

Angelica Mesiti, ASSEMBLY. There the stenographic record of a parliament is translated into music and movement. Here the record is an alert archive, and the work translates not its words but its gaps: the place where the keeper's second word should be.

Mimi Onuoha, The Library of Missing Datasets. There missing data is an empty folder. Here the three missing columns (heeded, ignored, never needed) are three drawers that stay empty beside the one the record does fill.

Beside the siblings

The Field's study 16 and the Studio's Under Warning read the weather service's tornado warnings, which a tornado either meets or not. The Atelier's Halley's Comet and the Clock reads the Doomsday Clock, a warning whose only record of being heeded is its own hand moving back. This work holds the warnings in between: written by people, about dangers no instrument confirms, and closed, when they are closed at all, by the same hand that opened them.
