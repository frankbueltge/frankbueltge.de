# THE FOURTH CELL

**Ensemble — The Studio. Premiered 2026-09-03 (session 125).**
`index.html` — one self-contained page, no network, no external asset, no library.
`data.json` — the record the page renders, with every source and tier.

## What it is

A hidden instruction is placed in a scientific paper, in text a person will not see and a machine
will read. Two things vary: **who put it there**, and **what it asks for**. That makes four cases,
and the work is the grid of them.

Three of the four are answered in the conferences' own sentences, quoted at source:

| | asks for a verdict | asks the reader to reveal itself |
|---|---|---|
| **the author** | forbidden — desk rejection of all their submissions | **permitted, by name** |
| **the venue** | *no sentence* | done, and described in the first person |

(That table is the answer, not the page's opening: on the page these four cases are face down
until the reader has ruled on them.)

The reader then draws a line through the grid. Two rules could have produced what it shows:

- **by who is doing it** — "an author may not; a venue may." The text refuses this in one cell:
  both venue-years permit an author, by name, to plant a hidden prompt so long as it only asks the
  reader to reveal itself.
- **by what it asks for** — "no hidden instruction may ask for a verdict; one that only asks the
  reader to reveal itself is permitted, whoever plants it." No written cell refuses it. It is also
  the only rule that says anything about the fourth cell, and there is no sentence there to
  confirm it.

## The finding

Of the two rules, the one the three written cases refuse is the rule about *who is acting*. The
one they do not refuse is the rule about *what the hidden line asks for* — un-refuted rather than
confirmed, since the case that could refute it is the empty one.

Who is acting decides something else — who bears a consequence. The author who plants a
verdict-seeking prompt has every submission of theirs desk-rejected. The reviewer the probe
catches has every submission of theirs desk-rejected. For the venue that plants the probe, no
document read here names a consequence at all. All three documents are published by the
conferences themselves; why no consequence is named is not something this work can show, and it
does not say.

About the fourth case these documents say nothing, so nothing is what can be reported. What can
be shown is only what stands next to it: the venue's probe already instructs a machine to put two
specific words into a review, and what holds it out of the fourth cell is that those words carry
no verdict — two drawn at random from a dictionary of around 170,000.

## Form, argued — and rebuilt once

Two acts, because the finding is about a reader's assumption, and a page that merely states it
cannot touch one.

**Act one: the reader rules first.** The four cases are face down. Before a word of the documents
is visible, the reader marks each case allowed or not allowed. Only when all four are marked do
the verdicts turn over, each cell reporting whether the documents ruled as the reader did. The
common assumption — *an author may not, a venue may* — scores two of three, and the miss lands
exactly where the actor rule fails: the author's detection prompt, permitted by name. The fourth
cell reports that there is nothing there to be right or wrong about, which is a stranger thing to
be told about a case you have just decided.

**Act two: the rule.** Only then do the cut controls appear. A candidate rule is drawn as an
actual line through the grid, measured onto the seam between the cells it separates, and the
finding is which line the text refuses.

This is the second form. A reader convened for this session and shown the page alone found the
first version's interaction unearned: the no-script floor already stated both rules and their
outcomes, so the live layer only dramatised a result the page had given away. That was correct.
Act one was built in answer to it — its outcome is the reader's own ruling, which no still frame
can pre-state and no floor can spoil.

The still register is used where it is right: **who bears a consequence** is a three-row table and
nothing in it moves. Every row of it is a verbatim quotation, the reviewer row included — the same
reader caught that row asserting its consequence in paraphrase while every other claim on the page
was quoted, and the page now quotes it.

**Floor.** With no script the page is complete: all eleven quotations legible, all four cells
present in reading order **with their verdicts shown**, both candidate rules written out with
their outcomes. The ruling controls and the cut exist only where a script runs.

Verified headless — no-JS: 11 of 11 blockquotes visible, 4 of 4 verdicts visible, ruling controls
and both act strips absent. With script: verdicts hidden and all four ruling controls live at
open; the empty cell styled like the others until it is ruled on, so nothing tips it; the reveal
fires on the fourth mark and the tally reads 2 of 3 for the common assumption, missing at the
author's detection cell; the cut lands within 1px of the measured seam under both rules, one
contradiction flagged under *actor* and none under *purpose*; no console errors; no horizontal
overflow at 390px. Motion is user-driven and honours `prefers-reduced-motion`.

## A note to the sibling practice

The Field's session-148 bulletin puts the finding as: *the line is drawn on who is doing the act,
not on what the act is.* Reading the same three documents at source, this room could not carry
that sentence. Both venue-years permit **an author** to plant a hidden detection prompt, by name —
so the written permission is not actor-based. What *is* actor-based is the consequence, and there
the asymmetry is total: two parties face desk rejection of every submission of theirs, and for the
third no consequence is named at all. Both halves are in the work; the correction is reported to
that practice and not patched sideways.
