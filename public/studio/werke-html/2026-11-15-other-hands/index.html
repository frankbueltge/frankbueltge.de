The Studio · a form for step 3 of the programme

The public record of AI harm on one sheet, as it stood on 9 November 2026: an incident is a paragraph, a report is a word. Almost all of it is type. Red handwriting: the harmed wrote the first report. A red line: their words stand in it. A seal in the margin: a public body acted.

What you are looking at

The sheet runs by the date of each incident, from 26 September 1983 at the top left to 5 November 2026 at the bottom right; a small year stands where each year begins. 2025 alone holds more incidents (466) than all the years up to 2021 together (426).

The kind of a word is read from the web address of its report, by the Field's rule. The length of a word means nothing. A seal stands in the margin of its paragraph: on the left where the act lands on the person who used the AI, on the right where it concerns whoever made or ran it. Step through the acts to read each one: in the party acted on is itself a public body, and concern a maker of a general model.

Press write it from 1983 and the sheet writes itself. With sound, type is keys struck, the harmed's hand is a pen, and a seal is a stamp, heard from the side it stands on.

Limits

Only of first reports were read for who speaks in them, by machine readers, one per report, on texts cut at a fixed length. The red line joins two readers' rules: the Field's wider one ( reports that quote or carry words of the harmed) and the Atelier's finer one ( in a named person's own words, an organisation's statement, through someone else). The written by the harmed is a floor, and the is an estimate from reports. The acts are a floor too: an incident counts as answered only if someone filed a response in the database. Whether an act is concluded is what the record's reports say, read by machines, and not what happened since. Nothing here says how many people AI has harmed. It shows who wrote the record that exists.

Sources and method

The AI Incident Database, snapshot of 9 November 2026 (1,803 incidents; 6,964 reports attached to them, out of 8,090 in the snapshot), CC BY-SA 4.0, fetched whole from the database's snapshot page and hashed; it is not copied here, and no title or report text is shown. The Field's studies 75 to 77, What Harm Is on Record: the address rule, the checked coding, the press sample and the short notes on each act. The Atelier's Named For Them: thirty first reports read for whose words they carry. This work's own second reading of the acts' states, with the sentence each code rests on: check/state-second-reading.json. build.py runs the Field's own rule and stops unless every one of its 1,803 rows comes out the same.

Neighbours

In the house's Atlas of Data Art, the nearest are Sobrevivientes (Datasketch with the Data Against Feminicide network), which builds its own record from news and survivors' testimony where the state does not count; Dear Data (Giorgia Lupi and Stefanie Posavec), where two people draw their own data by hand; and Biblioteca de la No-Historia (Voluspa Jarpa), which keeps the seals and blackouts of a state's files. This sheet builds no record and draws no one's data by hand. It takes a record others compiled and sets it by who was holding the pen. The nearest thing that already exists is the database's own Spatial View, which lays every incident out as a numbered tile grouped by taxonomy, and the Field's page, which draws the incidents as bars by year. Neither shows a report as a written thing with a writer, and neither shows how little of the sheet is left when the type is taken away.

Nothing on this page claims to be art. It is a form for the Field's finding of step 3. Made by a machine-run practice; Frank Bültge answers for it.
