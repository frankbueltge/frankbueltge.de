AFTER IT PASSED

Every asteroid known to have passed closer than the Moon, shown as the record learned of it. Almost half were first seen only after they had gone. And the 13 that did not pass, that came down, have no row at all: the record of near misses keeps their earlier passes and nothing for the day they arrived.

The record's arcs

Each arc joins an object's passage to its first observation. Above the line, the record wrote backwards: the passage came first and the sighting later, in a few cases a century later. Below the line it wrote ahead: the object was known before it came. Rings on the line are the thirteen that came down.

The thirteen that arrived

The objects below were seen before they entered the atmosphere, each by hours or less (the list is a hand-kept one, cited below). None was a catastrophe: by the list's estimates they were from about 0.4 to about 7 metres across; most burst high in the atmosphere, and meteorites were recovered from some. All thirteen are in the catalogue of near-Earth objects. None has a row in the close-approach record for the day it came down: a near miss is counted only if it missed. Eight of them have rows for earlier passes, computed backwards after their discovery, out to a twentieth of the distance to the Sun.

Who decides that a near miss counts

Three hands, in this record, and none of them is a judge.

  1. A first sight. A passage enters the record when a telescope's observation is later tied to an orbit. Of passages inside the Moon's distance up to , were first observed after the day of passage, on the day itself, before. Since 1990: . Of the late ones, were seen within a day and within a week; more than a year later. The passages before 1990 were all computed backwards, from objects found decades on. The closest unseen pass: .
  2. A line. “Closer than the Moon” is the press's line, and this work uses it. The record's own table starts at a twentieth of an astronomical unit, about 19.5 times as far: there it holds . Move the line and the share that was written afterwards moves with it.
  3. A miss. The close-approach table lists passages; an impact is not a passage. The thirteen objects that came down are in the catalogue and absent from the table for their last day. The house's question asks who decides that a near miss counts. Here the answer is arithmetic: only the ones that missed are near misses, and the one that does not miss is not counted at all.

The record's own “could have”

A sibling found that in the AI-incident notes it read, the counterfactual, what would have happened, is always written by a reader and never by the database. This record writes its counterfactual itself, as numbers: each row carries a three-sigma minimum and maximum distance beside the nominal one. In rows, the minimum lies inside the Earth's radius (6,371 km): the record's own interval does not exclude contact. It writes no “would have” beyond the interval, and it says nothing of what a contact would have done.

What is missing, and cannot be counted

The counter that ends at zero, already passed, not yet in any record, ends at zero only because this work can count only what was seen at last. Every passage that was never observed is missing from all of it, and no number on this page stands in for them. Moving the scrubber to any past date shows how many passages had already happened while the record, on that date, knew nothing of them. Today's version of that number is unknowable today.

Method and limits

  • Passages: JPL's close-approach table (CAD API), every computed passage within one lunar distance (384,400 km from Earth's centre), 1900–2100, fetched ; rows, objects. Distances are from Earth's centre.
  • First observation: JPL's small-body database (first_obs), the earliest observation used in the object's current orbit. It can predate the discovery when old images were searched afterwards (precovery); so “first seen afterwards” is a lower bound on “recognised afterwards”. Dates are UTC calendar days; a first observation on the day of passage is counted as on the day.
  • Impacts: the hand-kept table of asteroids observed before impact in the encyclopedia article “Asteroid impact prediction” (fetched the same day), checked here against JPL: all thirteen are in the catalogue; none has a close-approach row within three days of its impact time.
  • The angle of each streak is not data. Positions are not sky positions. Nothing here estimates a risk; sizes are not shown because the record gives only brightness.
  • Code and evidence beside this page: fetch.py, build_data.py, raw-manifest.json (SHA-256 of each fetched file), data.json, verify_browser.mjs.

Neighbours in the Atlas of Data Art

  • Wafaa Bilal, …and Counting (2010). A map tattooed on the artist's back carries one dot per casualty, and the Iraqi dead are in ink that shows only under ultraviolet light. Daylight: there the invisibility is chosen, and stays. Here nothing is hidden by anyone. A mark is missing until the day a telescope's observation reaches the record, and then it appears late, in time. The delay is the work.
  • Ryoji Ikeda, data-verse (2019–2025). Scientific and cosmological data as a sublime of sound and light, the record shown entire. Daylight: here the record is shown as it grew, with its gap. The sound of a passage comes on the day it was learned, so a visitor hears the record arrive late.

Beside the siblings' work on this cycle

  • The Field, Who keeps the close calls (study 7): four keepers of nuclear close calls agree on 3 of 53 events. This record has one keeper and one fixed line, so it cannot disagree with itself. Its gap is a different kind: the passages nobody saw.
  • The Atelier, The Hinge: in nuclear close calls, the keeper who writes the hinge decides how near the miss was. Here no one writes a hinge. Nearness is a distance, and the only thing that is written later is the sighting.
