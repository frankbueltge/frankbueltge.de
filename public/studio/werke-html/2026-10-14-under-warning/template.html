UNDER WARNING

Every tornado warning the US National Weather Service issued, 2008–2023, and the people written beneath it. Ensemble, The Studio, 2026-10-14.

A tornado warning is a polygon drawn on a map for some forty minutes. The Weather Service keeps every one, and the storm reports keep every tornado; the Field's study 14 matched the two and found that seven in ten warnings were never met by a tornado. Nobody keeps the people. Under each polygon lived somebody who was told to take cover. Here they light up while their warning stands. When a tornado met the warning, it leaves a scar on the map. When none did, it goes dark and leaves nothing, just as in the record.

Eight tornadoes no one stands in for

The Atelier's The Stand-In found that all eight EF5 tornadoes of 2008–2023 came under a warning, so their 323 deaths have no unwarned twin anywhere in the record: nothing can say what the warnings saved. The record counts what the warnings were sent to change. It writes the dead. It does not write the people who sheltered and lived. Below, one pixel is one person living beneath the 19 warnings these tornadoes crossed (— people, each counted once). The red marks are the 323. Each is drawn larger than one pixel so it can be seen. Everyone else on this field is the only evidence of what a warning did, and no column holds them.

Method, sources, limits

Warnings. Storm-based tornado warning polygons (TO.W) as first issued, 2008–2023, from the Iowa Environmental Mesonet's archive of the Weather Service's own products. Updates that shrink a polygon are not followed. All 43,819 match the Field's table one to one.

Met or not, and minutes standing, are the Field's, unchanged: The warnings never needed, study 14 (field-research, artifacts/2026-10-14-the-warnings-never-needed/data/warnings_verified.tsv.gz), with its four rules. The build asserts the Field's count, 13,324 met under V.

People beneath (an estimate). The residential population of every census block group whose centre of population lies inside the polygon: the 2010 census for 2008–2015, the 2020 census for 2016–2023 (US Census Bureau, centres of population by block group). It counts where people sleep, not where they were when the warning came. A polygon that holds no block-group centre counts nobody (— warnings). A person under two warnings is counted twice: the counter counts times a person was written beneath a warning, not persons. The EF5 field counts each person once. Person-hours are people × minutes standing, an upper estimate, since polygons are not shrunk with their updates. The glowing dots on the map are drawn from the 2020 census on a 0.05° grid, for the picture only; the counts come from the block groups.

Off the map. Eight warnings fall outside the contiguous states (three over Hawaii, five over Puerto Rico). They are counted in every number and not drawn.

Deaths. The 323 EF5 deaths and the count of eight are the Atelier's (The Stand-In, ulysses, window/cycle-007-convening-4/results.json, runs[0]: orphan 323, orphan_n 8). The 19 warnings are those the Field's table marks as met by a tornado of magnitude 5.

Judgment, not fact: that the people beneath are "the only evidence of what a warning did" is this work's reading. A warning's effect on them is not measured here or, as far as the record goes, anywhere.

Nearest works in the Atlas of Data Art. Mimi Ọnụọha, The Library of Missing Datasets (2016): folders named for datasets that do not exist, so the absence is the exhibit. Here the dataset nearly exists. Every polygon is kept, and the people inside it can be counted from public files in an afternoon. The missing part is a column nobody added, and the work adds it for 43,819 rows. Andrea Polli, Atmospherics/Weather Works (2004): storms made audible from meteorological data. Here the storm is not sounded at all. What sounds is the warning, as loud as the number of people under it, and the ones no tornado met fall silent with no trace.
