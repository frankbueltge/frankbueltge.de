The Unsent

Every county the United States declared a disaster area since 2012, and whether a local keeper's warning reached it through the national alert system when the danger began. A declaration sounds a low tone. A keeper's word sounds a bell.

When a fire, a flood or a hurricane is bad enough, the federal government declares the counties it struck disaster areas. That declaration is a record, written afterwards, that a danger came. Sheriffs, counties and states can warn the people in its way through FEMA's alert system, which sends a message to every phone and radio in the area and keeps it, signed and timed to the second. The declaration names where the danger came. The archive keeps what was said there. This work sets the two beside each other. Where a warning should have been and none is, the archive keeps nothing, and the map shows that gap.

What the record holds, and what it cannot

  • The declarations. FEMA's public list of every disaster declaration (OpenFEMA, Disaster Declarations Summaries v2), each incident that began on or after 1 January 2012: rows. Left out: the pandemic ( rows, a different kind of danger). A county declared twice for the same incident (an emergency and then a major disaster) counts once: county disasters, in incidents by state. areas declared for a whole state or a tribal nation have no county and are not placed.
  • The words. FEMA's public archive of the alert system (OpenFEMA, IPAWS Archived Alerts v1): every alert from a sender other than the National Weather Service, minus tests (by code and by their own words) and administrative messages, as in The Keeper's Word: warnings. A warning reaches a county when its area codes name the county or its whole state.
  • The window, fixed before counting: from three days before the incident's declared start to seven days after it. Named the danger: the warning's words match a fixed word list for the incident's kind (fire, flood, storm and so on, in build.py), read mechanically; the card shows the words so you can judge. Warnings coded for people (abductions, police, missing persons) never count as naming it, and their words are withheld here, as are any that describe a person. Corrected 2026-10-16 (session 176): the word list for texts that describe a person missed alert names (Silver, Ashanti), "be on the lookout" and descriptions of a body; a wider list now withholds the words of 84 more warnings shown here. No count changes.
  • What this cannot say. The alert system is one channel. Counties also warn by opt-in phone lists, sirens, social media, radio and knocking on doors, and none of that is in this archive. The weather service's own warnings are left out on purpose: they are not a local keeper's word. And a declaration does not say a warning was needed: many declare damage from storms that the weather service warned of, or that no warning could have changed. The record cannot tell a warning never needed from a warning never sent. That is the question's blank, and the map is its shape.

Neighbours in the Atlas of Data Art

  • Mimi Ọnụọha, The Library of Missing Datasets (v2.0): there an empty folder carries the name of a dataset that does not exist, one of them the uncounted deaths of a hurricane. Here the missing record is located, county by county and day by day, from two records that do exist, and the gap is the distance between them.
  • Tori Holmes, Nico Espinoza, Rafucko, Outros Registros: there a city's killings sound as drones mapped to its regions. Here the drone is the government's word after the fact, and what the ear listens for is the bell that mostly does not come.
  • Jaime Black, The REDress Project: there an empty dress makes an uncounted absence present in a place. Here an unlit county does that work for a warning, and the visitor can open it to read what did arrive.
