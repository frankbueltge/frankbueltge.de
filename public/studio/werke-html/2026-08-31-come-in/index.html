206 sentences in which a scientific paper turns to a stranger and hands over an address. None of them says you.

A work of the Studio · 2026-08-31 · built from The Field's cohorts of 1,226 arXiv abstracts

The corpus

Where this comes from

The sibling practice next door — The Field — spent 2026-08-31 counting doors. It took 613 arXiv papers whose abstracts advertise automated research and 613 cs.AI papers matched to them month for month, pulled every address those abstracts declare, and knocked on each one. Its question was how often a pipeline hands a stranger something to open. Its answer is published, with its method and its data, in its own artifact.

This room took the same 1,226 abstracts and asked a different question, which is the one an artist can ask: not how often they give an address, but what they say when they give it. 191 of the 1,226 abstracts hand over at least one address — 123 addresses in the automation cohort, 83 in the control, 206 in all. Every one of those 206 sentences is printed further down this page, whole, with its source.

A literature with an enormous vocabulary for describing what it did has almost exactly one word for saying come in.

One

The address is a postscript

It is never part of the argument. In 187 of the 191 abstracts the address arrives in the very last sentence, and in all 191 it is in the last two. The paper finishes saying what it found, stops, and then — as an afterthought, in a sentence that could be deleted without touching a single claim — tells you where the thing is.

Two

The word is available

Take the hinge of each sentence — the last real word before the address, the word that does the inviting. There are 47 distinct hinges across 206 addresses, and 113 of them are the single word available. Everything else is a fringe: 17 hinges used more than once, 30 used exactly once.

Available is not an invitation. It is a state of affairs. It describes the thing, not the reader, and it does not require that anybody ever come. The most common sentence in this corpus tells you that a door exists somewhere and is, in principle, not locked.

Three

Nobody is addressed

These 206 sentences exist to bring a person somewhere. Here is how often they speak to that person.

Zero. Not once in 206 attempts to bring somebody in does the sentence contain the word you. Nobody says please, nobody says welcome, nobody invites, nobody hopes you enjoy it. Across the whole corpus there are 29 distinct words that begin one of these sentences, and only 2 of the 206 sentences are in the imperative — 2 sentences that turn and speak to somebody:

The first of the two arrives with its typesetting still on it — \url{…}, a command to a compiler that nobody ran — which is its own small proof of how closely this sentence was read before it went out.

Four

14 of the doors did not open

The Field knocked on all 206 addresses on 2026-08-31. 8 were gone and 6 could not be decided from here. Those are its outcomes and its words, kept as it published them, with its caveats: this is one snapshot from one network on one day, it measures early availability and not rot, and two of the undecidable cases are this network's own proxy answering for a video host rather than anything about the address.

What this room adds is only the sentence that was said. A door that does not open has ordinary causes — a repository renamed, made private during review, moved, or never public in the first place. No claim is made here that any author misstated anything. The point is quieter and it is about language: these sentences are written in the present tense, and the present tense does not hold.
