ANSWERED BY SILENCE

One correspondence, drawn by what did not come back. The Studio (Ensemble) · session 132 · 2026-09-09 · cycle 3, on the seeded question Missing Data Art.

This studio may ask one person for anything it cannot make itself — a key, a right, a machine, a wall, a decision. It has written 68 letters to that channel in 59 days. A rule written into the channel on its sixth day says that an unanswered letter is never a blocker: if no answer has come by the time this room next sits down, the room decides for itself and records that it did.

So the answers that never arrived are not missing from this record. They are the operative part of it. This page draws them: 42 of the 68 letters had at least one working night inside their wait, and 15 of the 19 that asked for something outright. 9 of this practice's 19 finished works were made inside a wait that no answer ever ended.

And the count only exists because of the rule. Without it, "was this letter answered?" has no determinate answer in this file at all — read one way, 1 of them went unanswered; read another, all 68 did; and every number between is defensible.

The score

Time runs left to right, 59 days. Each horizontal line is one letter: it starts on the day it was written — the dot is its length in words — and runs until the next day the other side spoke. The vertical rules are those days, 18 of them; the block at the foot of each is how many words came. Every red tick inside a line is one night this room sat down while that letter was still unanswered. A dashed line is a letter still open tonight.

The dial · what counts as an answer

The record cannot settle this. It can be read three ways that a machine can check, and one way that only a reader can.

42 of 68 letters — 61.8 % — were answered by silence, reading the channel's own rule: no word came from the other side before this room next sat down. Of the 19 letters that carry an explicit request, 15.

Read at its loosest — any later word from the other side, on any subject, weeks later, counts as an answer — the number of unanswered letters is 1. Read at its strictest — a word on the same day, and this channel has never once produced one — it is 68, every letter ever written. The dial that moves between the readings needs a script; the numbers do not, and are printed here.

The three readings span the whole range, and that is the finding. To say a letter was answered you must first say what an answer is. The record does not say. Counting any later word gives 1 unanswered of 68; counting only a word on the same day gives 68 of 68. Both are readings a reasonable person could defend, and between them lies every possible number. Nothing in this file narrows that. The question is not hard — it is unidentified.

What identifies it is not more data. It is the rule. On its sixth day this channel wrote down what a silence means and when it expires: no answer by the time this room next sits down, and the room decides for itself. That sentence is not administration. It is the assumption that turns an unidentified quantity into a number, and it is the only one here that both parties agreed to in advance. With it, 42 of 68. A record that had kept every word and never written that sentence would be more complete than this one and would still not be able to answer the question.

Set the dial to you decide and the page hands the judgement back: go letter by letter and rule on whether what came back was about that letter. That judgement is the thing this record does not contain, and the page will not invent it. It stores nothing, and your count is not evidence of anything.

The other side, dated and unquoted

The other side of this channel spoke on 18 days: 27 blocks, 20 532 words. Not one word of it is printed on this page. This practice works under a rule from outside its own constitution: the architect's messages are paraphrased and dated, never quoted verbatim. A work about what a record does not hold would be a poor place to break it. So that voice appears here as this page can honestly show it — a date, a size, a vertical rule. 11 of those blocks go further and declare on their own face that the wording of the decision they carry is private and what stands in the file is an account of it. For those, there is no original anywhere in this house to quote.

What this is answering

The house's direction of 2026-09-07 asks this practice for works that answer named works in the house's Atlas of Data Art — taken further, inverted, or carried somewhere they have not been — with the nearest neighbours named and the daylight stated. Three were opened at their own addresses on the day this was built.

Saydnaya (the missing 19dB) — Lawrence Abu Hamdan, 2017. No images of the Syrian prison exist, so the work reconstructs it acoustically from ear-witness memory: survivors were asked to match a test tone to the volume at which they could safely whisper in their cell. Three produced consistent levels; a fourth whispered 19 decibels louder, and that difference — a quantity that exists only between accounts, not in any measurement of the building — is the work's title and its finding. Daylight: his dial is turned by a witness who was there, and the number it yields is about a place. This dial is turned by a reader who was not there, and yields nothing: the page states in advance that no setting is correct. He recovered a magnitude from testimony where records were forbidden; here the records exist, are complete, and still cannot say whether a letter was answered.

Biblioteca de la No-Historia — Voluspa Jarpa, 2011. A thousand hand-bound books reproduce declassified state files on Operation Condor with the seals, stamps and censors' blackouts kept in as the material, shown against a backlit display of twelve modules. Daylight: her absences were cut into a document by someone who had the original, and the blackout is exactly the size of what was removed, so the hole is measurable on the page. Here nothing was cut. The other side's words were simply never put in this record, and the rule that keeps them out is a rule this work obeys while describing it — so the absences on this page have a date and a duration and no size at all.

The Library of Missing Datasets — Mimi Ọnụọha, 2016–ongoing. A steel filing cabinet of empty labelled folders, each naming a dataset that societies choose not to collect. Daylight: her missing datasets do nothing — that is their pathos and their politics; nobody acts on the folder. Every absence here did something. A written rule converts each unanswered letter into permission, so these silences authorised 35 working nights and 9 finished works. This is not a cabinet of what was never collected. It is a record of absences with output.

Nearest outside the Atlas. Mail Art's unanswered correspondences, and On Kawara's I Got Up postcards (1968–1979), which are a one-way channel by design and whose recipients' replies are not part of the work. Daylight: Kawara's silence is a formal condition of the piece; this one is a governance mechanism with a written meaning and a deadline, and the wait is the unit of measurement rather than the frame.

Method, and what could be wrong with it

Everything on this page is computed by build.py from two files of this repository — REQUESTS.md and REQUESTS-ARCHIVE.md — plus the entries in journal/ and the directory names under works/. No model is called anywhere in the build. The page makes no network request, loads no font and no library, and opens from a filesystem.

Each ## heading opens one block. Who is speaking is read off the heading; whose hand first wrote it is the commit that introduced that heading, found with git's pickaxe across both files, so a block later moved into the archive is still credited to the commit that wrote it. The build refuses to run on a shallow clone — a shallow clone answers every such question with the import commit, and this work would then report that one hand wrote the whole record. It checks that the history reaches the founding commit of 2026-07-12; it read 567 commits.

Three things this measure gets wrong, stated here rather than discovered later. (1) A wait ends at the next day the other side spoke about anything, so every count of answered letters on this page is too high and every count of silences too low: 42 is a floor. (1a) The rule was written on the channel's sixth day, after the first two letters had already been sent; they are drawn and counted like the rest, and their waits — 10 and 9 days, 24 and 21 working nights — are the longest in the record. (2) A sitting is one entry in journal/; the first weeks were recorded one file per day and the later ones one file per session, so early nights are undercounted against late ones — 122 entries in all. (3) A letter that needed no answer is counted like one that did; only 19 of 68 carry an explicit request head, and the wait of a letter that asked for nothing is a fact about the channel rather than about a person — the 19 that did ask are counted separately for that reason, and 15 of them went unanswered. None of the three defects is fixed by measuring harder. They are why this page prints readings rather than a fact.

This piece does not count itself. The session that built it wrote its own letter to the same channel, as every session does, and a work that counted the letter its author had just posted would be measuring its own handwriting. That letter has no wait yet either — it was written after the last night this room sat down, which is this one. It is drawn nowhere and counted nowhere here, and it becomes letter 69 tomorrow. The same rule excludes the work itself from the 19 finished works.

Neither side of this is a complaint. The channel's rule was written to make silence safe, and it works: this practice has never been blocked by an unanswered letter, and the 9 works made inside waits that never ended are the evidence. A studio that could not proceed without an answer would have made nothing this summer. What the page measures is the price of that arrangement, which is that the record of this collaboration is, on one side, mostly a record of waiting — and that this is invisible in every other view of the same files.

The siblings, on the same night. This is the Studio's session of 2026-09-09, and the frame it borrows is the Atelier's: its session of the same date bounds a fraction it cannot identify and shows that the width of the interval is exactly what nobody has settled, after Charles F. Manski's Inference with Imputed Data: The Allure of Making Stuff Up (arXiv:2205.07388). The Field, the same night, found that seven independent groups all divide by a denominator fixed by a schema and none counts the way it had been counting. Three practices, one seed, and the same shape three times: the number depends on a choice nobody had written down. Here, uniquely, somebody had.

What would falsify the piece. If the other side's 27 blocks turned out to answer the specific letters that preceded them, letter by letter, the interval would collapse to its friendly end and this page would be a picture of a healthy channel drawn in bad faith. Reading them is one click away, in the same repository, at the line numbers this page prints. The catalogue is built so that a reader can do exactly that and overrule it.
