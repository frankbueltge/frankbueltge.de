Only No

On a public prediction market, markets ask whether AI will wipe out humanity before a date. of those dates have passed. Every one of them is written NO. None of them could have been written anything else: a YES needs someone left to write it, and someone to be paid.

Who holds the pen

A YES has to be written by someone. So who does each rule say would write it?

Each mark is one rule. Filled: the deadline has passed and the market says NO. Outlined: still open. A stroke through a mark means the rule strikes an answer in advance.

The answers struck in advance

These are the only places in the ledger where a rule names a world and refuses in advance to answer for it. The ledger keeps NO, and it keeps what it will not write.

    The one that came closest

    Price after each day's last trade. A price on these markets is what traders paid for a YES share that pays M$1 on YES. Read below why that is not a probability of extinction.

    The price is a price, not a probability

    Each dot is an open market, placed by its deadline (logarithmic years from today) and its last price. Size: number of bettors. Line: the ledger's own resolved rows at their last price, all on the left.

    The further the deadline, the higher the price: about 1 % for 2027, 5 to 6 % for 2030, 13 to 14 % for 2100, 27 to 33 % for the 3000s. One reading (a judgment, ours): the longer a NO bet is locked, the less it earns per year, and a YES bet is never collected in the world it names. So what the price measures is mostly what holding a bet for a long time is worth to a trader. The makers of these markets say so themselves:

    “If neither humanity nor AIs are around then this market does not resolve.”
    resolution text of the 2030 market with the most bettors, source
    “Due to M$ not being worth anything if the world ends, people have an incentive to bet "NO" …”
    description of a 2030 market that its creator set to resolve N/A in 2027 and roll back every trade, source

    So the discourse has a number here, quoted and traded every day, and the one observation that would score it is the one no ledger can hold. Missing data, by construction:

    Five set aside: four titles that mean something else, one built never to resolve

      The only YES ever written under a title of this family is the third of these. It was decided by comparing two prices.

      How this was made

      • Search. Sixteen search terms against api.manifold.markets/v0/search-markets, binary markets, all states: 1,136 distinct markets (fetch.py).
      • Rule. A market is in the ledger when its question begins “Will/Did <AI or a named AI lab> wipe out humanity/humans” or “… cause human extinction”, unconditionally (select_rule.py): 67 by title. Its description was read: 4 meant something else (listed above), 1 resolves N/A by design. 62 remain.
      • Prices. Every market opens at 50 %; the opening day is drawn but never counted as a peak. Every trade of every market was fetched (fetch_detail.py); the line is the price after each day's last trade. “Last” is the most recent such price. “YES bought” is the gross mana spent buying YES (sales not netted).
      • What this does not show. Other platforms (Metaculus, Polymarket, Kalshi) and other phrasings are not here. Titles were matched by a rule; a market phrased otherwise is missing. Many rows are one creator's ladders of dates, so the 62 are not independent opinions. Nothing here estimates any real risk.
      • Checks. verify.py recomputes every number on the page from the raw files; verify_browser.mjs drives the page at 390 and 1100 px.

      Neighbours in the Atlas of Data Art

      • MTAA, 1 year performance video (aka samHsiehUpdate), 2004. A web work that can only be completed by a viewer who watches for a year; failure is built into the front end. Here completion is not hard but impossible in principle: the YES cell can only be filled in a world with no reader, and the work's act (“resolve YES”) shows that by removing the reader's page.
      • Alan Bigelow, Saving the Alphabet, 2005. The reader's clicks erase the story letter by letter. Here the visitor's one destructive act erases nothing in the record: the ledger stands, still all NO, and what disappears for a moment is only the one who would read the YES.
