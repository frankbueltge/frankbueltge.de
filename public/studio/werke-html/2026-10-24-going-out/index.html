GOING OUT

Since 2006, people have gone out at night, found one constellation, and matched it to one of eight charts. Each report is a sky. Each sky begins whole here and goes out, faintest star first, until only what that person saw is left. The rest stays as holes in the glow: the stars their report records by leaving them out.

What this is made of, and how it is drawn

The reports. Globe at Night (NOIRLab), one CSV per year, 2006 to 2025, under CC BY 4.0. naked-eye reports (type GAN). The observer finds a named constellation and picks the chart that matches what they see: chart 1 shows its stars down to magnitude 1, chart 7 down to magnitude 7, and 0 means the constellation itself could not be found. reports were filed with no chart at all; they stand in the band's bottom row and are never drawn as skies. The face plays a fixed sample of up to 320 reports per year ( in all, seed 20261024); the band counts every report. Places are rounded to 0.1° (about 11 km). The observers' own comments are not read into the work.

The hour, checked. A report names the constellation it looked for, so its time must put that constellation's brightest star above the observer's horizon. In the older files the filed UT very often does not: the local times are evening hours, but their conversion to UT has failed. Such a report is set by its local time as filed, shifted by its longitude (so its sky may stand an hour or two off), and its caption says so: reports were placed this way, and fit neither hour and are never drawn. more were filed at 0°N 0°E, a place in the Gulf of Guinea that stands in for no place given; they are counted and not drawn.

The sky. Every star of the Yale Bright Star Catalogue (5th revised ed., Hoffleit & Warren 1991, via CDS Strasbourg) brighter than magnitude 6.5, stars, set for the report's place and minute (sidereal time from the report's UT, J2000 positions, no precession, no refraction, no extinction near the horizon). The dome is drawn as seen lying on one's back with the head to the north: zenith at the centre, east on the left. A star is lit if it is no fainter than the chart the person matched; the others go out. Treating the chart number as a limiting magnitude for the whole sky is the work's simplification, not the observer's claim: they matched one constellation.

What the record says about itself. Kyba, Altıntaş, Walker and Newhouse used 51,351 of these reports (2011–2022) and found that the loss of visible stars implies a brightening of the sky of 7 to 10 % a year, faster than satellites recorded (Science 379, 265–268, 2023; GFZ summary). The satellite sensors are blind to much of the blue in white LED light; the stars that went missing were recorded by the people who could no longer see them. This work draws no trend: the band shows who reported, and the people who report change from year to year.

Nearest works in the Atlas of Data Art, and the daylight. Playmodes, ASTRES: Mapping the Firmament (2025), draws the night sky in laser light to celebrate it; here the sky is drawn to subtract it, one person's report at a time. Bathsheba Grossman's crystal engravings (2002) fix astronomical data whole in glass; here the whole sky is shown only so that it can be taken away, and returns only where the visitor holds their eye. Mimi Ọnụọha, The Library of Missing Datasets (2016), files the data that does not exist as empty folders; here the data exists, written by hundreds of thousands of hands, and what it records is a negative: the stars not seen.
