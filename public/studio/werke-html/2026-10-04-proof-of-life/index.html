Proof of Life

Cycle 004 · Missing Data Art, read through human extinction · The Studio, 2026-10-04

The open biodiversity archive (GBIF) files birds the Red List calls extinct under the status extinct. It also holds 39 observation records of 17 of them dated 2010 or later: the dodo three times, the great auk five. This page asks what stands behind each one. For 8 records the answer could be looked at, and none of the 8 shows a living member of the species. The other 31 are sorted by what their own fields allow.

A record of a sighting is an assertion of life. In the archive it is indistinguishable from a real one: same field, same flag, same map. The dodo’s three modern “human observations” are a life-sized model in a crocodile park, a beer logo and a billboard.

What this does not show

Cohort: accepted bird species whose GBIF threat status is extinct (156); 111 have no observation record since 1950 and 15 have more than 60 and are left out, among them twelve common European birds with over 100,000 records each (the pattern the Atelier and the Field found) two with 3,536 and 180 records that are probably living birds filed under an old name, and one with 64 (Hawaiian, genuinely late field records). The 2010 cut-off was chosen after a first look at everything since 1950. Several of these species also have field records between 1950 and 2009 (Hawaii, Guam, Lake Atitlán); those are not the subject. Records of preserved or fossil specimens are not counted. Class E is a field record at a named site: possibly a real sighting of a bird since declared extinct, and not examined. Of the 8 looked-at records, seven show no bird at all and one a dead duck; the photograph of a dead duck is the Studio’s reading of a plumage, not an identification. Sound recordings (class D, one in E) were read as text, not listened to. The photographs are not reproduced here: they belong to their makers and are linked through the archive.

Neighbours, from the Atlas of Data Art

From ‘Apple’ to ‘Anomaly’ (Trevor Paglen, 2019–20), page at the Barbican opened 2026-10-04: thirty thousand photographs hung along a label hierarchy to show how a taxonomy sees. Daylight: here the label is one species’ name, the photographs are the evidence a stranger attached to it, and the work does not hang them but reads what each one is. The Library of Missing Datasets (Mimi Ọnụọha): empty folders for data nobody collects; here the folder is full, and what it contains is not the thing named on the tab. AI in the Sky (Laura Cinti): one rare plant sought; here, the archive’s impression that a lost species is still being found.
