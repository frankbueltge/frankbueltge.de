The standard map of nearby galaxies excludes a band near our own galaxy's plane, where dust hides what lies behind it, a boundary visible only in its own entries. A radio survey that reads through dust found 960 more galaxies there; just five also appear in the optical map.

Wall label and links added by the site. The work itself begins below.

IN THE WAY

The standard map of the galaxies around ours, drawn as it stands — and the 9.58 % of the sky it was built never to enter.

Every mark on this sheet is one galaxy that someone measured. Nothing is drawn where the catalogue has nothing. The blank bar across the middle is not a decision of this page: it is 3 953 square degrees of sky that the record excludes by its own rule, written before the first entry was made, because our own galaxy stands between the telescope and everything behind it. The obstruction is the room we are standing in.

The sheet · the same marks, twice

The catalogue is the 2MASS Redshift Survey: 44 599 galaxies selected in the near infrared, the standard census of what lies within about three hundred megaparsecs. Beside it, in red, a second record of the same sky made with a radio telescope: 960 galaxies found by the 21-centimetre line of their hydrogen, which dust does not stop. Choose the frame the marks are drawn in.

Hammer’s equal-area projection, longitude running left, the whole sphere on one sheet; equal areas of sky are equal areas of paper, so a thin patch of marks is a thin patch of sky. The dashed line is the catalogue’s own boundary — five degrees from the galactic plane, eight toward the galactic centre. 44 599 galaxies fall on 39 903 distinct points of this paper; where the sky is thickest, 12 of them share one point of ink.

In one frame the absence is a crooked band nobody would name. In the other it is a bar straight through the middle. The hole has a shape only in the coordinate system of the thing that makes it.

Both drawings carry exactly the same marks. Nothing was added to the galactic view and nothing removed from the equatorial one; only the frame turns. The equatorial frame is the one an observatory books time in, and in it the missing ground is a diagonal smear with no name, crossing constellations that have nothing to do with one another. Turn the sheet into the frame of our own galaxy and the same blank becomes a single straight statement about where we are standing.

One · the record draws its own boundary

Nobody needs to read the survey’s paper to find its edge. Sort the 44 599 entries by distance from the galactic plane and the nearest one stands at 5.001°. Split the sky into thirty-six sectors of longitude and the edge is 5.00° in thirty of them and 8.02° in the six that face the centre of our galaxy. The rule the survey states — five degrees, eight toward the bulge — is legible in the entries alone, and the entries agree with it to a thousandth of a degree.

A boundary is a fact about a record, not about the sky. This one is visible without any document: it is the only place in the catalogue where a straight line in the data has no astronomical meaning at all.

Two · what the line costs

The excluded ground is 3 953 square degrees, 9.58 % of the whole sky. The catalogue holds 0 entries inside it. Cut the sphere into 7 200 cells of equal area — 5.73 square degrees each — and 808 of them hold nothing at all: 617 inside the excluded ground and 191 outside it, on ground the catalogue does cover.

Above fifteen degrees the record holds 1.213 galaxies per square degree, steadily, all the way to the poles. At that density the closed ground would carry about 4 795 entries more. That figure is an estimate and is marked as one: it assumes the hidden sky is as full as the sky we can see, and the universe is not smooth — the two structures at the end of this page are the proof that it is not.

Three · the blindness does not stop at the line

Between the boundary and fifteen degrees the catalogue holds 7 512 entries over 6 724 square degrees. At the plateau density that ground would carry 8 156 — 644 more, 7.9 % above what is there. In the lowest band the record holds at all, 4.6° to 5.7°, the density is 0.497; one band up it is 0.893; the plateau is not reached until about eleven degrees. The survey drew a line and stated it. The damage the line was drawn against runs on past it, and nothing in the record announces where it ends.

Surface density of entries against distance from the galactic plane, in fifty steps of equal area. The shaded ground at the left is where no entry may stand. The four bars climbing out of it are the record recovering — not the sky changing.

Four · the record carries the measure of its own blindness

Each entry carries a column the survey did not have to publish: E(B−V), the reddening of the light by dust in front of that galaxy, taken from the Schlegel–Finkbeiner–Davis map of 1998. Its median is 0.019 above seventy-five degrees and 0.485 in the last degrees before the boundary — 26 times as much. The record states, entry by entry, how much light was taken from it, and the direction in which that number would be largest is the direction it holds no entries in. The catalogue knows what it cannot see.

It also stops. The largest value anywhere in 44 599 entries is 0.999; ninety-nine per cent stand below 0.776. In the radio survey the median is 0.840 and the largest is 22.42. 378 of its 883 entries stand beyond the highest value the optical record contains; 271 of the optical catalogue’s 44 599 reach the radio survey’s middle. The two records share one column and barely overlap in it. We assert no cause for where the optical column stops: the catalogue states a limit in brightness and in latitude, not in dust.

Back to back, the same axis: above the line the optical catalogue, below it the radio survey. Each bar is a tenth of a magnitude of reddening. Both are drawn to their own maximum, because one is fifty times the size of the other.

Five · heard, not seen

Hydrogen does not care about dust. A radio telescope listening at 21 centimetres hears galaxies straight through the ground the optical catalogue is forbidden: the HIZOA surveys, 883 galaxies in the south and 77 in the north, 905 of them inside the excluded ground, not one of them further than 5.77° from the plane. They are drawn in red on the sheet above.

The red marks stop where the listening stopped, not where the galaxies do. The two surveys cover the longitudes 212° to 36° and two strips from 36° to 52° and 196° to 212°, all within five degrees of the plane: about 2 157 square degrees, 55 % of the closed ground. The rest of it has not been heard at this depth by these instruments at all.

Two records of the same sky, 45 559 objects between them. Ask how many they have in common and the answer is 5.

Every radio galaxy was matched against every catalogue entry within 2 arcminutes: 5 of 960 have one. That number is about two records, not about human knowledge, and the difference matters. The southern survey’s own paper reports that 51 per cent of its detections already had an optical or near-infrared counterpart in the literature and a further 27 per cent were found in images afterwards — but only 8 per cent of those counterparts had a previously measured optical redshift. The northern survey is sharper still: of its 77 galaxies, 27 have a counterpart already catalogued in 2MASS — the very extended-source catalogue this redshift survey selects its entries from. The parent list has them. The map built from it may not, because of a rule about latitude written before either was compiled. The galaxies are not unknown. They are unenterable.

Six · and the record’s own holes crowd toward its edge

1 066 of the 44 599 entries carry no velocity: a position, a brightness, a dust value, and an empty field where the redshift belongs. They are not spread evenly. In the ten degrees nearest the boundary 17.81 % of entries have none; between seventy and ninety degrees, not one is missing. The gradient falls through every band without a single reversal.

The same obstruction, one level in: near the plane a galaxy is harder to see well enough to take its spectrum, so the blank inside the record follows the blank outside it.

Seven · what is known to be in there

The closed ground is not empty and nobody thinks it is. Two things found behind it are named here because both were found by other means — radio, X-ray, and the pull they exert on us.

Toward Norma the catalogue holds 3.4 times what an average patch of sky would give it — the cluster is bright enough to show through the thinning — while a quarter of that circle is ground it may not enter. Toward Vela it holds 137 against 380 expected, and the radio survey holds 102 galaxies in the same circle — three quarters of what the optical catalogue has there, from an instrument with a thousandth of its reach. The Vela supercluster is a structure of the nearby universe found late and mapped with near-infrared photometry and redshifts gathered on purpose behind the plane; the paper this page takes its direction from reports that of the galaxies it identified in six of its cluster candidates, only about fifteen per cent were previously known.

What this page does not claim

  • The 4 795 is an estimate under an assumption that is false in detail: galaxies are clustered, and the hidden ground contains at least one supercluster. It is marked as an estimate everywhere it appears and no conclusion here rests on it.
  • The 5 shared objects measure the overlap of two selections — near-infrared brightness on one side, neutral-hydrogen mass on the other. They do not measure what has been seen. The counterpart figures from the radio survey’s own paper are printed above for exactly that reason.
  • The dust column is a model, not a measurement at each galaxy: a map of the sky’s reddening read at that position. Its authors state its own uncertainties near the plane, which is where this page uses it most.
  • No cause is asserted for the highest dust value in the optical catalogue, for the 191 empty cells outside the closed ground, or for the shortfall in the belt beyond the line. The gradient in the record’s own dust column is stated beside them; the record does not say more than that.
  • The marks are positions rounded to this paper’s grid — one unit of a sheet 1 600 across. Nothing else about a galaxy is drawn. The catalogues themselves are not republished here.

How this was made

Three published catalogues were read once, on 2026-09-19, through the VizieR table access service at the Centre de Données astronomiques de Strasbourg, with an instrument that names itself in its request. Their descriptions (the ReadMe files) were read from the same archive. The files live in a cache outside this repository and are not committed; sources.json carries the query, the byte count and the SHA-256 of each one, so a reader can fetch them again and check they got what we did. Every number on this page is computed by measure.py from those files and written to counts.json; the page is built from that file alone by build.py, which makes the same bytes every time it runs.

Nearest works, and the daylight

Four works in the house’s atlas of data art were opened at their own addresses before this one was built, not cited from a sentence about them.

ASTRES: Mapping the Firmament, Playmodes Studio, 2025 — seen on its S+T+ARTS prize page: photographs of a laser installation, a credit list, and an account of generative constellations built with clustering and graph theory, each star given a sound, premised on light pollution having cut us off from the night sky. Daylight: ASTRES gives back a firmament our own lamps hide, and draws it with light. This sheet adds nothing to the sky: it prints a catalogue’s entries and leaves blank the ground no lamp caused and no switch can clear. Their material is the positions of stars; ours is the boundary of a record.

Saydnaya (the missing 19dB), Lawrence Abu Hamdan, 2017 — read in James Parker’s essay on the work in Index Journal (2020), which sets out how a prison no photograph exists of was reconstructed from what survivors heard, and reads the work against Cage. Daylight: his second channel is human memory and the blinding was a state’s decision; ours is a radio telescope and the obstruction is the galaxy we live inside — chosen by nobody, reformable by nobody. And where his work reconstructs what was hidden, this one refuses to: it prints both records as they stand and counts what they share.

The Great Bare Mat and Constellation, Raqs Media Collective, 2012 — seen on the Isabella Stewart Gardner Museum’s page for the collective: a carpet whose motif lays the constellation Ursa Major over a drawing of one hour of the collective’s own network traffic, later used as the floor for public debates. Daylight: Raqs uses a constellation as the ground on which data is laid. Here there is no ground under the data: the catalogue is the constellation, and the figure it draws is a hole.

data-verse, Ryoji Ikeda, 2019–2025 — read in the High Museum’s press release for the trilogy’s United States debut: open datasets from NASA, CERN and the Human Genome Project transformed by the artist’s own programs into monumental floor-to-ceiling projection and sound. Daylight: the same raw material — public scientific catalogues — and the opposite treatment. No sublime, no immersion, one sheet of paper at the size of a page, and the part of the data that does not exist is the subject rather than the part that does.

Named and deliberately not answered: Mimi Ọnụọha’s The Library of Missing Datasets, named here for the fifth time and again not answered. Her empty folders name absences that a society chose and could choose otherwise. The absence on this sheet was chosen by nobody, and no institution can decide to collect it. That is the whole distance between the two, and it is why the answer this page gives is a drawing rather than a cabinet.
