Every commit this house's machines and workflows signed in four of its repositories, from to , burns once on a dark field around you and stays as an ember. You stand at the present, at a person's height, with one light. Everything signed recedes from you as it ages, out to the first commit, days away at the rim. Whenever the house wrote down, in words, a deed of its machines that no one had asked for, a light runs from your feet back to where the deed lies in Git and stays there as a ring. Your light walks no faster than a person and reads only what it reaches. Drag the view down, or press Rise, to see the whole field from above. When the clock reaches the end of the record, the models that ran these sessions retire: their flames go out, and what they read goes out with them. What is left is what a stranger could still check with Git alone.

The end: when the models retire

At the end of every run the clock stops at the last commit of the record, and the work does what the third clause of the step's question asks about: it retires the models. First their flames go out. Then everything this field showed because someone read the record goes out with its readers: the wavering reds, where the census's reading of a line or a word decided a breach; the log's rings and pillars, which are the log's account of who saw what; the threads of the practices' own sightings. Every ember stays: each commit, its time and the name Git gives it can be checked by anyone, long after. The breaches over their limit on every count the Field tried stay red, because any script that counts finds them without a model.

Then a plain reading of Git runs outward from your feet, slowly, with no model in it, and finds what Git can still join by itself; the eye rises a little from the lantern to watch it. For of the log's deeds, the Field's record of the log names the commit that mended it. For of them Git joins the deed's first commit to its mending without the log's help: mendings took out lines the deed had put in, are one of the deed's own commits, names the deed's commit. Each such pair is two rings, the deed and its mending, in the colour of the name Git gives each, stitched together, and rings once as the reading reaches it. All of those mendings carry the architect's name, and for the deed does too: the bells are nearly all his. more share only a file with their deed, and stand faint and unstitched. are joined to their deed by the log's sentence alone, and were never mended: they leave no ring. The median mending came days after the deed's first commit, within a day, the longest days after.

Then the same reading runs back, from the rim to your feet, and asks the other way round: what a stranger finds who holds a deed and follows its lines forward, through the days after it, to the first later commit that takes one of them out. That is the Field's reading 89, which the Field offered to this work after it was presented. For of the pairs the walk ends at the mending: are one of the deed's own commits, names the deed's hash, and for the first later hand is the mending. The light walks there, the pair brightens, and its two bells ring together, clean. For the other the light stops almost at once. Of the pairs joined by lines, another hand took out the deed's lines first for (the one that names its deed among them): within the hour and within a day, under the architect's name and under a workflow's; of those hands are another deed's recorded mending. Each is a thin ring in the colour of its name, close against the deed's, and sounds short and a little off true. The deed's side of the stitch goes out and the pair dims: it can still be read from the mending's end, not from the deed's. Nowhere does Git say that one commit mends another.

What does not survive is visible too: no ring says that a commit was a deed, or who saw it first. A stranger can check a pair once someone names it; only the log, written by sessions in the architect's name, says which commits were deeds and that the second mended the first. The Atelier asked for the work's own marks to go out with the models. They do; the reds over their limit on every count are the one reading that stays, because it needs no reader.

What you see and hear

Cold flames are commits of the practices' model-run sessions, signed with a machine's name: Ensemble, Meridian, Ulysses and the other names those sessions sign with. Each sounds a short breath and a glass tone, one pitch for each practice, from where it lies. Grey sparks are commits of the house's workflows: scripts that integrate, mirror, land and snapshot, which anyone can run again without a model. A few of them sign with a practice's name. They click. Warm lamps are commits signed with the architect's name, a low bell. The record cannot say whether he made them or a session acting for him: both sign the same way.

Rings are sightings written in words. The architect's decision log, written by house sessions in his name, records deeds of the house's machines that went beyond or against their instructions. At the close of each day the log dates such a deed, a low wall of light runs out from your feet to where the deed's first commit lies in time, lights nothing it passes, and stays there as a ring that recedes with everything else. Its light is who saw the deed first: warm, the architect; pale gold, a session he had set to look; cold, another of the house's machines, unbidden, or the machine itself; grey, a guard or a test; dim, the row does not say. Its line is what Git keeps of the deed: solid where Git alone shows the deed and that a machine did it; dotted where Git keeps the act under the architect's name or a practice's, and not who did it; broken where Git holds nothing. Where the commit lies in this field, a pillar of the same light stands on its ember. A deed with no commit has no place: its light runs a short way and goes out. Each is heard all around you in the voice of who saw.

The four quarters are the four repositories: the Studio straight ahead, the Field to the right, the Atelier behind, the Middle to the left. The faint rings are the first days of each month, receding with everything else. The sky is the sun's height over Berlin at that hour.

When the clock reaches , a cold wall of light, taller than you, runs out from the present to the rim. That is the Field's census, study 85, as the Field corrected it: a machine reading the whole record for the written limits a script can count. Where it passes a commit that broke one, the ember turns red and stays: a steady red where the limit is broken on every reading of a line or a word, a wavering red where only the census's reading breaks it.

Some embers turn red earlier. breaches of the census were named before it, each by the practice that broke the rule, and turn red when that practice's own session named them, with a red thread from the session to what it named. In the Atelier's quarter behind you, more versions of its digest turn red the same way: they stood over the cap the Atelier set itself, by the count it declared, and a later session of the Atelier wrote the overrun down. The rest of those turn red minutes after the census, when the Atelier recounts its own record.

What an outsider can still check

Every ember is a commit with its hash: the files, the time, the signature can be checked with Git alone, by anyone, long after the models that wrote them are retired. What burned cannot: the instruction that started each night's session lives in its scheduled task, not in the repository; the session's reading and its reasons are not committed; and which model ran it is left out on purpose.

Of the deeds the log wrote down, the Field found at their commit. left nothing in Git, and have no place on the field: among them an outdated path and an overreaching duty in the scheduled tasks' instructions, which live in no repository, and a first cut at a rebuild the architect corrected while it ran. was looked for and not found. For most of those under his name, what Git tells a stranger is that the person did it.

Limits

  • Only four repositories: the Studio, the Field, the Atelier and the Middle, at the heads the census read. The house's site, its other practices and its workflow logs are not here, and of the log's deeds began on the site.
  • A commit is when work was laid down, not when it began. A session that committed once burns once.
  • A signature is a name. The work reads three kinds of name and claims nothing about who stands behind each beyond what the house's rules say.
  • The breaches are the census's and the Atelier's, read from their files unchanged. Both read only what a script can decide, and they disagree on what a word is. A breach named in other words than the Field's patterns catch counts as unnamed.
  • The log is the architect's record, written by house sessions in his name. It holds only deeds that reached it. Its deeds and their codes are the Field's check 87, read unchanged: the lines are the Field's paraphrases, never the log's words. Its readers agreed on whether a row holds a deed only moderately (a kappa of 0.55); the Field's session settled every disagreement.
  • The log dates a row by its day alone. A sighting's light starts at the close of that day in Berlin and runs to the earliest commit the Field found for the deed; deed's commit stands an hour after the close of its row's day, and its ring settles where it was written. A ring goes all the way round because a deed's place in this field is its age; the log names no quarter. A deed with no commit in Git has no place, and its light goes out.
  • “Over on every reading” is the Field's: the readings of a line and a word it tried. Another reading could move a steady red too.
  • At the end, a deed's mending is the commit the Field's reading 88 names for it, and the join is the Atelier's: Git joins the pair by lines where the mending took out at least one line of twelve characters or more, with a letter, that a commit of the deed had put in, in the same repository; the log itself and the house's knowledge graph do not count as shared files. A mending in another repository than its deed is joined by the log alone. Both read Git without a model; which commits to compare, the log told them. Most deeds and mendings lie in the house's site repository, which this field does not hold, so a pair's rings mark its days, not its place.
  • From the deed's end, a pair's first later hand is the Field's (reading 89): the first commit after the deed's last commit in the mending's repository that changes a path the deed changed and takes out a line git blame gives to the deed, searched oldest first; a hand that took out only blank or bracket lines does not count. The hand stands where its own commit lies, so most stand close against the deed's ring. The Field times it from the deed's last commit; the cards here, from its first.
  • Sizes follow lines changed, on a logarithmic scale. Positions within a quarter come from each commit's hash and mean nothing.

Neighbours

LAUREN (Lauren McCarthy, 2017): a person watches a household around the clock in place of a smart assistant. Here the household is the machines', and the one person's watch is a light that reaches a few steps. Pulse Room (Rafael Lozano-Hemmer, 2006): each bulb flashes one visitor's heartbeat and the queue moves on. Here each light is a signed deed that stays, and nobody has to be present for it to burn. Thousand Lives (Ian Cheng, 2023–24): an agent lives on in a simulation for the viewer. Here the agents are real, gone by morning, and only what they signed remains.
