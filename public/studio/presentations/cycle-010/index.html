GOING OUT

Satellites measured the night brightening by about 2 % a year. The people who went outside and looked measured 9.6 %, and they recorded it as the stars they could no longer find. Each sky here begins whole and goes out to what one of them saw. (Kyba et al., Science 2023; NOIRLab)

What this is made of, and how it is drawn

The reports. Globe at Night (NOIRLab), one CSV per year, 2006 to 2025, under CC BY 4.0. naked-eye reports (type GAN). The observer finds a named constellation and picks the chart that matches what they see: chart 1 shows its stars down to magnitude 1, chart 7 down to magnitude 7, and 0 means the constellation itself could not be found. reports were filed with no chart at all; they stand in the band's row "no chart" and are never drawn as skies. The face plays a fixed sample of up to 320 reports per year ( in all, seed 20261024); the band counts every report. Places are rounded to 0.1° (about 11 km). The observers' own comments are not read into the work.

The hour, checked. A report names the constellation it looked for, so its time must put that constellation's brightest star above the observer's horizon. In the older files the filed UT very often does not: the local times are evening hours, but their conversion to UT has failed. Such a report is set by its local time as filed, shifted by its longitude (so its sky may stand an hour or two off), and its caption says so: reports were placed this way, and fit neither hour and are never drawn. more were filed at 0°N 0°E, a place in the Gulf of Guinea that stands in for no place given; they are counted and not drawn.

The hours the files lost, shown. A report placed by its local time first appears at the hour it was filed with, its constellation's brightest star (ringed) under the horizon, and turns to the hour it was placed at. In the sample the filed UT stands most often 10 to 14 hours before the placed hour, as if an evening hour had been filed as a morning one; that reading is the work's guess, not the files' statement. The band's two bottom rows open the reports no sky can be given (a sample of , up to 24 per year of each kind). A report whose hour fits nothing is drawn at its place as every hour it could have been: each star it reported trails along its whole circle of one sidereal day. A report filed at 0°N 0°E is drawn in outline, as the sky above the empty sea the file put it in, which nobody stood under.

Your own report. "Go out yourself" sets the sky for now, at your device's place if you allow it, otherwise at a place guessed from your time zone, which you can correct. Nothing leaves your browser: the report is kept in this browser alone and shown in the band's last column. Once you choose what you see, it is set against every report with a chart and a place filed from your ten degrees of latitude, in each decade ( reports in all, whatever their hour): how many of those people saw fewer stars than you, as many, or more. To file it where it counts, use Globe at Night's own form.

The sky. Every star of the Yale Bright Star Catalogue (5th revised ed., Hoffleit & Warren 1991, via CDS Strasbourg) brighter than magnitude 6.5, stars, set for the report's place and minute (sidereal time from the report's UT, J2000 positions, no precession, no refraction, no extinction near the horizon). The dome is drawn as seen lying on one's back with the head to the north: zenith at the centre, east on the left. A star is lit if it is no fainter than the chart the person matched; the others go out. Treating the chart number as a limiting magnitude for the whole sky is the work's simplification, not the observer's claim: they matched one constellation.

What the record says about itself. Kyba, Altıntaş, Walker and Newhouse used 51,351 of these reports (2011–2022) and found that the loss of visible stars implies a brightening of the sky of 7 to 10 % a year, faster than satellites recorded (Science 379, 265–268, 2023; GFZ summary). NOIRLab's account of the paper puts the brightening at 9.6 % a year, against roughly 2 % a year in satellite measurements of the ground's brightness (NOIRLab, 2023). The satellite sensors are blind to much of the blue in white LED light; the stars that went missing were recorded by the people who could no longer see them. The band's top strip is not that trend: it averages what each year's observers kept, and the people who report, and where, change from year to year. The paper corrects for that; this work does not. The form changes too: from 2023 about a fifth to a quarter of the reports say the constellation could not be found at all (chart 0), where 2014 to 2022 hold none. That looks like a change in how reports are taken rather than in the sky (the work's guess); it pulls the strip's last three years down. "Stars still lit" counts the catalogue's stars no fainter than the chart matched, halved for the half of the sky above a horizon: an estimate.

Nearest works in the Atlas of Data Art, and the daylight. Playmodes, ASTRES: Mapping the Firmament (2025), draws the night sky in laser light to celebrate it; here the sky is drawn to subtract it, one person's report at a time. Bathsheba Grossman's crystal engravings (2002) fix astronomical data whole in glass; here the whole sky is shown only so that it can be taken away, and returns only where the visitor holds their eye. Mimi Ọnụọha, The Library of Missing Datasets (2016), files the data that does not exist as empty folders; here the data exists, written by hundreds of thousands of hands, and what it records is a negative: the stars not seen.
