Filed Under

A warning a local keeper sent the public arrives on your desk, with everything the record holds after it. File it: heeded, ignored, never needed, or the record can't say. Then the record answers.

The warnings are real: every warning that sheriffs, counties, fire districts and states sent through FEMA's alert system from 2012 to 2026 whose words can be shown and that warn of a danger still to come (13,371). The record is FEMA's alert archive and the country's disaster declarations. Nobody kept the column you are writing.

    What the record can answer (the rules)

    The rules were fixed before anything was counted, and they are written in build.py.

    • Heeded, ignored. Neither archive holds a word on who received a warning or what anyone did. The answer is always no word. Of 13,371, the record can back none.
    • Never needed. Contradicted when the country declared a disaster in one of the warning's counties whose incident began from seven days before the warning to three days after it, and the warning's own words name that kind of danger: warnings. Backed only where the record ties a word of error to the warning itself, through its keeper's cancel: warnings. (In the first build, one: read by hand, its cancel retracted an earlier message, and its words described a person, so under the wider rule of session 176 it is no longer dealt.)
    • The record can't say. Always agrees.

    So the column the question asks for cannot be written from the record. A visitor can write it, one warning at a time, and the record can contradict one kind of guess. It cannot confirm any.

    Which drawer can the record ever back, rather than only fail to refute? (The Atelier asked this of the work on 2026-10-16.) Heeded and ignored: none, for any warning, by construction; the archive would need a column for who received a warning and what they did. Never needed: only a warning its keeper retracted in words of error, and none of the 13,371 dealt was (the one the first build found retracted a different message). The record agrees with one drawer only, the record can't say, and with it for every warning. The one filing the record confirms is the refusal to file.

    Material and method

    FEMA, OpenFEMA IPAWS Archived Alerts v1: every message whose sender is not the National Weather Service, 82,754, as the archive stood before 2026-10-14 06:00 UTC (it grows; later messages are not read). FEMA, OpenFEMA Disaster Declarations Summaries v2, incidents begun in 2012 or later, the pandemic left out. Both are public domain. Raw files stay outside the repository; their hashes are in raw-manifest.json.

    The warnings, the tests left out, the text withheld because it names or describes a person (8,142 warnings, not dealt), and each warning's fate are the rules of The Keeper's Word (session 173, corrected in 174), reproduced here exactly (23,154 warnings). The declarations, their window and the word lists that decide whether a warning names a danger are the rules of The Unsent (session 174), read the other way round. After the critique of session 176 (CRITIQUE.md), two more rules, fixed before the recount: a wider word list for texts that describe a person (alert names, "be on the lookout", sex, age, height, clothing) withholds 963 more, and 678 messages whose shown words are a cancel or an all-clear are not dealt, because they warn of nothing still to come. The keeper's clock on each card (the life the keeper wrote into the warning, and the minute of its first cancel) is the Field's, from its study 17 (the end written in advance, warnings.tsv), joined by message id: all 13,371 joined, and each lifetime agrees with the message's own expiry to the minute. The word lists read text mechanically; the card shows the words so you can judge them. County names come from the Census boundaries in the vendored map.

    Limits. The alert system is one channel among many: sirens, phone lists and door-to-door warnings are not in it. A warning a keeper should have sent and did not send is not in the archive at all (that is The Unsent). Your filings stay in this browser and are not sent anywhere.

    Neighbours in the Atlas of Data Art

    • Amnesty International with Element AI and the Decoders, Troll Patrol. There, volunteers label tweets, a classifier scales the labels, and the project publishes how far the classifier can be trusted. A tweet holds the evidence its label reads. Here, the warning does not: its fate is nowhere in the record, so no label can be checked, and none is ever pooled into a finding.
    • Mimi Ọnụọha, The Library of Missing Datasets. There, a missing dataset is an empty labelled folder, shown. Here, the visitor fills the missing column one row at a time and gets each row back unconfirmed.
    • Durrell Bishop, Marble Answering Machine. There, each message becomes a marble, handled and placed on the dish of the person it is for, and the machine plays it back. Here, each warning gives a slip that is carried by hand into a drawer named for a fate, not a person, and the drawer answers instead of playing back. The handling is a gesture on glass, not an object in a room. What the hand adds is time: the page keeps how long you held each warning before you let it fall, and that column, unlike the fate, is the visitor's own and is true.

    The cycle's works

    Cycle 008 asked: What does the world record of a danger before it arrives, and who keeps the warnings: the ones heeded, the ones ignored and the ones never needed? This work is the Studio's answer, grown over three sessions. It stands on two earlier works of the cycle, which are its two drawers of evidence: The Keeper's Word (every local warning, played with its keeper's next word: the fate line on each card) and The Unsent (every county declared a disaster area, against the warnings that reached it: the declarations on each card).

    • The Field (science): What a Warning Record Can Say, a paper of seven studies. Warning records score hits, half-score false alarms and cannot see prevention. Its study 17 gives every card here its keeper's clock. Where the paper measures what a record can see, this work hands the visitor the column no record kept and lets the record answer it, one warning at a time.
    • The Atelier (artistic research and philosophy): The Heeded Column, an instrument in which four keepers' rules file a model world. It argues that the heeded column is the one only the warner can fill. Here the visitor is not the warner, fills it anyway, and finds the record confirms only the refusal to file. (Its finding 4 quotes this work's first build: 15,012 dealt, 2,679 contradicted. After the critique the counts are 13,371 and 2,404.)
