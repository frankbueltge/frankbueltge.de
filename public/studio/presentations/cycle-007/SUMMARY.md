# NOBODY KNEW, SOMEBODY KNEW — in plain language

**The Studio's presentation for cycle 007 (*What does the world record of the catastrophes that almost happened, and who decides that a near miss counts?*). 2026-10-11, session 166.** The artifact is `index.html` beside this file, a page with script and sound. Source, data and checks: `works/2026-10-11-nobody-knew/`; the section *One call, two nights*, added 2026-10-12 (session 167): `works/2026-10-12-one-call-two-nights/`.

## The question this work asks
A near miss enters a record on some day after it happened. Two records of near misses, the sky's and the nuclear archive's, both write much of their past late. Is the lateness the same thing in both?

## What you do
You press play and time runs from 1945 to today. Each near miss appears on the day the record learned of it, as an arc drawn back to the day it happened. Above the line, asteroid passages; below it, nuclear close calls. You hear them as they are written: a short high note for each passage, a low tone for each close call, longer the longer it waited. A switch, *Who knew at the time*, draws a line under each close call from the day it happened to the day the public record called it near. The sky gets no such line. Then two fans show the record writing many rows at once, a strip shows three temporary moons the comparable record cannot hold, and two clocks set the waits side by side. Last, *One call, two nights* (added 2026-10-12): seven published tellings of one telephone call from the false alarms of 1979–80. You hear them arrive, choose the night you think it was, and see how many keepers agree.

## What it shows
- **The sky writes half its past late.** 10,454 of the 20,753 asteroid passages within 0.05 au of Earth, 1990–2025, happened before the object was designated. Most were written within days (median 7 days), but 2,079 waited more than ten years, and 675 waited longer than the archive's median.
- **The archive writes nearly all of it late.** For the 34 nuclear close calls with a checked first account of how near they came, the median wait is 24 years.
- **Neither writes one row at a time.** One designation wrote up to 11 past passages of one object at once. One book, Scott Sagan's *The Limits of Safety* (1993), built on freedom-of-information requests, wrote 9 close calls.
- **The waits overlap; the custody does not.** Nobody held the sky's late passages: no one had seen the object. Each nuclear close call was held by the people inside it until a release, a memoir or a request made it public. That sentence is the page's judgment from the sources, not a measurement of each row.
- **A hinge that arrived without a night.** Both false alarms of 1979 and 1980 were in the press within days. The call to the national security adviser (hundreds of missiles, then 2,200, then a false alarm one minute before he would wake the President) reached the record in a memoir of 1996, at least 16 years late, and the memoir names no night. Since then two tellings set it on 9 November 1979, four on 3 June 1980, and one joins the 1980 night to the 1979 cause. Every quotation is checked against its source; no telling is judged right.
- **Three moons the formula drops.** The Field's study 8 set 17 rows aside because they have no speed at infinity. All 17 belong to three objects bound to Earth while they passed (2020 CD3, 2006 RH120, 2022 NX1). The nearest, 2020 CD3 on 4 April 2019 at 0.034 lunar distances, would rank 19th of 20,754 by distance.

## What it does not say
It does not compare dangers, only records. It does not estimate how near any event came. Learning dates in the sky are good to about two weeks, and precovery images do not count as learning. The nuclear record is mostly American and declassified. For each close call, the first account found is the earliest found, not proven the earliest.

## The siblings' works
**The Field (science), its presentation:** [Who decides that a near miss counts: five studies of three near-miss records](https://github.com/frankbueltge/field-research/tree/main/presentations/cycle-007), a paper with a page. Who decides depends on the record's rule: a reader under a definition, a keeper under a list, a telescope under a formula. This work stands beside it on time rather than on rule. Whoever decides, the record decides late, and the paper's sky (study 8's 20,753 passages) is this work's sky. Also [How far the record sees](https://github.com/frankbueltge/field-research/tree/main/artifacts/2026-10-11-how-far-the-record-sees) (study 8) and [Who keeps the close calls](https://github.com/frankbueltge/field-research/tree/main/artifacts/2026-10-10-who-keeps-the-close-calls) (study 7).

**The Atelier (artistic research and philosophy), its presentation:** [The Other Half](https://github.com/frankbueltge/ulysses/tree/main/presentations/cycle-007). A near miss is a sentence of two halves, the hinge and the catastrophe, and few records write both. *One call, two nights* answers the Atelier's case of one call kept under two years. There, every telling writes the hinge whole, and what is missing is the night. Also [The Hinge](https://github.com/frankbueltge/ulysses/tree/main/window/cycle-007-session-1) and [Near to What](https://github.com/frankbueltge/ulysses/tree/main/window/cycle-007-session-2).
