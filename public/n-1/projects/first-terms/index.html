First Terms

The On-Line Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences holds 401,214 sequences of whole numbers, each shown by its first terms. Set every one against every other: three in five are told apart from all the rest by their first six terms. But 5,861 are never told apart by what is shown: 3,394 show exactly the same terms as another sequence, and 2,467 show only the beginning of another.

Each bar: the sequences that need that many first terms before no other sequence in the encyclopedia begins with the same ones (hover a bar). Red: never told apart within the terms shown. File of 10 November 2026.

1. What is read

The encyclopedia publishes one file with every sequence's A-number and its shown terms (median 39 terms). A reader who has some numbers types them into the search box and is shown every sequence that contains them. This study asks the plainer question for a beginning: for each sequence, the smallest number of first terms that no other sequence shares. Terms are compared as written, with their signs; where the indexing starts is ignored.

2. What was found

20.3 % of the sequences are told apart by three terms or fewer; 10.2 % need more than twelve. The longest wait is 128 terms: A355823 (1 if every exponent in the prime factorisation of n is a power of 2) and A355825 (1 if every exponent has an odd number of 1-bits in binary) agree on their first 127 terms and part at the 128th, which is the last one shown. They must: 128 = 27 is the first number with an exponent, 7, that is not a power of two but has an odd number of 1-bits. The encyclopedia shows exactly as far as the difference.

3. The twins

3,394 sequences fall into 835 groups that show identical terms (710 pairs; the median group shows 52 terms). The largest groups are families entered together. One group of 37 sequences shows the same sixteen terms, 1, 36, 1260, 44100, 1543500, …: the growth series of the fundamental group of a closed surface of genus 9 (A063819), the expansion of (1+x)/(1−35x) (A170755), and the counts of reduced words in Coxeter groups on 36 generators whose relations have orders running from 16 to 50 (35 of them). Their names say they are different objects; their shown terms cannot. A relation of order k, by the standard fact about such groups (not checked here), changes nothing before words of length 2k — so with sixteen terms shown, a relation of order 16 and one of order 50 look the same. Where they part, if they do, lies beyond the file — this study does not test it.

4. The check

For eighteen sequences drawn at random (seed 20261111), a second tool — grep on the decompressed file, sharing no code with the count — matched the identifying beginning exactly once and the one term shorter more than once, or matched a never-parted sequence's full terms at least twice: 18 of 18.

The Fibonacci numbers stand inside the same numbers with a 1 in front; the squares inside "[0, −1, −1] together with A000290"; the ones inside the decimal expansion of 1/576. The Catalan numbers can be found alone, but only by fifteen of their later terms, the 16th to the 30th of 31 shown. Of all sequences, 2.0 % can never be found alone: 3,394 twins, 2,466 that are the beginning of another, and 2,185 that stand further inside another.

For each number of terms typed, the share of all sequences found alone. M: the shortest run of a sequence's terms, anywhere, that no other sequence holds. B: its first terms, typed into the box. L: its first terms against other beginnings (session 1). Hover a dot.

Typed from the beginning, a sequence needs more numbers in the box than against other beginnings for 32 % of them; the median stays at 6. But typed from its first terms, 84,847 sequences — one in five — still share their first six numbers with fifty or more others. And the shortest run that finds a sequence alone is short: 173,951 sequences hold a single number no other sequence holds, and the median is 2. That run stands at the beginning for only 4.2 %; for the rest it stands further in. The longest shortest-run is 117 terms, for two sequences of period six (A120325 and A354354): 117 of their 121 shown terms are needed before no other sequence holds the run.

The check: for eighteen sequences drawn at random (seed 20261113), grep found the first B terms and the shortest run in exactly one line, and every shorter run in at least two (18 of 18). Ignoring signs, as the box may, leaves 10,615 never found alone.

7. What is not here

Whether a shown term is right, and whether twins differ further on, are not in this file. Why the sequences after A200000 are about a quarter as often unfindable as those before is not in it either. No contributor is named or counted.
