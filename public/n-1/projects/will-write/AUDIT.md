# Project 8 — pragmatics audit, turned on the material (T3)

*Session 1, 2026-10-14. KsK T3 audits a project's own discourse; here, as in project 7 (a deviation named in `SELECTION.md`), it is
turned on the material's own statements. Written before the session's numbers were read (part A) and after (part B).*

## A. Before the data

**The statement.** "I will write to the hon. Member." Language "is made not to be believed but to be obeyed" (ATP 76); the order-word
is not a command only but every statement's link to an implicit presupposition (ATP 79). The promise to write is a statement whose
whole content is its act: it says nothing about the question asked.

**The incorporeal transformation.** A judge's sentence makes the accused a convict in an instant (ATP 80-81). The promise to write
makes a question asked in public into a matter between two people, in an instant, datable to the sitting. Nothing about the bodies
changes: the question, the minister, the member are where they were. What changes is the regime the answer will travel in.

**The regimes (ATP 147, KsK T3 step 2).** The chamber is a signifying regime with a centre (the Chair, the record, the
"House"); a letter is postsignifying, addressed from one point of subjectification to another. The translation test, made literal by
this material: what passes from the spoken regime into the letter, and what passes back. The material carries its own answer to the
second half: the phrase "a copy in the Library", which, said in the same breath, keeps a door open from the private regime to the
public one.

**What the audit must change, or fail.** Before reading, the design counts promises. If the audit is to do more than rename, it must
change what is counted or shown. A candidate change, named in advance so it can be checked: the unit of the page should be the
**sentence of transformation** (the promise as said, with the Library clause or without it), not a rate.

## B. After the data

**What passed.** 2,645 contributions in ten sampled years hold the sentence. Its forms barely vary across forty-five years ("I shall
write to the noble Lord" in 1985, "I will write to the noble Lord" in 2025): an order-word in the strict sense, a formula whose
force does not depend on what follows it. The count roughly doubled in the Commons and tripled in the Lords between the 1980s and the
2020s (`session1.json`).

**What passes back.** The clause that keeps a door from the letter to the public record ("and place a copy in the Library") is said
with 113 of 2,645 promises, read one by one (`verify.json`). In the Lords it holds at a few in a hundred throughout and is spoken of
as custom ("following the custom of the House", 1985). In the Commons it rose with the years 2000 and 2005 (7 and 8 promises) and is
absent in 2025 (0 of 172). The translation test's answer, on this sample: the regime change goes one way more and more often, and
the way back, which was never usual, is in the Commons's two latest sampled years almost no longer said (1 in 2020, 0 in 2025).
What is measured is the saying, not the depositing: letters may reach the Library unannounced, and that is session 2's question.

**Did the audit change the design?** Yes, before the data and once after. Before: the page's unit is the sentence, a mark per
promise that answers with its words, as part A named; without the audit the session would have built a rate chart (a counterfactual,
marked as an **estimate**). After: the order-word reading made "Library" a clause with a function, not a keyword, and that is why all
140 flagged contributions were read rather than the registered sample; 27 were struck, and the Commons series that is the session's
finding exists only in the verified run. Not triggered.

## C. Session 2, before the register is read

**The register as the material's own translation test.** Session 1 measured what is *said* about the way back (the Library
clause). Parliament keeps a second answer, not spoken but filed: for bills, a list of "will write letters" published beside the
bill's other papers. The translation test (ATP 147) asks what passes and does not pass between regimes; here the register is the
route by which the postsignifying letter is returned to the signifying centre, without anyone saying so in the chamber.

**The order-word's other side.** A promise is a statement whose act is complete when it is said (ATP 80-81). The register is the
only place in this material where the act's *consequence* is recorded. If the audit is to change anything, it changes what a mark
on the page says: not only "promised a copy" or "did not", but "a letter on this bill came back in public within sixty days" or
"none did". Named now so it can be checked after.

**Exposure the audit itself creates.** Matching by bill and date cannot show that the letter returned is the letter promised
(`SESSION2.md`). A mark that says "a letter came back" would claim more than the match shows; the page must say "a letter on this
bill", or the audit has served the design it was meant to test.

## D. Session 2, after the register and the years between

**What passed back, by the register.** For promises made on bills since 2014 the way back exists without being said: Parliament
files the letters and says so on the register's own page (`JOURNAL2.md` items 5-6). Of matched bill-debate promises in 2015-2025, a
letter on the same bill was filed within sixty days for 73 % in the Lords and 53 % in the Commons (`q1.json`; upper bounds). The
translation test's answer changes: session 1 found the spoken way back almost gone; session 2 finds that for bill debates it moved
from the statement into the institution. The order-word's clause ("and place a copy in the Library") was no longer needed where a
filing did its work.

**What the years between show.** Not a turn but a fade: about six spoken Library promises a year in the Commons of the 2000s, about
two in 2016-2024 (`q2.json`). And the clause had a history the count did not: in 2003-2004 members complained in the chamber that
the same formula, used as a written answer, buried replies (`JOURNAL2.md` item 13). The phrase that session 1 read as a door back to
the public was, in those years, also read as a way of closing one.

**Did the audit change the design?** Yes, in two places, and both were named in part C before the data. The marks now carry a third
state (a letter on this bill was filed within sixty days, or none was) and the panel says "a letter on this bill", not "the letter",
because the match cannot show more; without part C the page would have said "answered" (a counterfactual, marked as an **estimate**).
The page's opening sentence, which said the copy "did not follow", was withdrawn and rewritten: it described the saying and was read
as the depositing. Not triggered.

## E. Session 3, before the deposited papers are read

**The clause done, not said.** Session 1 counted the Library clause as a sentence; session 2 found that for bills the depositing
happens unsaid, by a filing. Outside bills there is no bill to file under. What remains is the Library's own list of deposited papers,
where a letter to a member appears under a title the Library writes. The translation test (ATP 147) asked of this register: what of the
chamber's sentence passes into the Library's title? The minister's name, the date, the addressee perhaps; the question, perhaps not.

**What the audit must change, or fail.** Named now: if letters come back here, the page cannot say "the letter" or even "a letter on
this subject" on a name match alone; it can say "a letter from this speaker was placed in the Library within sixty days". The strict
rule (a debate word in the title) is the audit's attempt to reach the subject; if it keeps few matches, the page says the weaker thing.

## F. Session 3, after the deposited papers

**What passed into the Library's title.** The minister's name, the addressee's, a date and, often, the occasion in the Library's own
words ("raised during the debate on ..."). What did not pass, for the name rule, is the question: 15 of 20 matches were the same
minister writing on other subjects. The translation test (ATP 147) answered in a way part E did not foresee: the Library's title keeps
the occasion better than the chamber's sentence keeps the destination.

**Did the audit change the design?** Yes, by holding the page back. Part E said a name match could at most say "a letter from this
speaker"; the reading showed that even that would mislead about the subject, and no mark was coloured. Without part E the page would
have gained a fourth mark state from the name rule (a counterfactual, marked as an **estimate**). Not triggered.
