I will write

In the UK Parliament a question asked in the chamber is asked in public. A minister who cannot or will not answer it there can say: I will write to the honourable Member. In that sentence the answer leaves the public record and becomes a letter between two people. Sometimes the minister adds that a copy will go to the Library of the House, where the public can find it again.

The promises roughly doubled in the Commons between the 1980s and the 2020s (217 in the three years 1980, 1985 and 1990; 466 in 2015, 2020 and 2025), and tripled in the Lords (244; 682). The copy for the Library did not follow. In the Commons it was promised 15 times in 2000 and 2005 together, and not once in the 172 promises of 2025. That counts what was said in the chamber; whether letters reach the Library anyway, unannounced, is the question this project follows next.

The return

Read with: the post (the blue marks are the promises that say so themselves).

For promises made in debates on bills, Parliament keeps a public register of the letters that were sent: the Bills API's "will write letters". Whether a promise in the chamber ever comes back as a letter anyone else can read is the question this project asks next. This session has not read that register.

How this was found

The sample, the definitions and six predictions were written down and published before any promise was read (SELECTION.md). Contributions were found with the Hansard API's phrase search and then read by a fixed pattern; a promise is "I/we will/shall [up to two words] write to", and a blue mark is a promise whose contribution names the Library: all 140 such contributions were read, and the 27 where the Library holds something else (a report, statistics, the British Library) are grey. What the search does not find is not counted, so every number is a floor. A random sample was read by hand to measure the pattern's errors: JOURNAL.md. Names of members are not shown; each sentence links to its debate.
