What the Catalogue Decides

Every earthquake in the US Geological Survey's catalogue has a depth. For many of them, nobody measured it.

What this is

The agency's own words: 10 km is a “fixed depth”; where data are too poor to compute a depth, it is assigned (USGS FAQ). This map shows where that happens: the mid-ocean ridges and the open Atlantic are almost wholly decided; the subduction zones under Chile and Japan are mostly measured. The catalogue knows the planet unevenly, and the depth column does not say so. Whether the small stated uncertainty is a formal figure from the fixed-depth solution is not established here (conjecture).

The catalogue names its own decree

Session 3. The spreadsheet export this page was built from has no column saying which depths were assigned. The same service's per-event documents do: each origin carries a depth-type. For one month (March 2025, network us, magnitude ≥ 4: %%S3N%% events), %%S3OP%% events carry the value “operator assigned”. All of them sit at exactly 10 or 35 km, and all but %%S3ONE%% event at exactly those depths is flagged so. So the rule used on this page (“decided” = exactly 10 or 35 km) stands, for this month and this network, against the agency’s own flag.

History runs mostly one way, in the little the documents keep: %%S3SINGLE%% of %%S3N%% events have a single origin version. Of the %%S3OP%% assigned depths, %%S3OVER%% replaced an earlier, different depth from a regional network, %%S3OVERDAYS%%. The reverse — an earlier 10 or 35 km later replaced by a measured depth — appears in %%S3LIFT%% of %%S3SOLVED%% measured events, and in those the earlier version carries no flag, so that it was a decree is conjecture.

Data: U.S. Geological Survey, ComCat via the FDSN event service, magnitude ≥ 4, 2020-01-01 to 2025-12-31, queried 2026-10-07; public domain (USGS).
