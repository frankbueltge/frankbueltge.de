The nominal

Sections can be read in any order; each says which others it connects to.

rings: how far each ring's heaviest bell sits from the note it is named by, in cents (a hundredth of a semitone), 6,549 ringsbells: how far each bell sits from its own ring's heaviest bell, after the scale between them is taken out. Both drawn as shares, so the two shapes can be compared; hover for counts.

1 · A ring keeps its own pitch

Church bells in England are hung in rings of five, six, eight or more and rung in turn. Dove's Guide for Church Bell Ringers lists 64,136 bells, and for 46,976 of them a measured frequency: the bell's nominal, the partial that mostly decides the note a listener hears. It also names every bell by a note.

Inside a ring, bells agree with each other to a median of 4.5 cents. The ring as a whole sits a median of 22 cents away from the note it is named by, and 45 % of rings sit more than a quarter of a semitone away: almost as if their pitch had been chosen at random between two notes of the piano.

The wide, flat shape in the figure is the rings against the world's tuning, A = 440 Hz; the narrow peak is each bell against its own ring. A ring is in tune with itself, not with the note it is called.

Connects to 2 (why the names still fit), 3 (when rings began to meet A = 440), 6 (the check).

2 · The named note

Dove does not name a bell by its sound. It names the ring by a key, usually from its heaviest bell, the tenor, and every other bell by its place in a major scale above that key (Dove, help on bell details). Measured against those names, the median bell is 22 cents from its note, and 94.2 % are within half a semitone of it, so that the name is the nearest note. 2,185 bells in rings are nearer to another note than to their own name; 1,919 of them because their whole ring sits past the midpoint between two notes, only 266 because the bell itself strays from its ring.

Connects to 1 (the ring's own pitch), 5 (what the metal decides).

3 · When rings began to meet A = 440

median cents of a ring's tenor from its named note, by the tenor's fifty years of castingmedian cents of a bell from its own tenor, by the bell's fifty years of casting

For rings whose tenor was cast from the 1350s to the 1900s, the distance from the named note stays between 22 and 29 cents; pitches scattered at random between two notes would give 25. Rings with tenors of the 1950s sit 15 cents away, of the 2000s 11. Inside the ring the distance shrinks slowly over six centuries, from about 9 cents for bells of the 1400s to 3 for bells of the 1900s. A bell's date here is its casting; a bell retuned later keeps its old date, so these eras are what the record says, not when the sound was made.

Connects to 1 (the two distributions), 4 (what the record's dates are).

4 · Years the record gives the oldest bells

years ending in 99years ending in 00 or 50other years · heights on a square-root scale

Of 4,568 bells dated before 1600, 3,759 carry a date marked circa. The record's own rule: a bell judged only to a century is dated to its last year, "mediæval" as 1499, "pre-Reformation" as 1599. So 593 bells stand at 1499, 276 at 1399 and 216 at 1599; 1,134 of the 4,568 at a year ending in 99, 1,067 more at a round fifty. Nearly half the bells the record dates before 1600 stand at a year ending in 99 or at a round fifty: a convention or a rounded estimate, not a year read from the bell.

Connects to 3 (eras built on these dates).

5 · Weight and pitch

A heavier bell sounds lower. For 38,905 bells with a measured weight and a nominal, the frequency falls with the weight's power -0.26 (bells of one shape scaled up would give one third, -0.33), with a scatter of 0.31 octave about that line. The line holds for bells cast before 1800 (-0.40, scatter 0.13 octave) and loosens for bells cast since 1900 (-0.22, scatter 0.38 octave). Why the newer bells scatter is not counted here; they include carillons and light rings of other profiles, which may be the reason (an assumption, not a finding).

Connects to 2 (the name ignores the metal).

6 · What was predicted, and the check

Ten predictions were registered and pushed before any bell was read (the selection). 5 held.

  • P1 held The file holds at least 40,000 bells. 64,136 bells (seen before the count, while the parser was written).
  • P2 held At least 25 % carry a nominal. 73.2 % (seen before the count, as P1).
  • P3 failed Median distance from the named note at most 20 cents. 21.8 cents.
  • P4 held At least 90 % within 50 cents of the named note. 94.2 %.
  • P5 failed Weight-pitch slope between -0.40 and -0.27. -0.260.
  • P6 failed Scatter about it at most a quarter octave. 0.31 octave.
  • P7 held Before 1600 the commonest year is 1499 or 1599. 1499, 593 bells.
  • P8 failed At least 20 % of weights are estimates. 16.6 %.
  • P9 failed Old trebles of eight at least 10 cents sharp of their tenor; new ones within 5. old: +5.0 cents (281 bells); new: +1.5 (575).
  • P10 held The check agrees on at least 19 of 20. 20 of 20; this page's cents within 1.0 of Dove's own.

The check: twenty bells drawn at random from those with a nominal, each compared with its tower's page on Dove's own site. Weight, frequency and note agree for all of them. Dove's page also shows each bell's cents from its note; this page's arithmetic agrees with it to within a cent.

Connects to every section above.

7 · What this is, and what it is not

A first session of a project of three to five, not a work. The bells were not heard: everything here is the record's numbers. The nearest neighbours are Dove's own charts of bells by nominal and by weight, and its cents figure on each tower's page; and Bill Hibbert's studies of how founders tuned (stretch tuning of old bells). What this page adds so far: the ring's distance from the world's pitch set beside its distance from itself, across every ring the record measures, and how the record dates its oldest bells.
