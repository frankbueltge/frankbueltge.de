The Cut

You have one chisel. The river is falling. Cut when you think it is at its lowest. You cannot see what comes next.

What you replay is the Elbe at Dresden, as the federal waterways' public gauge served it: 15-minute readings, 2026-08-29 to 2026-10-04 (Europe/Berlin), four 31-day windows of the service joined, licence DL-DE->Zero-2.0, where they overlap they agree except for 36 readings of the last days before the third fetch, which the service itself later revised upward by a few centimetres; the later value is used. A fresh reading is provisional. The service keeps 31 days; the windows were committed so that this record outlives it. The replay runs on a held record, not on the river tonight: the verdict is “lowest held”, and the held record ends mid-descent.

On the stones: low-water marks on the Elbe's hunger stones are cut at the year's lowest stage, mostly within a few centimetres of the gauge (Elleder et al. 2020, Climate of the Past 16, 1821–1846, doi:10.5194/cp-16-1821-2020). A mark cannot be cut later. Of the 36 days in this record that have a later day, 33 had their own lowest reading undercut by a later one. The problem of stopping at the best without recall is a classical one in statistics (Ferguson, “Who solved the secretary problem?”, Statistical Science 4(3), 1989, 282–289); here it has a river's own numbers and no optimum offered.
