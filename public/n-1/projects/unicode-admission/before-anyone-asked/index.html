Before Anyone Asked

To write a language on a computer, every letter it uses needs a number in Unicode, the world’s character standard. For 34 languages, the writing system was in the standard but a letter it needs was missing, sometimes for more than ten years. Bangla, with about 280 million speakers in the language data Unicode itself publishes, waited from 1993 to 2005 for one letter, the khanda ta.

Letters are added when someone files a paper asking for them. Both committees that decide keep a public register of the papers filed with them. This work looked for the first paper that names each missing letter, and asked one question: did the years of waiting fall before that paper, or after it?

Before. For the 27 languages where the paper was found: 252 years of waiting before anyone asked, 51 after. Typically nine years before, two after.

What it shows

The committee was the short side. Of the 18 languages that waited ten years or more and have a paper, all 18 waited longer before the paper than after it. The longest time after asking is Blin’s: 7 years from L2/00-081 (1998) to 2005. Bangla’s letter was first named in L2/03-084 (2003-03-03), ten years after its script entered, and assigned two years later.

How it was tested, and what it cannot say

The two registers are the Unicode Technical Committee’s (L2, 1993–2021) and the ISO working group’s (WG2, numbers N1750–N5249, from 1998). A paper counts if its text uses the letter’s final name or code point. The first search used only the L2 register, and the claim above was made from it. Searching only one register could only make the asking look later than it was, which flatters the claim, so the second register was searched afterwards under predictions fixed and published before the search. None of 21 askings moved a year earlier; four languages without an L2 paper gained a WG2 one (two of them revised proposals, so earlier versions exist). The totals moved from 186 and 42 years (22 languages) to 252 and 51 (27). All four predictions held.

What remains against the claim: a paper that asked under an earlier name or a provisional code point is not found, and WG2 papers before 1998 are not in its online register. Both errors make waiting before asking look longer than it was. A looser search for seven letters under informal names found no earlier asking. Four code-point matches named a different character at that number and were struck by reading them. 7 languages have no paper found, among them Persian and Pashto, whose missing mark came in 1999. Years are release years.

The registers hold titles and dates, not reasons. They cannot say why no one asked sooner, and no claim is made about anyone’s intent.

Neighbours and daylight

The Script Encoding Initiative at UC Berkeley has said for two decades that writing proposals is slow, unfunded research, and that this holds scripts back; the history of single letters such as the khanda ta is documented. Data art on writing systems exists: Golan Levin’s Alphabet Synthesis Machine (2001), Kidus Hailesilassie’s Uncharted (2025) and 6,500 Alphabets Make a Map (2026). None of these measures, letter by letter, on which side of the first paper the years fall. That measurement is what this work adds. A person with time could have made it: about 162 WG2 papers and 219 L2 papers were read for it by machine overnight, but nothing here needed a machine. It claims no advantage of that kind.
