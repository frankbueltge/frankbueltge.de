The Days, End to End

a study, not yet a work — project 1, session 3, 26 September 2026

The world's clocks count a day as 86,400 seconds. The Earth, measured turning every day, almost never takes exactly that: most days it takes up to a few thousandths of a second more, some days a little less.

Here are 19,622 of those differences, from 3 January 1973 to 23 September 2026, laid end to end. Each day lasts, below, exactly as long as it differed. More than fifty-three years of them take 27.6 seconds.

The years are not evenly spaced: a year takes as much room as its days differed. Where the days ran long, the clocks fell behind the planet, and 25 times a second was added to them to catch up. In the calendar those marks fall between one and seven years apart; here they fall far more evenly, because each one paid back about a second of long days.

The last second was added on 1 January 2017. Since then twenty bulletins, one every six months, have said: no second this time. Those years are 18 % of the calendar and 7 % of the strip — 2.0 seconds, enlarged here:

The days have come so close to 86,400 seconds that 1,448 of them were shorter (0.69 s in all), and five times since 2017 the planet and the clock agreed with no one making them agree.

Until now, whenever the difference neared a second, someone stepped in. A draft before the General Conference on Weights and Measures, meeting 13–15 October 2026, proposes that from 20 May 2027 no one will: the clocks may drift up to an hour from the planet. (As of this study the resolution is a draft; whether it is adopted is not yet known.) The days will go on being measured, and a strip like this one could go on growing. Only the marks would stop.

How it is made

Every day is one measured value: the length-of-day excess from the U.S. Naval Observatory's IERS Bulletin A (rapid-service values, “distribution unlimited”), committed in this repository and read by a script that writes this page; nothing is fetched while you look. A day's width on the strip, and its duration in the sound, is the size of its excess, whichever way it went; days shorter than 86,400 s are drawn below the line and heard as a low hum. In the sound each day begins with a click, so the pitch you hear is the length of the day: slow clicks for long days, a rising whine as the days approach 86,400 s. The added seconds are heard as a low thud. The twenty bulletins make no sound: they are statements that nothing need be done. The newest days are provisional and may be revised.
