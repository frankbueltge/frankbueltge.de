What the Repair Keeps

Project Gutenberg is a free library of books whose copyright has run out, typed and proofread by volunteers from old printed copies. When they find a misprint, they fix it, and sometimes they say so in a transcriber’s note.

Of 1,288 English books drawn at random from the library, 612 carry such a note. 106 of the notes list their corrections one by one: 969 corrections in all, 75 % of them one character away from what was printed. 224 say that errors were corrected and list none. The rest speak of other things: italics, hyphens, page layout.

The note spread: 6.5 % of the books released before 2005 have one, 65.2 % of those released since 2010. The list did not spread with it. In every period since 2005, about one book in ten lists its corrections (9 %, 12 %, 9 %, 10 %, 10 %). What the note gained went elsewhere: to italics and hyphens, and to saying “corrected” without a list, which grows from about one noted book in five in 2005–09 to about half in 2015–24. The word “silently” appears in 0 notes released before 2005, 2 from 2005 to 2009, and 116 since: the note now says, more and more often, that what it did is not in it.

The hand and the ear

A printed page was once set by hand from small pieces of metal type, one per letter, picked from the compartments of a case. One misprint that leaves a trace of that is the turned letter: a u set upside down prints as an n, a d as a p. With the sample completed (1,030 listed corrections from 1,495 books), 7 corrections are exactly this, where chance would give about 1.7. The type shows through, faintly: 4 of the 7 come from a single book. Here they are, the wrong letter drawn as the right one turned over.

The hand shows more plainly in what fell out. 355 corrections put back a missing character; 135 take out one too many, and 51 of those doubled the letter beside it.

It is a printed where e was meant (17 times), and 93 of the 224 one-letter swaps put a vowel in place of a vowel, in 36 books: the ear’s errors, words spelled as they sound, and names brought to one spelling. Five predictions were registered before this count (SESSION2.md). Two held: turned letters at least three times chance, and dropped characters at least one and a half times as common as extra ones. Three failed: the commonest swap is not n/u; corrections to punctuation alone are 5 %, not 10 % (the reading pattern misses a correction to a bare mark, so this is partly the instrument); and corrections of capital to small letter are 3 % of swaps, not 5 %. The vowel count is exploratory. Many of these books were set by machine, where a turned letter is rare; the catalogue does not say which.

What this does not say

A note is what the transcribers wrote, not everything they did: a book with no note may have been corrected, and a list may be incomplete. A listed change is not always a misprint: some are a chosen spelling (Wady to Wadi) or a change between editions. The notes were found and the corrections read out by pattern, and the pattern was checked by reading: of 200 doubtful pairs, 111 were struck and 89 restated as the note gives them; of 100 others read at random, one was wrong and stands. Release year is when the e-book appeared, not when the book was printed. Nothing here judges any volunteer’s care: the notes follow the conventions of their time, and those conventions are what the counts show. The words are quoted as printed and corrected in books of their own times. One of six predictions registered before the count failed: more books carry a note (47.5 %) than the 45 % expected.
