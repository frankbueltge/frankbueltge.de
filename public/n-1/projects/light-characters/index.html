Within sight of itself

A lighthouse says its name in light. Each one flashes, or goes dark, in a rhythm of its own: two white flashes every ten seconds, one green flash every three. A sailor who sees a light at night counts the rhythm, finds it in the list, and knows where they are. The International Association of Lighthouse Authorities puts the rule in one sentence: lights with the same character "should be separated in distance, so that they are outside each nominal light range, and not adjacent, to avoid confusion."

So a light's rhythm should be alone as far as the light reaches. Is it? Below is every rhythmic light in the US government's List of Lights for the world outside the United States (32,777 of 42,501 listed), drawn as a point and nothing else: the coastlines are drawn only by their lights. A line joins a light to the nearest light with exactly the same rhythm (same pattern, colour and period) wherever that light stands within the first one's own range.

a light seen 15 nautical miles or more, whose rhythm stands again within its reach the same, for smaller lights

Tap or click near a yellow line to see the two lights side by side, blinking as listed. Positions are rounded; the map is equirectangular, 60°S to 80°N.

What the list says

Of 30,765 rhythmic lights with a stated range, 8,201 (27 %) have a light of the same rhythm within their own range. Among the 4,314 great lights, seen fifteen miles or more, it is 624 (14.5 %); among the smallest, under eight miles, 29 %.

Most of the small repeats are not mistakes. On harbour walls and channel buoys the colour says which side to pass, and the rhythm is shared on purpose: one green flash every three seconds, on every green light of a harbour. There a light's character is grammar, not a name. Twenty such pairs, drawn at random and read in full, were the front and rear of one leading line, the rear lights of two neighbouring lines, the heads of the same or the next harbour's breakwaters, two faces of one bridge, a row of special marks, a wind farm, sector lights whose beams do not face each other, a coastal light and an aviation light, and three plain coastal lights repeating each other 16 to 36 km apart.

The question this page set out to ask was whether lights keep apart from their own rhythm: whether the same rhythm stands farther away than chance would put it. The list says no, at least not as a pattern. Shuffling rhythms among lights of the same volume and size, the same rhythm turns out to stand closer than chance (0.78 times the shuffled distance for great lights, 0.42 for small ones), because each coast keeps its own habits of rhythm. Shuffling only within squares of two degrees, the great lights stand at 1.03 times chance and the small ones at 0.90: each name is kept apart about as far as a random draw from its own coast would keep it, and no farther. (That last comparison was not registered in advance; it is reported as exploratory.)

What this page cannot say

  • The range here is the nominal range in the list. Whether two lights can actually be seen from one place depends on height, weather, the shape of the coast and the arcs in which each light is shown. A line is a repeat within reach on paper.
  • Two lights count as the same when the list gives the same pattern, colour and period; the lengths of each flash are not compared.
  • 4,328 fixed (steady) lights carry no rhythm and are left out; so are 5,344 entries whose rhythm states no period (quick flashing lights, radar beacons and others).
  • The list is corrected every week. This is its state as downloaded on 2026-10-16.
