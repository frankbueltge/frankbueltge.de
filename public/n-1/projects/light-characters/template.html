Within sight of itself

A lighthouse says its name in light. Each one flashes, or goes dark, in a rhythm of its own: two white flashes every ten seconds, one green flash every three. A sailor who sees a light at night counts the rhythm, finds it in the list, and knows where they are. The International Association of Lighthouse Authorities puts the rule in one sentence: lights with the same character "should be separated in distance, so that they are outside each nominal light range, and not adjacent, to avoid confusion."

So a light's rhythm should be alone as far as the light reaches. Is it? Below is every rhythmic light in the US government's List of Lights for the world outside the United States ({{N_RHYTHMIC}} of {{N_LIGHTS}} listed), drawn as a point and nothing else: the coastlines are drawn only by their lights. A line joins a light to the nearest light with exactly the same rhythm (same pattern, colour and period) wherever that light stands within the first one's own range.

What is left

Of the {{N_PAIRS2}} great lights whose own rhythm stands again within their reach, the list itself tells most pairs apart once everything it says is read: where each light is shown, how long each flash lasts, how far each one reaches, and what each is called. {{S_NOTHING}} pairs remain that nothing in the list tells apart: the same pattern, colour and period, no different flash length listed, each within the other's range, water from which both are seen at once, and no word that they belong to one leading line. Half of them stand more than {{S_NOTHING_KM}} km apart; {{S_NOTHING_10}} more than 10 km. For only {{S_NOTHING_SAME}} of them does the list give the flash lengths of both and find them equal; for the rest it does not say, so a watch might still tell them apart.

What the list says

Of {{N_RANGE}} rhythmic lights with a stated range, {{N_WITHIN}} ({{PCT_ALL}} %) have a light of the same rhythm within their own range. Among the {{N_MAJOR}} great lights, seen fifteen miles or more, it is {{N_MAJOR_WITHIN}} ({{PCT_MAJOR}} %); among the smallest, under eight miles, {{PCT_MINOR}} %.

Most of the small repeats are not mistakes. On harbour walls and channel buoys the colour says which side to pass, and the rhythm is shared on purpose: one green flash every three seconds, on every green light of a harbour. There a light's character is grammar, not a name. Twenty such pairs, drawn at random and read in full, were the front and rear of one leading line, the rear lights of two neighbouring lines, the heads of the same or the next harbour's breakwaters, two faces of one bridge, a row of special marks, a wind farm, sector lights whose beams do not face each other, a coastal light and an aviation light, and three plain coastal lights repeating each other 16 to 36 km apart.

The question this page set out to ask was whether lights keep apart from their own rhythm: whether the same rhythm stands farther away than chance would put it. The list says no, at least not as a pattern. Shuffling rhythms among lights of the same volume and size, the same rhythm turns out to stand closer than chance ({{R_MAJOR}} times the shuffled distance for great lights, {{R_MINOR}} for small ones), because each coast keeps its own habits of rhythm. Shuffling only within squares of two degrees, the great lights stand at {{R_MAJOR_2}} times chance and the small ones at {{R_MINOR_2}}: each name is kept apart about as far as a random draw from its own coast would keep it, and no farther. (That last comparison was not registered in advance; it is reported as exploratory.)

The arcs

Reach is not sight. Many lights are shown only over the water they serve: a lighthouse on a cape is dark toward the land behind it, a leading light shines down one channel, a sector light paints the safe water white and the dangers red and green. The list gives these arcs as bearings from seaward. So the second question: of the {{N_PAIRS2}} great lights whose own rhythm stands within their reach, how many have water from which both lights of the pair can be seen at once, each inside its own range and its own arc?

{{N_KEEP}} of {{N_PAIRS2}} ({{PCT_KEEP}} %) can be seen together; the arcs part only {{N_PARTED}}. {{N_RESTR}} pairs ({{PCT_RESTR}} %) have an arc on one light or both, but an arc rarely turns a light away from its twin. Half the pairs that can be seen together share at least {{MED_SHARE}} % of the great light's own lit water. And the arcs do not keep the same rhythm apart any better than they keep different rhythms apart: of {{N_WITHC}} great lights paired instead with their nearest light of another rhythm, {{N_KEEPC}} ({{PCT_KEEPC}} %) can be seen together with it. Neither distance nor direction holds the name. Of the eleven that the arcs part, six are a light turned away from a small twin that does not reach back; four are leading or directional lights looking down different channels; one is two sector lights whose sectors do not face each other.

Among the repeats that can be seen together, most are designed to be: the front and rear of a leading line are meant to be seen at once, one above the other. Twenty pairs drawn at random were read: nine were the two lights of one leading line, three the lights of two different lines, one two sector lights, and seven independent coastal lights. Among those seven: Nisis Dhisvato and Akra Gria in the Cyclades, 25 km apart, both one white flash every ten seconds, both shown all round; they can be seen together over 88 % of the water the smaller one reaches.

The rest of the character

Two lights of one rhythm may still differ in how long each flash lasts: one white flash every five seconds can be a tenth of a second or a full second long. The list gives these lengths for many lights. In {{N_BOTH}} of the {{N_PAIRS2}} pairs it gives them for both, and in {{N_PH_PARTED}} of those ({{PCT_PH}} %) they differ. They differ mostly where the lights are independent: Cabo Finisterre flashes for 0.3 s every five, the front light of a range 28 km away for a full second. In {{N_PH_PERC}} pairs the difference is half a second or more. {{N_ROT}} of the differing pairs are the same cycle, listed from a different first flash; they are counted as the same.

And reach runs both ways. A great light may reach its twin while the twin, a small harbour light, does not reach back: near the great light only one of the two is seen. Of the {{N_PAIRS2}} pairs, {{N_MUTUAL}} are within each other's range.

What remains after all this is mostly designed: in twenty pairs drawn at random from those the arcs, flash lengths and reach do not part, ten were the front and rear of one leading line, two the lights of two different lines, three lights of one harbour or bay, one an auxiliary light shown in fog, and four independent coastal lights: Nusa Lembongan and Nusa Penida in Bali, 23 km apart, both three white flashes every fifteen seconds. Where the list's own words say a pair is a leading line, the page counts it as the design; that is a word test, not a reading, and the twenty read agree with it.

What this page cannot say

  • The range here is the range in the list, and the arcs are the arcs in the list. Land and sea are not told apart: water from which both lights can be seen may be land, and a hill may hide a light the list shows. Whether two lights can actually be seen from one place depends on height, weather and the shape of the coast. Where a remark gives no bearing (“visible on range line only”, “obscured when bearing greater than…”), the light is taken as shown all round; nine pairs that can be seen together contain such a light.
  • Two lights count as the same when the list gives the same pattern, colour and period. Flash lengths part a pair only where the list gives them for both; the light on this page blinks in the listed lengths, or in lengths drawn from its pattern where none are listed. “Leading line” is a word test on the list's names and remarks (range, front, rear, leading, synchronized).
  • Two lights count as the same when the list gives the same pattern, colour and period. Flash lengths part a pair only where the list gives them for both lights; where it gives them for one or neither, the pair is not parted, and the light on this page blinks in lengths drawn from its pattern.
  • {{N_FIXED}} fixed (steady) lights carry no rhythm and are left out; so are {{N_UNPARSED}} entries whose rhythm states no period (quick flashing lights, radar beacons and others).
  • The list is corrected every week. This is its state as downloaded on 2026-10-16 and again, unchanged, on 2026-10-17.
