Translations of translations

Tatoeba is a collection of short sentences and their translations, made by volunteers: {{SENT}} sentences in {{LANGS}} languages. Each translation is a link between two sentences that one person made. A translation can itself be translated, and then the first sentence and the last are joined only through the one between: Tatoeba shows such pairs in grey, as "translations of translations", and its own wiki warns that "sometimes all the direct translations in a series are valid but the ends of the chain should not be linked."

The collection holds {{DIRECT}} direct pairs, and {{INDIRECT}} pairs joined only through a sentence in a third language. This page asks how often the far end still says what the near end said.

Read side by side, about one translation of a translation in five no longer says quite the same thing; about one direct translation in twenty-five.

{{CHART}}

Shifted or different, as judged by one reader of both ends: 29 of 147 readable translations of translations, 2 of 50 direct translations, drawn at random. The lines are 95 % intervals: with samples this small, the direct share could lie anywhere from about 1 % to 13 %. The reader did not know which pairs were which until all 200 were read.

The sentence in the middle

For every pair that lost something, the sentence in between was then read. In {{NFIRST}} the loss was already in the first translation, in {{NSECOND}} it came in the second. In {{NCHAIN}}, each translation can be read as faithful, and the loss is only in the chain: the middle sentence has a word that means two things, and the second translator took the other one. Most often that word is English "you", which does not say whether it is one person or several, close or formal.

Who is spoken to: counted across the whole collection

French and Russian say who is spoken to in the same way: tu and ты for one person you know well, vous and вы for several people, or for one you address politely. English you does not say which. The second session took every French sentence and every Russian sentence that are joined only through a sentence in between ({{S2CHAINS}} such chains), and read from the pronouns alone whether each end says tu or vous, ты or вы.

Where a French and a Russian translation were linked directly, they disagree on who is spoken to in {{S2DIRECT}}. Joined through an English sentence with "you", they disagree in {{S2ENGYOU}} of chains; through German, in {{S2DEU}}.

{{S2CHART}}

Share of French–Russian pairs whose ends disagree (one says tu/ты, the other vous/вы), by what stands between them; the number of pairs with both ends readable beside each bar. The upright mark on each row ("chance") is what two translators choosing independently, each by their own habit, would give.

That the English chains disagree more often than two independent choices would is not the English sentence's doing. Taken together, linked or not, a French and a Russian translation of the same English "you" sentence agree {{S2AGREEALL}} of the time, about what chance gives. But {{S2LINKEDAGREE}} of the agreeing pairs have since been linked directly by someone, and only {{S2LINKEDDIS}} of the disagreeing ones. So a translation of a translation is what is left over once people have linked what fits: the pairs nobody joined are mostly the ones that cross. Contributors often give both forms: of {{S2BRIDGES}} English "you" sentences with readable French and Russian translations, {{S2BOTHBOTH}} have a tu and a vous and a ты and a вы among them. How it was read, and what that is worth

The graph's counts are exact. Every reading is a machine's: one language model, in one session, reading the two ends in their own languages and judging same, shifted (one distinction or detail changed), different or cannot read. Those are estimates, not ground truth. 28 of 200 readings are marked low-confidence, where one language was read by its resemblance to others (Old Prussian, Kadazan, Guarani, Klingon, Berber, Marathi, Tatar, Waray, Volapük); 3 pairs could not be read at all. A rule fixed during the reading, before the kinds were revealed: where a language's grammar forces a choice (a gender, a formal "you") that the other did not make, the pair counts as same. That rule covered 11 translations of translations and 5 direct pairs; without it the shares would be 27 % and 14 %. Whether a stranger reads this page as meant is untested.

Session 2's pronoun rules are word lists fixed before reading (registration). They were checked by reading 80 sentences without knowing their class: on all {{S2CHECKN}} sentences the rules called tu-like or vous-like, the reading agreed ({{S2CHECKA}} of {{S2CHECKN}}; 36 was the registered floor). Their errors run the other way: "rendez-vous" holds a "vous", "ton" can be "tone", Russian "ух ты" is an exclamation, and an imperative without a pronoun is missed, so some readable sentences are set aside, never read wrongly in this sample. The readings are a machine's and are estimates.
