What is held back

When a state joins a human rights treaty, it may say, in the same act, which parts it does not accept, or how it will read them: a reservation, or a declaration. It may later withdraw what it held back. The United Nations keeps the record of these statements. Scholars have coded it provision by provision, for every state that joined nine of the treaties, up to 2014.

Read that way, no treaty is held whole by everyone who joined it, and most of what was held back is still held back: 224 of 1,293 held-back provisions (17 %) have been withdrawn, after a median of 14 years.

Each column is one provision (an article, or a paragraph of one), in the treaty's order. Its height is the share of the treaty's parties that hold it back; a column reaches the top at one party in five. Hover a column for its article and counts. Only provisions that oblige states are coded here: the clauses on disputes, committees and procedure are not in the file.

Where it gathers

The provision held back by the most states is article 16 (1) of the Convention on Discrimination against Women, on equality in marriage and family relations: 33 of its 185 parties. But the shape is not only a few dark columns. In the Convention against Torture only 11 of 154 parties hold anything back, yet 1 of its 29 coded provisions is held by all: a handful of statements, some of them covering a whole run of articles at once, are enough to leave almost no provision whole.

What stood still in 2014 had stood for a median of 20 years; what was withdrawn had stood 14 years (half of the withdrawals between 5 and 18).

How far the coding can be trusted

Forty rows of the file were drawn at random and read again, by this practice, against the United Nations' own pages, without seeing how the file codes them. Where the pages could be read, the reading and the file agree at 32 of 37. Four of the disagreements are the file being finer than the reading (it codes paragraphs, the reading counted articles), and one is a footnote the reading missed. The pages have moved on since 2014: they record withdrawals as late as 2025 that the file cannot hold.

After 2014: what came back, and what arrived

The file stops in 2014; the United Nations' pages do not. For each state that still held something back from a treaty in 2014 (218 such pairs could be found on the pages), the pages were searched for a withdrawal dated 2015 or later. And each state that joined one of the nine treaties after 2014 was read for what it held back on joining.

In twelve years, 27 of the 218 (12 %) withdrew something, of the ten read by hand seven only in part. In the same years, 38 times, a state joined a treaty holding something back.

a state withdrew some or all of what it held back from a treaty (by the year of its latest withdrawal)
a state joined a treaty holding something back…only clauses on disputes or committees

Above the years, returns; below, arrivals. 12 of the 38 arrivals hold back only clauses this file never coded (going to the International Court of Justice, letting a committee inquire), so the count of 2014 could not have shown them even had it gone on. The search was checked by reading twenty pairs by hand without seeing its answer; the two agreed at 20 of 20. Hover an arrival for the state and treaty.

What was predicted before the file was opened

  • the provision held back by most states is a dispute-settlement clause: failed. CEDAW 16 (1), 33 states; then CEDAW 9 (2), 25; CERD 4 (a)-(c), 20-21. The file codes only provisions of function "obligation" (all 72,396 rows); no dispute clause is in it, so the prediction could not hold here, and says nothing of the record itself.
  • at most 25 % of held-back rows withdrawn by the last year: held. 224 of 1293 (17.3 %), last year 2014
  • median standing time of withdrawn rows at least 10 years: held. median 14 years (quartiles 5 and 18), n 223
  • in every treaty at least half of the provisions held by all parties: failed. CAT 1 of 29; CED 46 of 51; CEDAW 11 of 46; CERD 3 of 17; CRC 32 of 98; CRMW 116 of 144; CRPD 58 of 92; ICCPR 9 of 63; ICESCR 9 of 27

What this page does not do. It does not judge any reservation lawful or unlawful, and it ranks no state. A provision held back is a fact about a statement made in the open, which the state may withdraw; the people the treaties protect are those it touches, and the page says no more about them than the record does.
