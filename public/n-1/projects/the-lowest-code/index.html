The Lowest Code

Usually every two years, more often for a weak one, an inspector stands under each highway bridge in the United States and gives its deck, its superstructure and its substructure (or, for a culvert, the culvert) a number from 9, excellent, to 0, failed. Since 2018 a bridge is called Poor when the lowest of these is 4 or less. Concrete and steel left alone only decay; the number is a judgement made at a date. Followed through 33 years of Alabama's records, the number rose 6,882 times — and 6,611 of those rises (96.1 %) came with no reconstruction recorded. In 2025, 410 Alabama bridges are Poor and open to traffic; half of them have been Poor for 13 years or more, and 46 in every file since their number began in 1993.

The 545 bridges whose lowest code is 4 or less in the 2025 file (410 open, 135 closed to all traffic), one row each, by structure number in the record; no names, no places. Codes 5 to 9 in blue, 4 orange, 3 red, 2 and below dark red, N (not applicable) grey, blank where the number is not in that year's file.

1. What is followed

The Federal Highway Administration publishes each state's inventory once a year, one fixed-width line per structure. For each bridge this study takes the least of the four condition codes (deck, superstructure, substructure, culvert), whether it is open, posted or closed, the date of its inspection, its year built and year reconstructed, and the traffic the record says crosses it each day. A bridge is followed by its structure number. The Coding Guide defines 4 as "POOR CONDITION - advanced section loss, deterioration, spalling or scour", and 3 as "SERIOUS CONDITION … Local failures are possible."

The record holds its bridges by number, and the numbers break. Not one of the 15,421 structure numbers of the 1992 file recurs in 1993: Alabama's 1992 file numbers its bridges in another scheme, so every line on this page begins in 1993. After that, between 51 and 381 numbers enter each year and between 71 and 285 leave — replacements, new bridges and renumberings, which the record does not tell apart.

2. How long Poor lasts

From 1993 to 2025, 4,745 times a bridge's lowest code fell to 4 or less and stayed there for a run of years. 1,730 of those runs ended with the code rising to 5 or more, after a median of 3 years; 2,458 ended with the number leaving the files; 545 are still running in 2025. Of the runs that ended in a rise, 91.5 % ended with no reconstruction recorded — neither the year built nor the year reconstructed changed.

That is what the record says, not what was done. The Coding Guide does not count as reconstruction, among others, "Emergency repair to restore structural integrity to the previous status following an accident" or "Work performed to keep a bridge operational while plans for complete rehabilitation or replacement are under preparation". A rise with nothing recorded may be such work, or a different judgement of the same steel. The record cannot say which.

Among open Poor bridge-years, 15.5 % were open with no restriction; most were posted for load. The traffic the record gives for those years adds up to about 21 billion crossings — an estimate, the record's own daily figure times 365, and that figure is not always counted again: bridge 006850, steel, built 1960, code 3 in its substructure in every file from 1993 to 2025, posted, carries 35 vehicles a day in each of them.

The line measured here is this record's line.

4. Who this is about

People who cross these bridges, and people beneath them; the public whose money decides which bridge is repaired when; and anyone hurt at one, who cannot bring this record into a damages action: the disclaimer before every file quotes 23 U.S.C. §409, under which such data "shall not be subject to discovery or admitted into evidence in a federal or state court proceeding". Nothing on this page judges a bridge safe or unsafe.
