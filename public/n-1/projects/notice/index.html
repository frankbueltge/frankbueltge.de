Notice

When a government moves the clocks, almost every phone and server learns of it from one file, kept by volunteers: the time-zone database. Across thirty years of its releases, 114 changes of a region's clock reached it only after the clocks had already moved — and until a release came, software that relied on it showed the old hour. Since 2020, 3. Counted in hours (section 7), the file's newest release showed some region a wrong hour for 918,348 region-hours since 1997 — four fifths of them only found wrong years later.

1. What is read

The tz database records, for about three hundred regions, the offset of local time from universal time and every change of it, past and announced. IANA keeps each release since 1993. This study compiles every release with the same compiler, reads every change of offset from 1997 to today that the newest release (2026f, 2026-10-27) records — 8,518 changes in 312 regions — and finds the first release that carried each one, to the second and the offset. The release's date, from the database's own log, against the day the clocks moved, is the notice the world's software had.

2. Late

Most changes are known years ahead: a daylight-saving rule, once made, predicts itself. The median change was first carried 6.2 years before it happened. The tail is the finding. In the exploratory run (all releases from 1996h), 4,285 changes could be timed; 393 first appeared after they had taken effect, and 105 more less than thirty days before. The late ones are of three kinds:

  • 114 were news arriving late: on the day the clocks moved, the database still had the old offset.
  • 51 re-timed a change already carried (the database had the right offset by then, at a slightly different instant).
  • 228 came more than a year late: the record correcting its past, not news. The latest correction reached back 19 years.

3. Fewer late, still short

The late arrivals thin out: 44 news arrivals in 1997-2004, 3 since 2020, none in 2024-2026 so far. Short notice does not thin out in the same way: a change carried only days ahead recurs to the last years (in this reading most often for Gaza and Hebron: eight events). (Exploratory: found after the registered count, explore1b.json, explore1c.json.)

Connects to 2.

4. The check

A second tool (zdump, not this study's reader) looked at twelve late changes drawn at random, in the release before each one's first appearance and in that appearance. It confirmed 8 of 12. In the other four, the earlier release already showed the new offset at that instant: the change had been carried, at a slightly different moment, and the "late" release only moved it. The registered count did not separate these; the exploratory reading in 2 does. (check1.py, check1.json.)

Connects to 2 (the second kind).

5. What this cannot tell

When a government decided, and when a device received a release: neither is in this material. The release date is the day the file was published; a phone has it later, so its notice is shorter than what is measured here. The registered count compiled only the releases from 2000f on: the 61 older ones failed, because their data ask the compiler to run a helper (yearistype, for rules in even and odd years) that today's compiler has dropped. Every change before 2000f's date that the earlier releases had carried therefore looked late; that is why the registered peak year is 2000. The exploratory run supplied a two-line helper written for this study (helper/yearistype) and compiled 252 releases from 1996h on; the 23 still failing are older than the window. Whether the database is right about the world is not tested. Nearest neighbours: NEIGHBOURS.md.

7. The hours shown wrong (session 2)

Session 2 counts in hours instead of changes. On every day since 1996 one release was the newest: the file anyone updating faithfully had. For every region and every hour, that file's offset is set against what the newest release of all (2026f) says the offset was. Where they differ, the file showed the present wrong. In total: 918,348 region-hours in 103 of 312 regions, 1.26 % of all the region-hours the file covered, in 505 stretches of three kinds:

  • 161,743 hours (144 stretches, 78 regions): news that came after the clocks moved; the median stretch lasted 21 days.
  • 16,627 hours (118 stretches): a change the file had, at the wrong moment — within a month of the right one.
  • 739,978 hours (243 stretches, 53 regions): hours that were found wrong more than a year after they were lived — the record rewriting its past.

One row per region, longest total first; each bar a stretch in which the newest release showed that region an hour the record now says was wrong (hover for the release, the two offsets at the stretch's start and the correcting release). The longest: Antarctica/Vostok, 21.4 years from 2001 to 2022, wrong by one hour in all but 552 of its hours: the file showed the station UT+6 h where the record now says +7 h (read with zdump on releases 2010a and 2026f); in its first weeks, release 2001a had put it on Moscow time. Next: Asia/Urumqi and Pacific/Fakaofo. Bars starting at 1997 began earlier; the count starts there.

News arriving late thins out as in section 3: 8.7 % of its hours fall after 2016. The hours found wrong years later do not thin in the same way through 2022: they are where the record learned late what a place had done, often a remote one (an Antarctic station, an atoll, a province). Hours after late 2025 cannot be counted in this kind yet: a year has not passed. (SESSION2.md, count2.py, session2.json, check2.json.) 10 of 11 predictions held:

  • P1 Exactly 252 releases compile with the helper. — held: 252.
  • P2 Zone-hours wrong lie between 200,000 and 5,000,000. — held: 918,348.
  • P3 Hours found wrong more than a year later carry over 70 % of the total. — held: 80.6 %.
  • P4 Re-timed episodes carry fewer hours than news. — held: 16,627 against 161,743.
  • P5 60 to 200 zones have an episode. — held: 103.
  • P6 Hours wrong are 0.1 % to 3 % of exposure. — held: 1.26 %.
  • P7 The median news episode lasts 1 to 30 days. — held: 20.6 days.
  • P8 The longest episode exceeds a year. — held: 21.4 years (Antarctica/Vostok).
  • P9 News hours since 2017 are under 10 % of all news hours. — held: 8.7 %.
  • P10 At least 90 % of hours wrong are wrong by exactly one hour. — failed: 77.3 %; 17.2 % by two hours, and 1,477 hours by twenty-three.
  • P11 The zdump check holds for at least 11 of 12. — held: 11 of 12; in the twelfth zdump returned no reading inside its range (the file has no change after 1969), and read without a range it agrees.
