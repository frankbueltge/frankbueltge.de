How Sure Is a Planet

When two papers measure the same planet, how often do they disagree by more than they said they could?

Every line below is one thing that was measured about a planet by different authors. If every stated error bar were honest, the lines would fall away at the grey curve: a gap of three error bars between two papers should turn up once in about 370 pairs. For the length of a planet’s year it turns up once in about 12. Near one error bar the papers agree about as promised; it is in the tail, where the big surprises live, that they fall short.

What counts. One planet, two different papers, one quantity. The gap is divided by the two papers’ error bars added in quadrature (the average of the stated plus and minus). Pairs where the second paper simply repeats the first paper’s number are counted separately (19 % of period pairs) and left out of the curves, because a copy cannot disagree. The most precise number is not safer than the rest: against the other papers’ own scale, its middle is no reliably nearer or further than any other paper’s (further for periods), and it is less often far off in the tail (session1b.json). Why the tails are heavy is not in the data: shared stars, shifting transit times, and optimistic error bars would each do it, and this page does not choose.

Data: NASA Exoplanet Archive, table of all solutions, queried 2026-10-07 (40,194 rows, 6,375 planets).
