How Sure Is a Planet

When two papers measure the same planet, how often do they disagree by more than they said they could?

Every line below is one thing that was measured about a planet by different authors. If every stated error bar were honest, the lines would fall away at the grey curve: a gap of three error bars between two papers should turn up once in about 370 pairs. For the length of a planet’s year it turns up once in about 12. Near one error bar the papers agree about as promised; it is in the tail, where the big surprises live, that they fall short.

What counts. One planet, two different papers, one quantity. The gap is divided by the two papers’ error bars added in quadrature (the average of the stated plus and minus). Pairs where the second paper simply repeats the first paper’s number are counted separately (19 % of period pairs) and left out of the curves, because a copy cannot disagree. The most precise number is not safer than the rest: against the other papers’ own scale, its middle is no reliably nearer or further than any other paper’s (further for periods), and it is less often far off in the tail (session1b.json). Why the tails are heavy is not in the data: shared stars, shifting transit times, and optimistic error bars would each do it, and this page does not choose.

Session 2: where the shortfall sits

The disagreement is not spread evenly. Split by the year of the later of the two papers, the share of pairs beyond three error bars is not falling as instruments improve — for periods it is highest for pairs whose later paper is from 2020 on. Across the whole table the shortfall is also a matter of which planets were measured often: planets with eleven or more papers carry about half of the period tail, a good part of that only because they have many pairs.

What this does not show. Neighbouring planets of one star share a tail more than chance: for radius, a planet whose sibling has a tail pair has one itself 10 times as often as one whose siblings have none. The obvious reading is the shared star. But when the tail pairs of two siblings are looked at pair by pair, they come from the same two papers less often than ordinary pairs do (32 % of radius tail pairs against 55 % of all pairs), so the data do not show it; well-observed systems may be the whole story. The later paper states the smaller error in 72 % of the large disagreements, against 67 % of all pairs: the sharper paper is usually the later one, and that alone explains most of it.

Session 3: is the rise a property of recent papers?

The rise for 2020 on is real in the table but it is not the whole story. The planets whose period was found by transit carry it (15.2 % for 2020 on against 6.8 % for 2015–2019); planets found by radial velocity disagree about as often in every era (17.0 %, 14.9 %, 14.5 % by era), and at a higher level than transit planets for most of the record. The long middle band, 2015–2019, is almost all transit pairs and is the low one. Disagreement also grows with distance in time: 7.0 % of pairs whose papers are a year apart or less, 14.0 % when they are five years or more apart. It does not depend on a few much-measured planets (planets with ten papers or fewer: 13.6 % for 2020 on against 5.3 %). A much sharper later paper is only a little more often in the tail (10.1 % against 6.7 %), and a much less sharp later paper is more often in it than either (13.0 %): the simple story that new, better measurements leave the old interval is not what the table shows.
