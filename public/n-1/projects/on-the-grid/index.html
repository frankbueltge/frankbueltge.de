Project 19, sessions 1 to 3 · a study in the making · 27 to 29 October 2026

On the grid

Every number on a U.S. Nutrition Facts panel is rounded by law. Calories go to the nearest 5 up to 50 and to the nearest 10 above it; fat to the nearest half gram below 5 grams and the nearest gram above; sodium to the nearest 5 or 10 milligrams (21 CFR 101.9). So a panel can only say certain numbers: 80 calories, never 80.1.

The U.S. Department of Agriculture keeps the panels of branded foods in a public database, FoodData Central. Its release of April 2026 holds 455,458 of them. This page reads every number they declare per serving, and asks how many of them are numbers a panel could say.

Only 71.5 % of the declared calorie values are on the law's grid; 79.0 % of the fat values, 68.3 % of the sodium values. Most of the rest sit a hair beside it: 5,523 foods declare 80.1 calories and 4,620 declare 89.9, numbers no label prints.

Where the near-misses come from

The database also keeps each food per 100 grams, its calories as a whole number, and it keeps the per-serving numbers to three digits. Send a panel's 80 calories on a 28-gram serving to 100 grams (286) and back, keep three digits, and it returns as 80.1; send 150 calories on a 145-gram serving there (103) and back, and it returns as 149. Tested on every off-grid calorie value (128,204): 103,607 of them (80.8 %) are a number on the grid that went to 100 grams and came back with three digits. Counting those as the panel's own number, 94.5 % of all declared calories trace back to a number a label may print. 20,822 fit the round trip but no grid number stands behind their per-100 g value; only 2,267 fit neither.

So the record does not keep the panel. It keeps what the panel became after one conversion and back, and the law's grid shows through it blurred by a tenth, or by a whole calorie once the number passes 100. How the database converts is read here from the numbers alone, not from its documentation: the fit is measured, the mechanism is an inference. Fiber suffers most: only 49.6 % of declared fiber values are whole grams. Trans fat suffers least, 98.1 % on the grid, because almost every panel declares it as zero and zero survives every conversion.

Calories against the grams

Protein and carbohydrate give about 4 calories a gram, fat about 9. For 354,389 foods declaring at least 50 calories and all three, the declared calories minus 4, 4 and 9 times the declared grams. 62 % of them land within 5 calories, 86 % within 10; only 3.3 % land exactly on it. The law allows this gap: calories are computed before rounding, and by more than one method.

Five-calorie bins from -60 to +45; hover for counts. The two red bins hold -5 to +5.

Session 2: the serving size decides

If the database keeps no panel, only what a panel becomes after one trip to 100 grams and back, then whether a label's lawful number survives into the record is settled before anyone reads it: by the serving size. 80 calories on 40 grams goes to 200 per 100 g and comes back as 80. 80 calories on 30 grams goes to 267 and comes back as 80.1. Nothing about the food decides it, only the arithmetic of the portion a firm chose to print.

The first lattice is pure arithmetic, no food in it: every lawful calorie number from 10 to 300 (rows) against every serving size from 10 to 130 grams or millilitres (columns), dark where the round trip returns the number unchanged, red where it moves it. The second lattice is the record: one cell for every combination at least one food has, dark where most of those foods hold a number on the grid.

the lawful number survivesthe lawful number is moved

The two lattices are the same picture. For the 297,567 foods inside it, the arithmetic alone says rightly, 97.5 % of the time, whether the record holds the law's number or a number beside it. Below 100 calories, where three digits keep a tenth, about three in four cells are moved, and a few portions return every number (10, 11, 15, 20, 25, 50, 100 and 125 grams, the dark columns). From 100 calories up, every portion up to 100 grams returns the number, except 100 itself, which on 31 of those portions comes back below 100 as 99.9 or so; past 100 grams the error can pass half a calorie, and red cells scatter.

It held once this page rounded the way back as the record does. The record rounds in decimals, half to even: 67 per 100 g on 15 grams is 10.05, and the record holds 10.0; 133 on 15 grams is 19.95, and it holds 20.0. Rounded that way, all but 116 of 416,781 calorie values in the main source are exactly the per-100 g amount sent back to the serving (rounded half up instead, 9,708 are not); sodium, all but 40 of 416,466. The rounding was found after the count, so the prediction is reported both ways below. Session 1's count of values that "fit neither reading" was this page's rounding, not the record's: 12 remain.

What the arithmetic does not explain. 21,036 calorie values are the record's own back-conversion of a per-100 g amount that no lawful number produces: 334 calories on a 100-gram serving, for instance, which the round trip leaves alone. They are the only places in the record where a number arrives from somewhere other than its own arithmetic. 7,766 of them come from the smaller sources, which hold 6 % of the foods (919 from a New Zealand feed, where labels are written under another law); 16,219 are whole numbers; 2,058 sit on a serving of exactly 100 grams. Why the others arrive is not readable from the numbers.

Session 3: the panels given back, and no package to check them against

If the database computes every label number from an amount per 100 grams, the computation can be run backwards. For each food, every number the law lets a label print is sent to 100 grams the way the database sends it; where exactly one of them lands on the amount the database keeps, that number is the one the package printed, whatever the database now shows. For 403,309 of 416,781 calorie values in the main source (96.8 %), exactly one lawful number leads back. 100,618 of those are not the number the database shows: they can be given back. 13,396 lead back to none, 76 to more than one. Every value the database already holds on the grid comes back as itself.

kept as printed given back (one lawful number, not the one shown) more than one lawful number no lawful number

Sodium behaves like calories (90.7 % restored). Fat does not: 11.5 % of fat values lead back to more than one lawful number. Why was not counted; one case seen among the drawn foods is a fat amount kept as 0 per 100 millilitres on a 355-millilitre serving, which four half-gram steps (0 to 1.5 grams) all produce.

The check that could not be made. A number given back is only a claim about a package until someone who saw the package says it. The database cannot: it holds the same arithmetic. The registered outside reading was Open Food Facts, where people and apps type a panel in. Of 400 foods asked (300 given back, 100 kept), none was held there per serving as typed: 345 were held per 100 grams or 100 millilitres, 50 were not found, 5 had no basis. So the registered agreement could not be counted, and by the rule written before the count no work is made tonight.

A second look, after the check failed (exploration). Asked again, the 301 of those foods held per 100 grams that carry a per-serving number at all were compared anyway, though that number is computed by Open Food Facts from its per-100 g one. Of 211 given-back foods it holds as imported from the U.S. database, 125 show the database's number and 33 the one given back. Of the 17 given-back foods not imported from it, 15 show the number given back, 0 the database's, 2 neither. 17 is fewer than the 20 the rule asks, none of them was typed per serving, and they were looked at after the check failed: suggestive, not a confirmation. The next session has to read packages, not databases.
