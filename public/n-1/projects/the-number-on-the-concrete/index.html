The number on the concrete

A runway is named by the direction it points, measured by compass: a runway pointing 087° magnetic is painted 09. The concrete does not move. The earth's magnetic field does. Read against the field of each year since 1900, the numbers of 12,178 runways fit the field of 2002 best — not today's.

Sections can be read in any order; each says which others it connects to.

1. The rule and the field

The number is the magnetic heading divided by ten and rounded; the two ends of a runway differ by 18 (09 and 27). The heading is measured from magnetic north, which stands apart from true north by the declination, and declination drifts: in places by tenths of a degree a year. When it has moved far enough, an airport repaints its numbers, closes the runway for a night, and the runway becomes, by notice, another runway; nothing else about it changes. This study reads the world's runways in the open, crowd-kept OurAirports database against the International Geomagnetic Reference Field (IGRF-14), year by year from 1900 to 2026.

Connects to 2 (how many have fallen behind) and 4 (the date in the numbers).

2. How many have fallen behind

Of 12,178 runways long enough for their direction to be read from their two ends (48,388 in the file), 72 % carry the number today's field gives them; 3,417 (28 %) do not. The median runway points 3.2° away from the heading its number names; one in ten more than 7.5°.

Connects to 3 (in which direction) and 6 (what this cannot tell).

3. They trail the field

A number left behind could be off in either direction. It is not. Of the out-of-step numbers, 93 % stand off on the side the field moved away from since 1996 — 96 % where the field moved 1.5° or more. Where the field has hardly moved (under 0.5°), the few out-of-step numbers (145) split 62 % to 38 %, close to chance: there, a wrong number is a mistake or a choice, not a lag. Where the field moves fastest the numbers fall behind fastest: 45 % out of step against 18 % where it is still.

Connects to 4, where the lag becomes a year.

4. The date in the numbers

For every year since 1900 the field can be computed, and each runway's number checked against that year's field. Taken together, the numbers are right most often for the field of 2002 (80.6 %), against 71.9 % for today's and 49.3 % for 1900's (figure at the top). 5. North itself

Near the magnetic pole a compass is little use, and in Canada's northern airspace runways are numbered by true north, a T after the number. 26 such runways are long enough to read; 23 are in Canada and 24 point within 5° of their number. They do not drift, and are not counted above.

Connects to 1.

6. What this cannot tell

Whether a number in this database is the number painted today. OurAirports is kept by its users; a runway repainted last year and not yet edited reads as out of step, so the lag in section 4 is the paint's and the record's together. 354 runways (2.9 %) fit no field since 1900; most stand 5 to 15° off, where a parallel runway numbered apart, a local magnetic anomaly the global model does not hold, or an error in the record could each put them. The ends' coordinates are good: where the record also gives a true heading, it agrees with them to a median of 0.24°. The field this study computes agrees with the British Geological Survey's IGRF-14 service to 0.001° at 20 points.

Connects to 2, 3, 4.
