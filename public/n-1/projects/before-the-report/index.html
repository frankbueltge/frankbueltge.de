Before the Report

When a firm calls back a food, a drug or a medical device, the FDA classes the danger and lists the recall in its weekly enforcement report. Across 88,310 listed products since 2012, the list comes a median 50 days after the recall began. For drugs, the gravest recalls — Class I, products that “predictably could cause serious health problems or death” — wait longer than the Class II ones, in every one of the fifteen years.

1. What is read

The FDA publishes every enforcement report entry since June 2012 as three open files, one each for food, drugs and devices. Each entry is one product in a recall and carries three dates: the day the firm began notifying the public or its customers, the day the FDA classified the hazard, and the day the report listed it. This study counts the days between them, for every entry. A recall event (one firm, one problem, often many products) is counted once, at its earliest dates and its gravest class.

For food the three classes wait alike (the gravest was the longer in 5 of 15 years); for devices the gravest is usually the quicker (3 of 15). For drugs Class I was the longer in 15 of 15. The whole difference lies before the FDA's classification: once a recall is classed, every centre and every class is listed a median 7 or 8 days later — the weekly rhythm of the report. Between 2013 and 2016 the gravest drug recalls waited a median of 118 to 149 days; since 2018 the gap is smaller (26 to 45.5 days against 22 to 27.5) but has not closed.

One drug recall shows why products are the wrong unit. A single event of 465 homeopathic products, recalled for microbial contamination, began on 20 July 2018 and was listed on 20 March 2019, 243 days later; it alone is a quarter of all Class I drug entries. Its first notice was a press release. So the wait measured here is the register's wait, not the public's: a firm may have told the public long before the list did. How often the register is the first public word — and how long those recalls wait — is section 5.

The register also holds dates that cannot be true: a food recall begun in the year 212 (F-0880-2013, a wait of 657,484 days) and a device recall begun in 1930 (Z-0139-2014). Six device entries were listed before they began. They are counted as found and move no median.

4. Who is affected

People who ate, took or were treated with a recalled product, and the public that reads of recalls in the FDA's report. What waits is the public record of a danger the firm has already acknowledged.

5. Whose word came first

Session 2 · 19 November 2026 · files of 18 November 2026, the same 88,310 entries.

Each entry also says how the firm first made the recall known. Of 27,896 recall events, 958 (3.4 %) began with a press release. In 21,848 (78.3 %) the first notice went only to those who held or passed on the product — by letter, telephone, e-mail, visit or fax. For these the register may have been the first public word, and it came a median 47 days after the recall began; 856 waited more than a year (two of them the impossible start dates of section 2). 4,571 events cannot say: their entries carry a form's own sentence, “Two or more of the following: Email, Fax, Letter, Press Release, Telephone, Visit”, which lists every method and marks none, or the word “Other”. In food that is 3,164 of 7,955 classed events. 519 record no method.

The gravest recalls are the most often announced: 210 of 659 Class I drug recalls began with a press release, against 4.3 % of Class II. But announcing does not hurry the register. Class I drug recalls first announced to the public were listed a median 61.5 days after they began; those first told to customers only, 50. And where the register may be the first public word, the gravest drug recalls still wait longer than the lesser ones — 50 days against 28, and in 15 of the 15 report years (not registered: from 236.5 against 83 days in 2012 to 33 against 22 in 2026). The longest: a Class I drug recall first made known by telephone and listed 2,455 days later (event 35255, reported 2012).

What this cannot say. The field holds only the first method. A firm may have announced the recall later; and for devices the FDA has issued “Early Alerts” for likely Class I recalls since a pilot begun in November 2024, which a law firm's review puts at 1 to 3 weeks after the first customer letters (FDA Law Blog, 1 October 2025). Neither shows in this register. The customers-only count is therefore an upper bound on the recalls for which the register was the first public word.
