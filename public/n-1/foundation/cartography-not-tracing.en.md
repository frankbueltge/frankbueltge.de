## 1. Introduction

Artistic research has a method problem that more method will not solve. Since its Bologna-driven institutionalisation in doctoral programmes, funding lines and publication formats, it has stood under the double pressure of, on the one hand, being recognisable as research — that is, of showing procedures, quality criteria and result-forms — and, on the other, of not surrendering precisely what distinguishes it from scientific research: the sensuous, singular, non-propositional constitution of its knowing. Conceptually, this field has been surveyed since Christopher Frayling's distinction of research *into*, *through* and *for* art and design (Frayling 1993, p. 5) and Henk Borgdorff's triad of research *on*, *for* and *in* the arts (Borgdorff 2012, pp. 36–38); the performative variant, "research in the arts", in which art production is itself a fundamental part of the research process, counts as the most contested. Julian Klein productively displaced the definitional question early: not *what* artistic research is, but *when* research is artistic — namely when it operates in the "mode of artistic experience" and aims at a "felt knowledge", a felt, embodied knowing (Klein 2010, pp. 1, 3, 6). Against this, Catarina Almeida diagnosed that the permanent debate over the essence and episteme of artistic research keeps the field in a state of "pendency", an institutionalised suspension: two decades of discursive overproduction — "productivitism" — have yielded few verifiable instruments, and it is "no longer acceptable that discussions about the episteme of artistic research are regarded as the core study of artistic research" (Almeida 2015, pp. 159–160, 163). And the *Manifesto of Artistic Research* by Henke, Mersch, van der Meulen, Strässle and Wiesel drew the counter-balance: artistic research submits itself to a "university-academic regime" and thereby gambles away its own potential, which lies precisely in "asserting indisciplinarity, admitting indeterminacy, integrating negativity, and seeking perceptibility" (*"Disziplinlosigkeit zu behaupten, Unbestimmtheit zuzulassen, Negativität zu integrieren und Anschaulichkeit zu suchen"*; Henke/Mersch et al. 2020, pp. 6, 18). The field thus oscillates between an elaborate epistemology of the aesthetic that finds methodology as such suspect, and an institutional reality that demands it.

Into this situation, the present study proposes a candidate for a methodological foundation that is at first sight paradoxical: Gilles Deleuze/Félix Guattari: *A Thousand Plateaus. Capitalism and Schizophrenia*, trans. Brian Massumi, Minneapolis/London 1987 [French *Mille Plateaux*, Paris 1980]; hereafter cited as ATP with page number. *A Thousand Plateaus* is promising because the book — unlike most theory-imports circulating in artistic research — contains not only an ontology or an aesthetics but a spelled-out *pragmatics*: a repertoire of procedures, test-questions, "concrete rules" and precautionary maxims, developed across hundreds of pages against art practices (music, painting, literature, film) and repeatedly formulated as working instruction. It is at the same time risky, because the same book explicitly rejects any methodology understood as a fixed path: "A 'method' is the striated space of the cogitatio universalis" — method is the striated space of State thought, against which D&G set "relays, intermezzos, resurgences" (ATP 377). Whoever distils a toolkit from *A Thousand Plateaus* risks exactly the "tracing" the book criticises (ATP 12–13), and exactly the use the Manifesto holds up against the artistic-research scene: Deleuze is "less received and critiqued than used and exploited like citation quarries" (*"weniger rezipiert und kritisiert, als vielmehr benutzt und wie Zitatsteinbrüche ausgebeutet"*; Henke/Mersch et al. 2020, p. 12).

From this follows the research question of the present study: *Can a methodologically load-bearing model of artistic research, together with a practical toolkit, be won out of* A Thousand Plateaus *without betraying the book's anti-methodical character?* The thesis is: yes — but only if the transposition itself proceeds by the rules the book lays down for handling books; that is, if the toolkit is designed as a map handed to the respective research project for reworking, and not as a copy applied to projects. "Cartography, not Tracing" is in this respect not only the title but the methodological self-commitment of the study.

The course of the inquiry: Chapter 2 develops the transposition problem and grounds the study's own four-step procedure. Chapter 3 reconstructs the state of research in three steps: the methodology debate of artistic research in five lines of position (3.1), the field's Deleuze reception in five modes of reception (3.2), and the resulting research gap, formulated in four stages, together with the demarcation from the closest precursors (3.3). Chapter 4 explicates seven operative concept complexes from *A Thousand Plateaus* close to the text and page-exactly. Chapter 5 synthesises from them a process grammar of artistic research in six postulates, together with a determination of problem-finding; Chapter 6 translates it into eight concrete instruments, gives each a testable failure criterion, regulates the economy of their use, and fixes a trial protocol in advance. Chapter 7 distils quality criteria; Chapter 8 discusses the limits and dangers of the undertaking in seven problem zones — including those Deleuze and Guattari themselves named, and two objections this study raises against itself. Chapter 9 draws the conclusion. What would make the undertaking fail as a whole is laid down in advance in Chapter 2. Throughout, the line between textual finding and the study's own extrapolation is explicitly marked: what counts as a "textual finding" is documented with page references; where the study goes beyond this, it is flagged as a proposal.

## 2. The Transposition Problem: How to Win Tools from an Anti-Method

The undertaking of winning a toolkit from *A Thousand Plateaus* must begin with an objection the book itself formulates most sharply. In the rhizome plateau, Deleuze and Guattari distinguish the map from the copy, or tracing (map/tracing): tree-and-root logic is copying logic, "All of tree logic is a logic of tracing and reproduction" (ATP 12), and the tracing is dangerous because it injects redundancies and propagates them, because it turns the rhizome back into roots and in the end "only reproduces itself" (ATP 13). The imperative accordingly is: "Make a map, not a tracing" (ATP 12). A toolkit, however, looks like the epitome of a tracing: a repeatable template detached from the living text and applicable to arbitrary cases. Whoever transfers the book's concepts into analysis sheets and test-questions seems to do exactly what D&G demonstrate in Freud's treatment of little Hans: to break the rhizome of a process and to "blotch" its map (ATP 14). The transposition problem is thus not an external reception problem but one the text itself poses.

The answer of this study rests on three book-internal findings that not only permit but recommend a tool-reading.

*First,* the book defines itself as a tool. Its opening pages dismiss the hermeneutic question of meaning in favour of the pragmatic question of functioning: "We will never ask what a book means, as signified or signifier; […] We will ask what it functions with, in connection with what other things it does or does not transmit intensities" (ATP 4). A book exists "only through the outside and on the outside. A book itself is a little machine" (ATP 4) — a little machine whose value is measured by which other machines it can be wired to. And: "There is no difference between what a book talks about and how it is made" (ATP 4). The textual finding is thus: a reading that asks *A Thousand Plateaus* what it functions with — here: with the practices of artistic research — follows the book's own instructions for use more closely than one that comments on its meaning. Wiring the book to a field outside it is not misuse but its declared mode of existence.

*Second,* the pragmatics in *A Thousand Plateaus* is no vague ethos but a spelled-out procedure. At the close of the regime-of-signs plateau it is defined as a four-component analysis — generative, transformational, diagrammatic and machinic components (ATP 145–146) — and translated into a sequence of four operations: tracing the mixed semiotics, mapping the transformations, diagramming the abstract machines, outlining the programmes of the assemblages (ATP 146–147). It is remarkable that the "tracing" devalued in plateau 1 recurs here as the first, legitimate sub-operation: the map grows "along the lines of the tracings" (ATP 146–147). The copy is therefore not forbidden but subordinated to an asymmetrical ordering rule already stated in plateau 1: "It is a question of method: the tracing should always be put back on the map" (ATP 13). The subsequent analysis protocol — the proposition "I love you" run through four regimes of signs, through translation tests and the invention of as-yet-unknown statements (ATP 147–148) — is in substance already a toolkit component, performed by the text itself.

*Third,* the closing chapter, plateau 15, is an explicit rulebook. Its title is "Conclusion: Concrete Rules and Abstract Machines", and it keeps what the title promises: "The first concrete rule for assemblages is to discover what territoriality they envelop, for there always is one" (ATP 503–504); "The concrete rules of assemblage thus operate along these two axes: On the one hand, what is the territoriality of the assemblage, what is the regime of signs and the pragmatic system? On the other hand, what are the cutting edges of deterritorialization, and what abstract machines do they effectuate?" (ATP 505). For the construction of the plane of consistency, selective construction rules hold (ATP 508); for the distinction of the forms of deterritorialization: "There are thus at least four forms of D that confront and combine, and must be distinguished from one another following concrete rules" (ATP 510). Even a rule of caution is marked out as such: "Every undertaking of destratification […] must therefore observe concrete rules of extreme caution" (ATP 503). The textual finding is thus: *A Thousand Plateaus* already contains a rulebook of itself; the closing chapter systematises all the book's main concepts in rule form and in doing so — through marginal numbers that refer back to the individual plateaus — practises the rhizomatic cross-connection it describes. A toolkit makes this rule-form explicit; it does not invent it.

At the same time, D&G mark the limits of any such systematisation. They expressly claim no scientific status for their concepts: "Nowhere do we claim for our concepts the title of a science" (ATP 22); and they recommend the precarious frame precisely in theory: "even in the realm of theory, especially in the realm of theory, any precarious and pragmatic framework is better than tracing concepts" (ATP 24). Rules in this book have the status of "optional rules" — the language theory of the plateaus consistently thinks systems in terms of "variable or optional rules", not of obligatory constants (ATP 93–94, 103). A toolkit won from ATP must inherit this status: it can only be an optional, revisable rulebook to be proven case by case, never a compulsory one.

From these findings follows the procedure of the present study, in four steps. (a) *Concept explication on the text:* each concept complex is reconstructed philologically, with page-exact references to the Massumi edition — including the warnings and cautions D&G attach to their concepts. (b) *Operative distillation:* for each concept the question is what it *does* — which distinctions it enables, which operations it implies, which imperatives the text itself ties to it. This question is covered by the book's function-question (ATP 4). (c) *Transposition into research operations:* the distilled operations are translated into procedures of artistic research — mapping protocols, analysis grids, journal forms, audits. Here the study leaves the textual finding behind; every transposition is therefore marked as "textual finding" or "extrapolation". (d) *Testing against three criteria:* closeness to the text (does the transposition distort the concept?), connectability to the discourse of artistic research (does it solve a problem formulated there?), and practicability (can it be carried out in a real project without specialist philosophy?).

Finally, the procedure includes stating what its failure would be recognisable by — a procedure that cannot be made to fail would be no procedure at all. Three criteria, named in advance and translated into a trial protocol at the end of Chapter 6. (i) The *relabeling test:* if in real projects the instruments merely rename what would happen anyway — if, that is, no documented case can be produced in which an instrument changed a working decision — then the toolkit is exactly the jargon the SHARE Handbook mocks in the "rhizomatic project description" (Wilson/van Ruiten 2013, p. 278), and it is to be struck, not defended. (ii) *Performative self-refutation:* if the use of the instruments systematically produces the copies they warn against — the map as ornament for the grant application, the journal as alibi — the transposition has violated its own building instruction (ATP 13). (iii) The *difference test:* if projects working with the toolkit show, measured against the criteria of Chapter 7, no describable difference from comparable projects without the toolkit, it is without consequence. This at the same time marks the limit of this study itself: its third test criterion, practicability, can only be made constructively plausible in the text — through procedural steps and constructed examples marked as such — not proven. The proof is a matter of the trial, and its absence would be a result, not an operational accident.

A terminological preface is needed at this point. The term "transposition", which this study uses for the transfer of *A Thousand Plateaus* into a research instrumentarium, is already occupied in the field: Michael Schwab's edited volume *Transpositions* (2018) introduces it as an aesthetico-epistemic *operator* of artistic research whose pertinent forms operate "outside registers of representation, resemblance, or mimesis" (Schwab 2018, p. 7). The kinship must be acknowledged: there too, "transposition" means a carrying-over that does not depict but displaces and produces the new. The difference must nevertheless be named. For Schwab and the authors of his volume, transposition is an *artistic practice* of transfer between aesthetic and epistemic registers — an operator within the work and within the conduct of research itself. The usage intended here is, by contrast, a *philological-methodological* procedure of translation: the transfer of the concepts of a philosophical text into the operations of a research design. To disclose the collision, the study's own usage is specified in what follows as *methodological transposition*; where the artistic practice of transposition in Schwab's sense is meant, this is expressly noted.

This procedure is to be distinguished from an "application" of Deleuze to art of the kind the Manifesto rightly criticises: theories are processed into "theory building blocks" (*"Theoriebausteine"*) "that do not even begin to seek to grasp aesthetic thinking" (*"die ästhetisches Denken nicht ansatzweise zu erfassen suchen"*; Henke/Mersch et al. 2020, p. 12). The map/copy distinction supplies the counter-model: the toolkit developed here understands itself as a copy of a book that must, per the rule of ATP 13, be *laid back onto the map of each research project* — it is material each project should deform, cut and rebuild, not a grid under which projects are subsumed. That this self-commitment too can fail, that any diagram can congeal back into a blocking axiomatic (ATP 143), is discussed separately in Chapter 8. The Manifesto's measure — receive and critique Deleuze rather than "use" him (Henke/Mersch et al. 2020, p. 12) — remains the external touchstone throughout: receiving here means expounding page-exactly, critiquing means turning the text's own warning-layers against one's own construction.

## 3. State of Research: Artistic Research between Epistemology and Methodlessness

The state of research is reconstructed in two passes: first the methodology debate of artistic research in five lines of position (3.1), then the field's Deleuze reception in five modes of reception (3.2); from these follows the gap at which this study aims (3.3). Both passes carry the reading status of the sources along with them: where a source could be verified only bibliographically, not in full text, it is reported without page reference (cf. the Editorial Note).

### 3.1 The Methodology Debate in Five Lines

The *founding and definitional line* asks what artistic research is and how it relates to established concepts of research. Its founding document is Christopher Frayling's distinction of research *into*, *through* and *for* art and design (Frayling 1993, p. 5), derived from Herbert Read. It is instructive for the history of reception that in Frayling himself it is not "research through" but "research *for* art" that is the "thorny one" — the research whose thinking is "embodied in the artefact" (p. 5); only the later debate shifted the controversy onto "research in/through the arts". Frayling's egalitarian point, "making art is a practice" just as "doing science is a practice" (p. 4), delivers a classification, but no process theory of research. Henk Borgdorff refined this ordering for the continental debate: his triad of research *on*, *for* and *in* the arts (Borgdorff 2012, pp. 36–38) determines "research in the arts" as "the most controversial of the three ideal types" — the immanent, performative perspective in which art production is itself part of the research process. Methodologically, this path unites "experimentation and participation in practice and the interpretation of that practice" (p. 52). Brad Haseman contributes a third paradigm: *performative research* alongside quantitative and qualitative research, distinguished by the expressive form of its results (Haseman 2006, preprint pp. 5–6). His core sentence radicalises the immanence thesis: the form of expression "not only expresses the research, but in that expression becomes the research itself" (p. 6). The *Vienna Declaration* (2020) marks the institutional close of this line: artistic research is "practice-based, practice-led research in the arts … an epistemic inquiry" (p. 1) and fulfils the Frascati criteria in all respects. With it, the question of legitimation is consolidated — the model question of how such research proceeds in concrete terms remains open.

The second line answers this model question with *methodological pluralism*. Its first-generation reference work, Hannula/Suoranta/Vadén's *Artistic Research*, sets two basic metaphors: the "democracy of experiences" — "no area of experience is in principle outside the critical reach of any other area of experience" (Hannula/Suoranta/Vadén 2005, p. 30) — and, with Feyerabend, the "methodological abundance" of a controlled pluralism, for "artistic research is a way in which experience reflectively changes itself" (p. 36). The prehistory of this line reaches back behind the field: Donald Schön's figure of the *reflective practitioner* — reflection *in* action rather than on it — supplied the praxeological core idea that artistic research radicalises epistemologically (Schön 1983). The pluralism was canonised in two reference anthologies: Barrett/Bolt's *Practice as Research* anchors making-as-research in a materially situated epistemology of practice (Barrett/Bolt 2007; the volume's Deleuzian exception, Vincs's rhizome chapter, is treated in 3.2), and Smith/Dean model, with the "iterative cyclic web", the reciprocal generation of practice-led research and research-led practice (Smith/Dean 2009). Both volumes order the relation of practice and research; a process vocabulary for its enactment they do not provide. Annette Arlander shifts the method question into the practical: "research methods should preferably be developed from working methods, not imposed on an emerging field from the outside" (Arlander 2014, p. 27), with the triad question/method/material and the "rhythm" of alternating "between creativity and critical reflection" (p. 33) as instruments. With its tenth chapter, the SHARE Handbook delivers the most toolkit-shaped material of the debate: a model of "method disclosure" with a deliberately minimal lexicon (Wilson/van Ruiten 2013, p. 275), together with a "Rhetorics of Method" that unmasks the inherited "method doctrine" (Ramus, Descartes, Bacon) as a rhetorical guarantee of knowledge and puts operative coherence in its place: not "this way is guaranteed to work" but "this way works". Robin Nelson's *Practice as Research*, the de facto standard of British doctorates, systematises the "multi-mode inquiry" via the knowledge triad "'know-how' combined with 'know-what' in relation to 'know-that'" (Nelson 2013, Introduction). It is telling that these founding methodologies, despite their structural proximity to the rhizomatic figure of thought (anti-hierarchy, processuality), consistently manage *without* Deleuze.

The third line is the philosophically most demanding; it grounds the claim that artistic research brings forth a knowledge of its own, non-discursive in kind, and thereby tends towards method-scepticism. Its centre is the work of Dieter Mersch, who determines artistic research as work "in the aisthetic" (*"Arbeit im Aisthetischen"*) and as "inquiry into the singular" (*"Er-Forschung des Singulären"*) — "But deviation is the rule in the sensuous" (*"Die Abweichung ist aber die Regel im Sinnlichen"*; Mersch 2015, typescript p. 5) — and has the arts research "zetetically" (*"zetetisch"*), in a probing and self-referential way, as an "indispensable corrective" (*"unerlässliches Korrektiv"*) to the sciences (typescript p. 16). In his more recent balance-sheet, Mersch holds against the debate that it re-imports, "behind our backs, the old hierarchies" (*"hinterrücks die alten Hierarchien"*) from philosophy of science and STS; what is needed is to establish artistic practices "in the first place as a knowledge-practice in their own right" (*"allererst als eine Wissenspraxis eigenen Rechts auszuweisen"*), relating to the sciences as "other" (*"alteritär"*; Mersch 2022, pp. 158–159). The *Manifesto of Artistic Research* radicalises this position: the arts proceed "methodically unmethodically" (*"methodisch unmethodisch"*), in "leaps, side-passages or detours" (*"Sprüngen, Seitengängen oder Umwegen"*; Henke/Mersch et al. 2020, pp. 13, 41), and "the practices of aesthetic thinking can be neither algorithmised nor programmed" (*"die Praktiken ästhetischen Denkens sind weder algorithmisier- noch programmierbar"*; p. 52). Julian Klein supplies the most succinct conversion: not *what* artistic research is, but *when* research is artistic — namely when it operates in the "mode of artistic experience" and aims at a "felt knowledge" (Klein 2010, pp. 3, 6). Sarat Maharaj opposes a "no-how" to the "hard-nosed know-how" and warns against "method fever" and the "institutional captivity of art research" (Maharaj 2009). Closest to an understanding of process comes Coessens/Crispin/Douglas's *The Artistic Turn*, which reframes the knowledge question, rehabilitates *techne* and makes the artistic process itself the "focus and object of the research" (Coessens/Crispin/Douglas 2009, p. 89).

The fourth line is institution-critical. Catarina Almeida locates artistic research "ontologically … within the institution of higher arts education" (Almeida 2015, p. 139) and diagnoses a "pendency", an institutionalised suspension: "After twenty years of generalized sterile literary 'productivitism', a slight difference in discourse is thus regarded with eagerness" (p. 163); it is "no longer acceptable that discussions about the episteme of artistic research are regarded as the core study of artistic research" (pp. 159–160), and a distinction must be drawn "between artistic research and research about artistic research" (p. 167). Dieter Lesage reads the Bologna reform as a conflict over hegemony and warns against the analogy trap of output measurement ("artometrics") as well as against the compulsion to a written supplement: "To impose a medium on the artist is to fail to recognize the artist as an artist" (Lesage 2009, pp. 7–8). The volume *Reclaiming Artistic Research*, edited by Lucy Cotter, calls for winning back artists' agency against academic appropriation; art processes cannot be codified, the search is "intrinsically open-ended" (Cotter 2019, after the review by Joynes 2020). For the present undertaking, this line is the sharpest touchstone: a toolkit that merely prolongs the meta-debate misses its aim; it must be a working instrument for concrete projects.

The fifth line gathers the already existing process models and toolkits — and with them the immediate objects of comparison for this study. The most direct is van der Tuin/Borgen/Lund's "prospective and retrospective toolkit", which seeks to respect the "non-linear and unpredictable" process of artistic research and describes the output formats "diffractively" along seven axes — diffraction as a coinage for "what happens BETWEEN two or more concepts, methods, or formats" (van der Tuin/Borgen/Lund 2021); its theory base is new materialism (Barad/Haraway) and Bolt's performativity, not Deleuze. Vytautas Michelkevičius's *Mapping Artistic Research* proposes "diagrammatic knowing": diagrams as "visual knowledge models" that "not only depict but also create" relations (Michelkevičius 2018, p. 268); theoretically, however, this rests on a single Deleuze passage (the diagram concept from the *Foucault* book, p. 269), not on the abstract machine of the plateaus, and thus threatens to become a visualisation technique in the service of social-scientific presentation. A/r/tography institutionalises the rhizomatic relationality of the identities artist/researcher/teacher most broadly, but remains situated in art education (Arts-Based *Educational* Research; after Marín Viadel/Roldán/Genet 2014). Christopher Willard's bimodal grid determines artistic making as "in most instances ipso facto artistic research" ("Mode One") as against the additionally epistemically justified forms of "Mode Two" (Willard 2015, p. 1) — Wittgensteinian-analytic and expressly without any Deleuze reference. Finally, Barbara Lüneburg adapts Goodman's "worldmaking" into a "creative but controlled and systematic way of doing" (Lüneburg 2023, p. 10) and calls institutionally, for the third cycle, for a framework of criteria together with a "sound toolkit". None of these models wins its instrumentarium from a page-exact evaluation of *A Thousand Plateaus*.

### 3.2 The Deleuze Reception in Artistic Research

Where Deleuze/Guattari appear in artistic research at all, this is by no means comprehensive: in the methodological core literature, any reference to *A Thousand Plateaus* is for the most part entirely absent, and no pertinent text develops a systematic methodology from it. Where Deleuze is received, five modes of reception can be distinguished.

*First, punctual canon citations.* The earliest programmatic enlistment is performed by the third chapter of *The Artistic Turn*, "Deterritorializing the research space": it opposes the rhizome to the tree as a model of knowledge, quotes the motto "Faire la carte, et pas le calque" and reads the refrain (the child in the dark) as a three-step model of the research process, issuing in the imperative "Deterritorialize the research space to provide room for the artist's experience and creations in the world" (Coessens/Crispin/Douglas 2009, p. 177). This enlistment, however, remains metaphorical-programmatic without becoming an elaborated model or toolkit — it is the most important direct precursor and at the same time marks the gap. Similarly early, Kim Vincs applies the rhizome principle to studio research — "any point of a rhizome can be connected to anything other, and must be" (Vincs 2007, pp. 99–100) — without building it out into a procedure. Borgdorff, in turn, calls on Deleuze only as a witness to non-propositionality, who sets "the evocative power of that which is embodied in art" against conceptual knowledge (Borgdorff 2012, p. 48) — as a methodologist he does not appear. And Maharaj's agglutinative logic of the "and+ and+ and+" draws its Deleuzian substance from the cinema books, not from the plateaus (Maharaj 2009).

*Second, explicitly Deleuzian AR models.* Alice Koubová grasps the founding of the field "artistic research" itself as a "plane of consistency" with direct ATP reference — the act of naming produces "this newly viewed plane of consistency (Deleuze, Guattari, 1987, 380)" (Koubová 2017, p. 8) — but leaves it at the framing, without conceptual elaboration. With its *Cahiers of Artistic Research*, the Aveiro school presents an explicit *model*: the criterion is a mythopoetic double movement — research is artistic research "when an artistic production generates a new mythopoetic construction critically deconstructing an old one" (Correia/Dalagna et al. 2018, p. 23) — carried by "material thinking" and a Deleuze grounding that runs primarily through *Différence et répétition* and *Francis Bacon*; *Mille plateaux* is cited only punctually (2018, p. 21; Correia/Dalagna 2020, p. 72). At the same time, the school formulates the sharpest critique of methodolatry: "we live in a situation where we have strong and robust means to achieve weak and vague purposes" (2018, p. 7). A Deleuzian process theory of its own is formed by Manning/Massumi's *research-creation*, which sets "enabling constraints" in place of method — Deleuzianly impregnated, but Whiteheadianly grounded and event-based (Manning/Massumi 2014, accessed via the review by Lyubchenko 2015); Mika Elo, in turn, draws out the Guattari line of an "ecologisation" of the concept of research via *Les trois écologies* (Elo 2020, p. 2). As the most comprehensive ATP reception in the corpus, Spencer Roberts's dissertation must be named, which reformulates the legitimation debate with Deleuzian means and replaces transcendent judgment with a jurisprudence model (Roberts 2015, pp. 231–232) — though primarily critical-diagnostic against the sceptics, hardly constructive as a tool for practice.

*Third, the DARE/Orpheus line*, the established research context "Deleuze and Artistic Research". Its most concise clamping-together of ATP and artistic research is achieved by Paulo de Assis, who reconstructs the conceptual history of *agencement* up to the "tetravalence" of the assemblage (ATP 88) (de Assis 2021, p. 16) and wins from it a "general logic of assemblage … not intended as a fully-fledged theory but as a sophisticated elaboration of descriptive and operative principles that allow creative problematisations" (p. 23). Central is the conversion of the question type from "What does it mean?" to "How does it work? How does it move?" — "intensive explorations of qualities (cartography)" instead of "extensive measurements of quantities (mapping)" (pp. 22–23). This appropriation is philologically informed — precisely not a citation quarry — but remains predominantly analytic of works and practices and hardly poses the question of the research process. In the same orbit, Michael Schwab's volume *Transpositions* occupies the term "transposition" as an aesthetico-epistemic *operator* operating "outside registers of representation, resemblance, or mimesis" (Schwab 2018, p. 7); de Assis's own contribution there, "Memories of a Haecceity", connects directly to the haecceity complex of the plateaus and coins the "micro-haecceity" — the most direct approach so far towards a "toolkit" from *A Thousand Plateaus*.

*Fourth, the Deleuze-and-art philology without AR reference*, which supplies the conceptual foundations but does not negotiate artistic research as a field. Anne Sauvagnargues reconstructs Deleuze's "dynamic cartography" as an anti-hermeneutic procedure and shows that Deleuze uses artworks as a "terrain for experimentation and validation" of his concepts (Sauvagnargues 2013, p. 2) — with him, art is philosophy's method laboratory, not the other way round; her operationalisation aims at analysis, not at production. The series edited by Buchanan and Collins explicitly wants to construct a "methodological core" out of schizoanalysis (Buchanan/Collins 2014, p. 1), but remains art criticism and work analysis. Kamini Vellodi reconstructs the diagram as a generative sign that draws "maps of sensation rather than maps of form" (Vellodi 2019, p. 16), combined with the anti-application thesis that concepts are to be deformed and reinvented on the material. Jakub Zdebik supplies the most thorough exegesis of rhizome principles five and six and corrects the "jubilatory" standard reading by the co-dependence of map and tracing (Zdebik 2019, pp. 39–40). And the volume edited by van Tuinen and Zepke uses ATP as a methodology substitute for art *historiography* — with an explicit barb against artistic research (van Tuinen/Zepke 2017, p. 8). Across all these titles the cross-finding holds that "artistic research" is absent from them as a systematic point of reference.

*Fifth, critique and scepticism* towards the use of Deleuze itself. It ranges from the "citation quarry" charge of the Manifesto (Henke/Mersch et al. 2020, p. 12), via the jargon irony of the SHARE Handbook, which cites the "rhizomatic project description" as an example of over-interpreted self-description (Wilson/van Ruiten 2013, p. 278), to van Tuinen/Zepke's verdict that Deleuze's logic of sensation is "a rather old-fashioned plea for medium specificity" and precisely not research-based practice (2017, p. 8), and Attiwill's warning that Deleuze's ideas "cannot be simply applied to practice" (Attiwill et al. 2017, p. 88). For the present study these objections are not a marginal note but a condition of construction; Chapter 8 takes them up separately.

### 3.3 The Research Gap

The gap at which this study aims is not the thesis that nobody uses *A Thousand Plateaus* for artistic research — after 3.2, that claim would be demonstrably false. What is defensible is a more cautious, four-stage formulation.

*(i) ATP is cited punctually.* Individual concepts or passages — rhizome, map/tracing, refrain, the "and+ and+ and+" — function as figures of legitimation, framing or inspiration (Borgdorff 2012; Vincs 2007; Coessens/Crispin/Douglas 2009; Maharaj 2009; Wilson/van Ruiten 2013), not as an elaborated procedure.

*(ii) ATP is occasionally used for model-building.* Some works go beyond the citation — Koubová (the field as "plane of consistency"), Correia/Dalagna, de Assis (tetravalence as "descriptive and operative principles"), Manning/Massumi, Elo and Roberts — yet none of these model-buildings is at once page-exact, related to the book as a whole and directed at the research process.

*(iii) Nowhere is there a systematic transfer of ATP into a process model together with an instrumentarium of artistic research — one that evaluates the whole book and is explicated page-exactly.* Precisely the plateaus most productive for a research procedure — the pragmatics of the regime-of-signs plateau, the typology of lines of flight, nomad science and the war machine, the body without organs as a form of exercise — remain largely untapped in the existing literature.

*(iv) No existing work at the same time takes the anti-method objections seriously as a condition of construction.* The ATP-near approaches react to the critique assembled in 3.2 either not at all (Coessens/Crispin/Douglas; Correia/Dalagna), by renouncing systematics (Attiwill et al.; Maharaj), or by restricting themselves to analysis instead of procedure (de Assis; Sauvagnargues; Vellodi). A position that treats the objections as the *building instruction* of a toolkit — operators instead of rules, cartography instead of copying, repeatable operators for unrepeatable assemblages — does not exist in the field surveyed.

The study can thus be demarcated sharply from its three closest precursors. Against *de Assis* and the DARE line, it shifts the object: de Assis analyses works and practices with the logic of assemblage, whereas here a process model of researching — problem-finding, procedure, exposition, assessment — is built, the whole book (not only *agencement* and haecceity) is evaluated page-exactly, and this is wired to Mersch's question of the aisthetic of knowledge, which is almost absent in de Assis. Against *Correia/Dalagna*, it inverts the textual relation — ATP as base text instead of a single reference alongside *Différence et répétition* and *Francis Bacon* — and presents an instrumentarium for the conduct of research where the Aveiro model remains a model of criteria. And against *van der Tuin/Borgen/Lund*, it exchanges the theory base: their diffractive toolkit rests on Barad/Haraway and Bolt's performativity, not on Deleuze, and orders output formats instead of modelling the process of generation. In this threefold demarcation lies the contribution of the study: to explicate the operative layer of *A Thousand Plateaus* philologically (against the citation quarry), to condense it into a process model (against the meta-debate), and to transfer it into a toolkit that does not refute the non-propositional epistemology of the aesthetic but underpins it with a relational, handleable vocabulary.

## 4. Explication: Seven Operative Concept Complexes from *A Thousand Plateaus*

The following seven subsections explicate the concept complexes from which the model (ch. 5) and the toolkit (ch. 6) are won. Each follows the same three-step: what does the text say, what does the concept do, and which warning does the text attach to it.

### 4.1 Rhizome and Map

The rhizome is not an image for networkedness but a principle of construction with explicitly named characteristics — D&G frame them cautiously as "certain approximate characteristics" (ATP 7). Principles one and two, connection and heterogeneity, state: "any point of a rhizome can be connected to anything other, and must be" (ATP 7) — unlike the tree, which plots a point and fixes an order; the concatenations here connect heterogeneous registers, "semiotic chains, organizations of power, and circumstances relative to the arts, sciences, and social struggles" (ATP 7). The third principle, multiplicity, withdraws the many from any reference to the One: a multiplicity has "neither subject nor object, only determinations, magnitudes, and dimensions that cannot increase in number without the multiplicity changing in nature" (ATP 8); in the rhizome there are no points or positions, "only lines" (ATP 8). The fourth principle, asignifying rupture, holds that a rhizome may be broken at any spot and starts up again on old or new lines (ATP 9) — every rhizome containing both: lines of segmentarity according to which it is stratified, and lines of deterritorialization along which it flees (ATP 9). The paradigm is wasp and orchid: no imitating, but "a capture of code, […] a veritable becoming" (ATP 10).

Principles five and six — cartography and decalcomania — contain the distinction central to this paper. The rhizome is "a map and not a tracing" (ATP 12): the map is "entirely oriented toward an experimentation in contact with the real" (ATP 12); it does not reproduce a closed unconscious, it *constructs* it (ATP 12); it is "open and connectable in all of its dimensions; it is detachable, reversible, susceptible to constant modification", can be reworked by individuals, groups and social formations, and can expressly also be "conceived of as a work of art" (ATP 12). The map belongs to performance; the tracing invokes an alleged competence (ATP 12–13). Operatively, two things follow: the asymmetrical rule of return — always put the tracings back on the map (ATP 13–14) — and the construction imperative of n − 1: "Subtract the unique from the multiplicity to be constituted; write at n - 1 dimensions" (ATP 6). The positive application example is supplied by Fernand Deligny's maps of the gestures and movements of autistic children (ATP 14); the negative one by Freud's handling of little Hans, whose "rhizome is broken and map blotched" (*"das Rhizom gebrochen und die Karte bekleckst"*; ATP 14).

Added to this is the concept of the plateau, taken over from Bateson: a "continuous, self-vibrating region of intensities whose development avoids any orientation toward a culmination point or external end" (ATP 22). A plateau is "always in the middle, not at the beginning or the end" (ATP 21–22), and the book itself is built that way: "Each plateau can be read starting anywhere and can be related to any other plateau" (ATP 22). The quality criterion that carries this principle of form is formulated by plateau 15: what is preserved, created, consistent is "only that which increases the number of connections at each level of division or composition" (ATP 508). The textual finding is thus a complete testing grid for forms of knowledge: connectivity instead of hierarchy, lines instead of positions, map instead of tracing, middle instead of beginning and end, increase of connections instead of unity. The warning given along with it: rhizomes have "their own, even more rigid, despotism and hierarchy" (ATP 20) — the rhizome is no guarantor of freedom.

### 4.2 Assemblage and Pragmatics

The assemblage (agencement) is the book's unit of analysis. Its canonical determination stands in the linguistics plateau: on a first, horizontal axis an assemblage comprises two segments — it is "machinic assemblage of bodies, of actions and passions, an intermingling of bodies reacting to one another" and at the same time "collective assemblage of enunciation, of acts and statements, of incorporeal transformations attributed to bodies"; on a second, vertical axis it has "territorial sides, or reterritorialized sides, which stabilize it, and cutting edges of deterritorialization, which carry it away" (ATP 88). Hence the formula: "The assemblage is tetravalent" (ATP 505, carried out on the feudal assemblage at ATP 89: interminglings of the bodies of earth, overlord, knight, horse and stirrup; statements, coats of arms, oaths; feudal territorializations; the deterritorialization line of the Crusades). Important is the anti-reduction: the assemblage has "neither base nor superstructure" (ATP 90) — discourse does not explain the bodies, nor the reverse.

The expression side is determined via the order-word (mot d'ordre): "The elementary unit of language—the statement—is the order-word" (ATP 76); language does not primarily inform, it orders — "Language is made not to be believed but to be obeyed" (ATP 76). What statements effect are incorporeal transformations: the judge's sentence transforms the accused instantaneously into a convict (ATP 80–81); the transformation of the passengers into hostages in a hijacking is "an instantaneous incorporeal transformation" (ATP 81), datable "to the hour, minute, and second" (ATP 81). The circumstances are constitutive: "I swear" is "not the same statement" in the family, at school and in court (ATP 82). Beneath the order-words, however, lie "pass-words […] components of passage" (ATP 110) — the task is "to transform the compositions of order into components of passage" (ATP 110).

Pragmatics is the corresponding procedure: four components — generative, transformational, diagrammatic, machinic (ATP 145–146) — whose closing sentence settles the question of rank: "pragmatics is not a complement to logic, syntax, or semantics; on the contrary, it is the fundamental element upon which all the rest depend" (ATP 148). Its centrepiece is continuous variation: every system is "in variation" and defines itself not by constants but by an immanent, continuous, regulated variability (ATP 93–94); "Constant is not opposed to variable; it is a treatment of the variable" (ATP 103). On this hangs the minor usage: "'Major' and 'minor' do not qualify two different languages but rather two usages or functions of language" (ATP 104); the procedure runs "Subtract and place in variation, remove and place in variation: a single operation" (ATP 104), and its literary model is Kafka's Prague German: "Minor authors are foreigners in their own tongue" (ATP 105). Style, accordingly, is not a psychological expression but "nothing other than the procedure of a continuous variation" (ATP 97), to be attained "only by sobriety, creative subtraction" (ATP 98–99). The text's warning: minorisation is not niche-building — "It is a question not of reterritorializing oneself on a dialect or a patois but of deterritorializing the major language" (ATP 104).

### 4.3 Minor Science

The nomadology plateau contains the book's densest passage in terms of the theory of science: the distinction between royal and nomad, or minor, science (ATP 361–374). Nomad science has four characteristics: a hydraulic model in which flow is reality itself (ATP 361); a model of becoming and heterogeneity instead of the stable and identical (ATP 361); spreading out in the open space of the vortex instead of parcelling out a closed space (ATP 361–362); and above all: it is "problematic, rather than theorematic" — one does not proceed deductively "from a stable essence to the properties deriving from it, but rather from a problem to the accidents that condition and resolve it" (ATP 362). Figures here designate "an 'event' much more than an essence; the square no longer exists independently of a quadrature" (ATP 362). The relation between the two sciences is one of repression and appropriation: "State science retains of nomad science only what it can appropriate; it turns the rest into a set of strictly limited formulas without any real scientific status, or else simply represses and bans it" (ATP 362).

Methodologically central is the distinction between two procedures: reproducing and following. "Reproducing implies the permanence of a fixed point of view that is external to what is reproduced: watching the flow from the bank" (ATP 372). Following, by contrast: "One is obliged to follow when one is in search of the 'singularities' of a matter, or rather of a material, and not out to discover a form" (ATP 372) — with D&G expressly noting: "Not better, just different" (ATP 372). Bound up with this is the critique of hylomorphism: royal science is inseparable from a model that presupposes an organising form and a prepared matter — a schema that derives less from technology than from a society divided into governors and governed (ATP 369). The counter-position is a thinking in material: matter is "essentially laden with singularities" (ATP 369); the matter-flow "can only be followed" (ATP 409); what is at stake is a "surrendering to the wood" — yielding to the wood and following it, that is, connecting operations to a materiality instead of imprinting a form upon a matter (ATP 408). The historical model is the Gothic masons' lodge: "The vault is no longer a form but the line of continuous variation of the stones"; "One does not represent, one engenders and traverses" (ATP 364). The concepts of this science are anexact: "vague, in other words, vagabond or nomadic, morphological essences […] anexact yet rigorous" (ATP 367) — anexactness is not a deficiency but "the exact passage of that which is under way" (ATP 20).

This is flanked by noology, the doctrine of the image of thought: thought takes over from the State apparatus a model that prescribes it "goals and paths, conduits, channels" (ATP 374); method is "the striated space of the cogitatio universalis", while the thought of the outside knows only "relays, intermezzos, resurgences" (ATP 377). What is demanded is "a problem-thought instead of an essence-thought or theorem" (ATP 378). The warning layer: ambulant sciences are structurally not autonomous — "problematics is still its only mode" (ATP 373), and their problems are solved scientifically only through the royal translation (ATP 374). For artistic research this means, as a textual finding: its relation to academic formalisation is constitutive, not merely repressive; the field is "a single field of interaction" in which only "the constantly shifting borderline" counts (ATP 367).

### 4.4 Becoming and Haecceity

Becoming (devenir) is a reality in its own right, not an as-if figure: "Becomings-animal are neither dreams nor phantasies. They are perfectly real. […] Becoming produces nothing other than itself" (ATP 238). It is neither correspondence nor resemblance, "an imitation, or, at the limit, an identification" (ATP 237); what is real is "the becoming itself, the block of becoming, not the supposedly fixed terms through which that which becomes passes" (ATP 238). The unit of becoming is the block that runs *between* the terms — wasp and orchid form a block "from which no wasp-orchid can ever descend" (ATP 238) — and it is always double and asymmetrical: "Becoming is always double, that which one becomes becomes no less than the one that becomes" (ATP 305). Operatively defined: "becoming is to extract particles between which one establishes the relations of movement and rest, speed and slowness that are closest to what one is becoming" (ATP 272). The text's instruction is precisely anti-mimetic: "Do not imitate a dog, but make your organism enter into composition with something else in such a way that the particles emitted from the aggregate thus composed will be canine" (ATP 274); art supplies the evidence — in *The Birds* Hitchcock does not reproduce bird calls but produces "an electronic sound like a field of intensities" (ATP 305); "One does not imitate; one constitutes a block of becoming" (ATP 305).

The corresponding model of individuation is haecceity: "There is a mode of individuation very different from that of a person, subject, thing, or substance. […] A season, a winter, a summer, an hour, a date have a perfect individuality lacking nothing" (ATP 261). On the plane of consistency a body is defined "only by a longitude and a latitude" — longitude as particle relations of movement and rest, latitude as the capacity to affect and be affected (ATP 260–261); "Latitude and longitude are the two elements of a cartography" (ATP 261). Haecceity even has a semiotics of its own: "Indefinite article + proper name + infinitive verb constitutes the basic chain of expression" (ATP 263). Behind this stands the distinction between two planes: the plan(e) of organization and development concerns "the development of forms and the formation of subjects" and exists in a supplementary dimension (n + 1) (ATP 265–266); the plane of consistency or composition knows "only relations of movement and rest, speed and slowness between unformed elements", only "haecceities, affects, subjectless individuations" (ATP 266). Musically attested: Cage unfolds "this fixed sound plane, which affirms a process against all structure and genesis, […] experimentation against any kind of interpretation" (ATP 267), and failing belongs to the plan(e) — "As Cage says, it is of the nature of the plan(e) that it fail" (ATP 269).

Two determinations are decisive for the research subject. First: "all becoming is a becoming-minoritarian" (ATP 291) — the majority is not a matter of greater quantity but a standard of domination, and even members of a minority must "become" instead of reterritorializing themselves on a state (ATP 291); this becoming "necessitates a labor of power (puissance), an active micropolitics" (ATP 292). Second, becoming-imperceptible as the "immanent end of becoming, its cosmic formula" (ATP 279) — with the corresponding working rule of sobriety, supplied by Virginia Woolf: "Saturate, eliminate, put everything in" (ATP 280). Becoming, finally, is "an antimemory" (ATP 294): "A line of becoming has only a middle. […] A becoming is always in the middle; one can only get it by the middle" (ATP 293). The text's warning: there is the constant threat of relapse into "playing" the other — "There is always the danger of finding yourself 'playing' the animal" (ATP 260) — and the line of flight of becoming can tip over into a "line of abolition, annihilation, self-destruction" (ATP 250).

### 4.5 Refrain, Territory, Consistency

The refrain (ritournelle) is D&G's model for the relation of chaos, order and opening — and thereby, as chapter 5 will argue, the best available process model of artistic work. It has three aspects, which the text expressly does not understand as phases: "These are not three successive moments in an evolution. They are three aspects of a single thing" (ATP 312). First, the child in the dark who, by singing, procures itself a fragile centre in the chaos — "The song is like a rough sketch of a calming and stabilizing, calm and stable, center in the heart of chaos" (ATP 311). Second, the circle around home: "home does not preexist: it was necessary to draw a circle around that uncertain and fragile center, to organize a limited space" (ATP 311). Third, the opening: "Finally, one opens the circle a crack […] One launches forth, hazards an improvisation. But to improvise is to join with the World, or meld with it" (ATP 311) — and not on the side where the old chaos presses, but in a region "one created by the circle itself" (ATP 311). This is undergirded by the distinction between rhythm and metre: "Meter is dogmatic, but rhythm is critical"; rhythm is "the Unequal or the Incommensurable" (ATP 313), and "Rhythm is the milieus' answer to chaos" (ATP 313).

The territory arises when milieu components cease to be functional and become expressive: "There is a territory when the rhythm has expressiveness. What defines the territory is the emergence of matters of expression (qualities)" (ATP 315); the causality is the reverse of what one thinks — "it is the mark that makes the territory" (ATP 315). Here the book situates the beginning of art: "The artist: the first person to set out a boundary stone, or to make a mark" (ATP 316); "Take anything and make it a matter of expression. The stagemaker practices art brut. Artists are stagemakers" (ATP 316) — with the bowerbird Scenopoeetes, which turns leaves over so that their paler underside stands out against the ground (ATP 315), as non-human chief witness.

How does such a territory hold the heterogeneous together? Through consolidation, with three nameable factors: intercalated elements (intercalation), intervals ("it is sometimes necessary to make a hole in order to consolidate") and the superposition of disparate rhythms (ATP 328–329). Consistency is "the 'holding together' of heterogeneous elements" (ATP 323), and its formula shifts the entire understanding of art: "It is no longer a question of imposing a form upon a matter but of elaborating an increasingly rich and consistent material, the better to tap increasingly intense forces" (ATP 329). This shift is unfolded historically in the sketch of the three "ages": classicism as a form–matter relation (ATP 338), romanticism as territory and earth, in which form itself becomes force (ATP 340), the modern as the age of the cosmic: "It is now a direct relation material-forces. A material is a molecularized matter, which must accordingly 'harness' forces" (ATP 342) — with Klee's maxim "Render visible […] not render or reproduce the visible" (ATP 342). The corresponding working discipline is sobriety: "Sobriety, sobriety: that is the common prerequisite for the deterritorialization of matters" (ATP 344); "there is no imagination outside of technique" (ATP 344–345); the modern figure is "not the child or the lunatic, still less the artist, but the cosmic artisan" (ATP 345). The close of the plateau radicalises this into a determination of the task that at the same time relativises all system-building: "Produce a deterritorialized refrain as the final end of music, release it in the Cosmos—that is more important than building a new system" — and, signed with the name Schumann: "we can never be sure we will be strong enough, for we have no system, only lines and movements" (ATP 350).

### 4.6 Smooth and Striated

The conceptual pair smooth/striated distinguishes two types of space — the nomadic space of the war machine and the space instituted by the State apparatus (ATP 474) — but the first and most important thesis is the mixture thesis: "the two spaces in fact exist only in mixture: smooth space is constantly being translated, transversed into a striated space; striated space is constantly being reversed, returned to a smooth space" (ATP 474). The de jure distinction nonetheless remains possible (ATP 475), and D&G develop it through models: in the technological model, woven fabric with warp and weft stands against felt, which is "in principle infinite, open, and unlimited in every direction" (ATP 475–476). The musical model, which goes back to Pierre Boulez, yields the most succinct formula: "in a smooth space-time one occupies without counting, whereas in a striated space-time one counts in order to occupy" (ATP 477); the smooth is "the continuous variation, continuous development of form; […] the pure act of the drawing of a diagonal across the vertical and the horizontal" (ATP 478). Important is the clarification: "'smooth' does not mean homogeneous, quite the contrary" (ATP 477) — homogeneity is rather the extreme result of striation (ATP 488).

Smooth space has a perceptual form of its own: it is "filled by events or haecceities, far more than by formed and perceived things. It is a space of affects, more than one of properties. It is haptic rather than optical perception" (ATP 479). The aesthetic model unfolds this as close-range vision: "A painting is done at close range, even if it is seen from a distance" (ATP 493) — with Cézanne's "need to no longer see the wheat field", to lose oneself, without landmarks, in smooth space (ATP 493), and with Riegl's and Worringer's haptic space and abstract line: "The abstract line is the affect of smooth space, just as organic representation was the feeling presiding over striated space" (ATP 499). The epistemic point for a research model stands in the mathematical model: "all progress is made by and in striated space, but all becoming occurs in smooth space" (ATP 486). Between the two mediates translation, which is double-faced: it can subjugate and overcode smooth space — or give it "a milieu of propagation", "without which it would perhaps die of its own accord" (ATP 486–487).

Precisely because the pair invites evaluation, the warning layer is thickest here. The smooth can be "drawn and occupied by diabolical powers of organization" (ATP 480); capitalism itself reconstitutes smooth space in the shape of machinic enslavement (ATP 492). The plateau ends with the sentence that cuts off every expectation of salvation directed at open, nomadic, "smooth" procedures: "Of course, smooth spaces are not in themselves liberatory. […] Never believe that a smooth space will suffice to save us" (ATP 500). For artistic research this secures, as a textual finding, both things: a precise analytic tool for institutional spaces (where is counting done in order to occupy?) and a prohibition on using this tool as a good/evil axis.

### 4.7 Body without Organs, Lines, Dangers

The body without organs (BwO) is "not at all a notion or a concept but a practice, a set of practices. You never reach the Body without Organs, you can't reach it, you are forever attaining it, it is a limit" (ATP 149–150). Its enemy is not the organ but "that organization of the organs called the organism" (ATP 158). The plateau's basic epistemic decision is the switch from interpretation to experiment: "Substitute forgetting for anamnesis, experimentation for interpretation" (ATP 151) — and its format is the programme as distinct from the phantasy: "This is not a phantasy, it is a program" (ATP 151). On the BwO only intensities circulate (ATP 153); two things are to be asked: how is it fabricated, and what happens (or does not happen) on it (ATP 152).

Decisive for a research model is that this experimenting is bound to an explicit code of caution. Caution is immanent to experimentation: "Not wisdom, caution. In doses. As a rule immanent to experimentation: injections of caution" (ATP 150). The dosage rule: "You don't do it with a sledgehammer, you use a very fine file" (ATP 160). The rations rule: keep enough organism "for it to reform each dawn", small supplies of significance and subjectivity, so as to be able to answer the dominant reality — "Mimic the strata. You don't reach the BwO […] by wildly destratifying" (ATP 160). The ranking of evils: "Staying stratified—organized, signified, subjected—is not the worst that can happen; the worst that can happen is if you throw the strata into demented or suicidal collapse, which brings them back down on us heavier than ever" (ATP 161). And the positive instruction: "Lodge yourself on a stratum, experiment with the opportunities it offers, find an advantageous place on it, find potential movements of deterritorialization, possible lines of flight, experience them, […] have a small plot of new land at all times" (ATP 161). The "three-body problem" — distinguishing full, empty and cancerous BwOs (ATP 163) — turns this into a permanent task of selection: "keep an eye out for all that is fascist, even inside us, and also for the suicidal and the demented" (ATP 165).

The novella plateau translates this into a self-analytics of the lines. "For we are made of lines" (ATP 194): a hard, molar line of segmentarity on which "everything seems calculable and foreseen" (ATP 195); a molecular line with segments "like quanta of deterritorialization" (ATP 196); and a line of flight, "no less deadly and no less alive than the others" (ATP 197–198). The procedure is expressly cartographic: "It is an affair of cartography. They compose us, as they compose our map" (ATP 203), with a veritable catalogue of questions: "What is your body without organs? What are your lines? What map are you in the process of making or rearranging? What abstract line will you draw, and at what price, for yourself and for others?" (ATP 203). Lines of flight, in this, are not found but drawn: "the only way we can invent them is by effectively drawing them, in our lives" (ATP 202).

The micropolitics plateau, finally, systematises the dangers of the lines — expressly without moralising: "We cannot say that one of these three lines is bad and another good, by nature and necessarily" (ATP 227). There are four: Fear, which makes us harden our segments — "We flee from flight, rigidify our segments" (ATP 227); Clarity, the danger of the molecular line itself, which brings forth "a thousand little monomanias" and "self-appointed judge[s]" (ATP 228); Power (Pouvoir), which operates on both lines at once and seeks to bring the lines of flight to a standstill (ATP 228–229); and the great disgust, the "Passion for abolition" (ATP 227): the line of flight which, instead of connecting with other lines and "each time augmenting its valence", tips over into "destruction, abolition pure and simple" (ATP 229). On this fourth danger hangs D&G's analysis of fascism — fascism as a war machine that seizes hold of the State, as "realized nihilism" (ATP 230) — and with it the heaviest mortgage on any emphatic talk of deterritorialization.

## 5. The Model: Artistic Research as Cartographic Practice

The explication condenses into a model that determines artistic research as a cartographic practice. It consists of six postulates. The status of this "model" must be stated precisely: it is not a procedural prescription for research — to supply a phase model would mean sequentialising the refrain, which the text rules out (ATP 312) — but a *process grammar:* a language of description for the object, form of knowledge, procedure, temporal form, subject and ethics of research, from which an instrumentarium (ch. 6) and criteria of assessment (ch. 7) follow. Where "model" stands in what follows as shorthand, this triad of grammar, instruments and criteria is meant. "Postulate" is meant deliberately weakly: not axioms to deduce from, but working hypotheses to be re-proven in every project — in the status of those "optional rules" that chapter 2 established (ATP 93–94, 103).

**Postulate 1 — Object: assemblages, not works.** The unit of analysis of artistic research is not the work as a closed object but the assemblage in which bodies, materials, techniques, spaces, statements, contracts and institutions hang together. The textual finding is the tetravalence: every assemblage is at once a machinic assemblage of bodies and a collective assemblage of enunciation, with territorial sides and cutting edges of deterritorialization (ATP 88, 505); both must always be ascertained, "what is said and what is done" (ATP 504). The consequence for authorship is equally textual: "There are no individual statements, only statement-producing machinic assemblages" (ATP 36) — the researcher speaks in her own name precisely when she produces the statement of an assemblage (ATP 37). Going beyond this, I propose treating the research situation itself — studio, rehearsal stage, doctoral programme — as an assemblage: the first concrete rule, to discover the territoriality every assemblage envelops (ATP 503–504), then applies also to the "home" of research.

**Postulate 2 — Form of knowledge: map, not copy.** Artistic research produces maps of processes, not representations of results. Textual finding: the map is oriented toward "experimentation in contact with the real," constructs its object rather than depicting it, and is open, reversible, constantly modifiable (ATP 12); to map is to plot longitudes (relations of matter) and latitudes (affects) — "Latitude and longitude are the two elements of a cartography" (ATP 261) — and in doing so "not to represent, interpret, or symbolize, but only to make maps and draw lines" (ATP 227). Writing itself is so determined: "Writing has nothing to do with signifying. It has to do with surveying, mapping, even realms that are yet to come" (ATP 4–5). Existing copies — theories, method chapters, traditions — are not discarded but put back on the map (ATP 13–14). With de Assis this can be sharpened as the difference between extensive mapping and intensive cartography (de Assis 2021, p. 22): the map does not measure out quantities, it explores qualities.

**Postulate 3 — Procedure: following, not reproducing.** The procedure of artistic research is that of minor science: to follow the singularities of a material rather than reproduce from a fixed external standpoint (ATP 372); to yield to the material and connect operations to its materiality rather than impose a form on it (ATP 408); to invent problems rather than derive theorems (ATP 362, 374). The design of research is therefore itinerant: "with the ambulant model, the process of deterritorialization constitutes and extends the territory itself" (ATP 372) — the field emerges through the movement of the work, not before it. Equally textual is the modesty clause: "Not better, just different" (ATP 372); the model grounds no superiority of artistic research, only a different regime of formalisation that remains dependent on interaction with "royal" formalisation (ATP 373–374).

**Postulate 4 — Process form: the refrain.** The temporal course of artistic research follows no phase-model of hypothesis, execution and evaluation but the rhythm of the refrain: to win a fragile hold in chaos, to organise a working-home, to open the circle at a self-created point (ATP 311) — these three aspects coexisting, not succeeding one another (ATP 312). The form of holding-together is consistency, not system: consolidation through intercalated elements, intervals and superposition (ATP 328–329), "Never unifications, never totalizations, but rather consistencies or consolidations" (ATP 507). Going beyond this, I propose using territorialization, deterritorialization and reterritorialization as a language of description for the courses of projects — for instance for the cycle of studio work, opening into the uncertain and publication — with the textual proviso that the opening occurs "not on the side where the old forces of chaos press" but in a region generated by one's own territory (ATP 311).

**Postulate 5 — Knowing subject: becoming, not standpoint.** Artistic research has no fixed knowing subject contemplating an object from outside; it changes the researchers. Textual finding: becoming is real and double — "that which one becomes becomes no less than the one that becomes" (ATP 305); the proper name is "the instantaneous apprehension of a multiplicity," attained at the highest point of depersonalisation (ATP 36–37); the research subject is more a haecceity — "You are longitude and latitude, a set of speeds and slownesses between unformed particles, a set of nonsubjectified affects" (ATP 262) — than an author-subject. For documentation this means not declaring a standpoint but recording the zone of proximity the work has entered (ATP 272–273). This converges with Klein's finding that artistic experience is non-delegable and at the same time itself a form of reflection (Klein 2010, pp. 4–5) — except that the model situates the experience in the assemblage rather than the subject.

**Postulate 6 — Ethics: caution, not transgression.** The model breaks with the avant-garde rhetoric of boundary-breaking. The textual finding is the dosage ethics of the body-without-organs plateau: caution as the rule immanent to experimentation (ATP 150), the file rather than the sledgehammer (ATP 160), the rations rule and the "small plot of new land at all times" (ATP 160–161). Radicality is no value in itself: "a too-sudden destratification may be suicidal, or turn cancerous" (ATP 503), and the worst is not to stay stratified but the demented collapse that brings the strata back "heavier than ever" (ATP 161). For collaborative research the compossibility test is added: "There is no assurance that two lines of flight will prove compatible, compossible" (ATP 205).

The six postulates are not additive but interlock. Postulate 1 determines the object that postulate 2 maps; following (postulate 3) is the movement-form of that mapping; the refrain (postulate 4) describes its temporality; becoming (postulate 5) names what happens to the researchers in the process; caution (postulate 6) regulates the intensity of the whole. Their shared vanishing-point is the plane of consistency — and its decisive property is that it is not given but made: "The field of immanence or plane of consistency must be constructed. This can take place in very different social formations through very different assemblages (perverse, artistic, scientific, mystical, political)" (ATP 157). That D&G here expressly name artistic assemblages in one series with scientific ones is perhaps the single most important passage for the model: artistic research is one of the ways in which the plane is "constructed piece by piece" (ATP 157) — piece by piece, with no building plan preceding it. Consistency acts "in the middle, through the middle" (*"in der Mitte, durch die Mitte"*) and stands opposed to all planes of principle or finality (ATP 507); the image plateau 15 finds for it will serve as the emblem of the whole model: "The plane is like a row of doors" (ATP 508) — a row of doors, not a staircase.

*Problem-finding.* A process grammar that only set in once a project was under way would be incomplete; how projects begin must also be determined. The textual answer the model gives here is that of the problematic procedure: one does not go from a stable essence to its properties but "from a problem to the accidents that condition and resolve it" (ATP 362); what is demanded is "a problem-thought instead of an essence-thought or theorem" (ATP 378), and the figure is an event — the square does not exist independently of a quadrature (ATP 362). Problems, accordingly, are not chosen like themes from a list; they are found where something generates resistance and can only be followed — "this matter-flow can only be followed" (ATP 409): a singularity of material, an institutional striation, a crack in one's own practice. Formulated as extrapolation: the beginning of artistic research is, in this model, not a knowledge interest but a site of encounter, and its test-question is whether it carries a problem in the strong sense — a quadrature that can generate a square — or only a theme under which cases can be subsumed. T4 operationalises this test in its first procedural step; the refrain complementarily determines when a territory carries an opening (ATP 311). More than this the model does not claim about beginnings — in particular no heuristic that would generate problems: to possess one would be to have the quadrature before the square.

*Relation to the epistemology of the aesthetic.* The model converges with Mersch and the Manifesto on the core thesis of non-propositionality: what artistic research brings forth are not statements about the world. ATP, however, supplies a relational rather than a negative vocabulary for this: the diagram "has neither substance nor form, neither content nor expression" (ATP 141) and functions not by representing but constructively — it "constructs a real that is yet to come, a new type of reality" (ATP 142). Non-propositionality thus need not be described as a lack of statements but as an asignifying semiotics with its own productivity. The convergence reaches into the details: Mersch's epistemology of the singular — "But deviation is the rule in the sensuous" (*"Die Abweichung ist aber die Regel im Sinnlichen"*; Mersch 2015, typescript p. 5) — touches the anexact, "vague yet rigorous" essences of nomad science (ATP 367), and Mersch himself builds the bridge in a footnote when he connects Duchamp's *inframinces* with the concept of microperception in Deleuze/Guattari and Massumi (Mersch 2015, typescript p. 6). The productive difference lies in the gesture: Mersch's vocabulary is passive (befalling, receiving, zetetics; Mersch 2015, typescript pp. 8–10, 15–16), D&G's vocabulary is constructive (to make, to map, to experiment); and where the Manifesto grounds aesthetic manifestation phenomenologically and binds it to judgment (Henke/Mersch et al. 2020, pp. 39, 61), D&G are decidedly anti-phenomenological and set against any philosophy of judgment. The model does not claim to dissolve this difference; it claims that the two sides correct each other: the pragmatics preserves the epistemology of the aesthetic from inconsequentiality, the epistemology preserves the pragmatics from letting the aisthetic dissolve into procedure. That the arts form no system in the process is itself a textual finding: "To us, Art is a false concept, a solely nominal concept" (ATP 300–301) — the model presupposes no unity of "art" but heterogeneous problems (painting: face–landscape; music: the refrain).

*What the model is not.* It is not a worldview: the concepts are tools with an expiry date, their selection must remain "active and temporary" and be renewed (ATP 10). It is not a style: a project that looks rhizomatic does not fulfil the model — D&G themselves dismiss typographic feats as "Technonarcissism" (ATP 22). And it is not a rhetoric of legitimation: the model supplies no argument that artistic research is "really" research already, but working instruments and test-questions; its proving is practical, not discursive — whereby it takes up Almeida's demand for the transition from meta-debate to instrumentation (Almeida 2015, pp. 159–160).

*Situating in relation to related models.* The cartographic model differs from the field's closest Deleuzian approaches not in vocabulary but in its cut. De Assis's "general logic of assemblage" remains work- and practice-analytical — it describes works with the logic of the assemblage and addresses the question "How does it work? How does it move?" to the work (de Assis 2021, pp. 22–23) — whereas the model presented here addresses the research process itself: problem-finding, procedure, exposition, assessment. From Correia/Dalagna's Aveiro model it is separated by text base and model type: its Deleuze grounding runs primarily through *Différence et répétition* and *Francis Bacon*, and its result is a criteria model (the mythopoetic double movement as definition), not an instrumentarium for the conduct of research (Correia/Dalagna et al. 2018, p. 23). And from van der Tuin/Borgen/Lund's prospective-retrospective toolkit it is separated by its foundation: that toolkit is won diffractively from Barad and Haraway and built toward output formats (van der Tuin/Borgen/Lund 2021), not explicated page-exactly from a single work. The model thus distils from *A Thousand Plateaus* a pragmatics of research where those approaches analyse works, define criteria or order formats.

## 6. The Toolkit: Eight Instruments

The following eight instruments translate the model into handleable procedures. Each instrument is presented in a fixed form: ATP grounding, procedural steps, a brief application example, limits and typical misuse — and a *failure criterion:* a testable condition by which one's own use proves to be running idle. The difference is deliberate: misuse warnings describe how others can get an instrument wrong; the failure criterion lays down how I recognise that, in my hands, it is doing nothing. The application examples, where they do not come from ATP itself, are constructed demonstrations and are to be read as such — they render the handling plausible, they do not prove it; one of them (T6) therefore deliberately presents a failure. For all instruments the framing from chapter 2 holds: they are optional rules (ATP 93–94, 103), copies to be laid back onto the map of the given project (ATP 13) — and they are accountable to the quality criteria of chapter 7, which lie before them argumentatively even though they stand after them in the text.

*Instrument economy.* Eight instruments are an offer, not a quota. Whoever runs all eight at once has misunderstood the toolkit as a compulsory menu and will spend more time on self-auditing than on the work — the self-mirroring T6 warns against begins with the very accumulation of instruments. Three ordering rules. *First:* T1 and T2 share their object — T1 is the overview mapping of the project in breadth, T2 the tetravalent deepening at one spot on that map; whoever uses both keeps one map, not two. *Second:* T4, T5 and T6 are not three notebooks but three lenses of a single working journal — the following lens records continuously at the material, the refrain lens draws a balance per working phase, the lines analysis tests at long intervals (roughly every six months); one entry can serve several lenses at once. *Third:* T3 and T7 are occasion instruments (statement, grant application, change of institution, format decision), T8 concerns exposition alone. As a minimal entry set it suffices to keep T1 as a living document, one journal lens according to the state of the material, and the five criteria of chapter 7 as topoi of the concluding deliberation; everything further has to justify itself by occasion.

### T1 Mapping Protocol

*ATP grounding:* The six rhizome principles (ATP 7–13), the properties of the map — open, connectable, reversible, constantly modifiable, with multiple entryways (ATP 12) —, the rule of laying copies back onto the map (ATP 13–14) and the instruction to resituate dead ends on the map, "thereby opening them up to possible lines of flight" (ATP 14). The model is Deligny's procedure of mapping the "lines of drift" and "customary lines" of gestures and movements — lines that do not "mean" anything but are plotted (ATP 202–203).

*Procedure:*

- Inventory one's own project as an assemblage: elements of all registers (materials, persons, discourses, institutions, techniques) on one sheet — the book's ideal of the single plane: "lived events, historical determinations, concepts, individuals, groups, social formations" (ATP 9).

- Draw lines rather than positions: which connections actually operate, which segmentations, which movements of flight (ATP 8–9)?

- Apply the six principles as test-questions: can every point be connected to every other? Are the connections heterogeneous? Does the project change its nature when connections are added (ATP 7–8)?

- Lay existing copies (theories, method texts, models) back on the map instead of discarding them (ATP 13–14); enter dead ends as dead ends and resituate them (ATP 14).

- Rework the map periodically — it is "susceptible to constant modification" (ATP 12).

*Application example:* A performance project maps, on the Deligny model, the lines of movement and attention of several rehearsals on top of one another (cf. "combine several maps," ATP 14) and discovers that an incidental transition gesture generates more connections than the planned centre of the piece — the map, not the intention, reorganises the work.

*Limits/misuse:* The map becomes illustration — the pretty network diagram for the grant application. Then it is copy again: extensive mapping instead of intensive cartography (de Assis 2021, p. 22). The touchstone is the performance clause: the map belongs to performance, not to an alleged competence (ATP 12–13); a map that is no longer being worked on is not one.

*Failure criterion:* The map was never revised after its creation — the performance clause is testable: no dated reworkings across the project's duration — or no case can be named in which the map changed a working decision. Then it was illustration: copy, not map (ATP 12–13).

### T2 Assemblage Analysis

*ATP grounding:* The tetravalence of the assemblage (ATP 88–89) and the concrete rules of plateau 15: discover the territoriality every assemblage envelops (ATP 503–504); survey content and expression each separately — "In each case, it is necessary to ascertain both what is said and what is done" (ATP 504); interrogate the two axes: territoriality, regime of signs and pragmatic system on the one hand, cutting edges of deterritorialization and effectuated abstract machines on the other (ATP 505). D&G name Kafka's work as the "privileged example" of this four-valued analysis (ATP 505).

*Procedure:*

- Field 1 — bodies: which interminglings of bodies constitute the situation (materials, spaces, apparatuses, physical bodies; the stirrup logic of the feudal example, ATP 89–90)?

- Field 2 — statements: which statements, titles, contracts, judgments, "oaths" belong to it; which incorporeal transformations do they effect (ATP 80–82, 88)?

- Field 3 — territorializations: what stabilises the assemblage — institution, format, routine, "home" (ATP 503–504)?

- Field 4 — cutting edges of deterritorialization: where does the assemblage leave its territory, and which abstract machine becomes effective in the process (ATP 505)?

- Cross-check: no derivation of one field from another — the assemblage has "neither base nor superstructure" (ATP 90).

*Application example:* An exhibition is described tetravalently: hanging, climate control, visitors' bodies (field 1); wall texts, loan contracts, press release (field 2); museum architecture, genre conventions (field 3); a work that draws the audience into the storage area and thereby opens the institution at its edge (field 4). What becomes visible is that the "critical" wall-text rhetoric (field 2) is neutralised by the territorialization (field 3) — a diagnosis that neither work analysis nor the sociology of institutions can deliver alone.

*Limits/misuse:* The grid is misunderstood as a classification and "filled in" bureaucratically. But the four fields are vectors, not drawers; the analysis is complete only when it captures the movement between the fields — the second axis is not a residual category but the point of the instrument (ATP 505).

*Failure criterion:* Field 4 stays empty or without consequence — the analysis names no cutting edge of deterritorialization on which work continues — or the four fields are filled without a single movement between them being described. A tetravalent form without a second axis is bureaucracy in the literal sense (cf. ATP 505).

### T3 Pragmatics Audit of One's Own Art Discourse

*ATP grounding:* The four components of pragmatics (ATP 145–146), the sequence of operations tracing–mapping–diagramming–programming (ATP 146–147) and the three-step analysis protocol D&G demonstrate on "I love you" (ATP 147–148). In addition, the theory of the order-word: language as "the set of all order-words, implicit presuppositions, or speech acts current in a language at a given moment" (ATP 79) and the incorporeal transformations that statements effect on bodies (ATP 80–82).

*Procedure:*

- Choose a core proposition of one's own project discourse (artist statement, grant sentence, press text: "my work investigates X").

- Determine the regime: is the proposition addressed signifyingly (soliciting interpretation, referred to a centre), postsignifyingly-passionally (avowal, point of subjectification), presignifyingly (collective-polyvocal) or countersignifyingly (polemical-distributive) (ATP 147)?

- Translation test: what happens on transfer into another regime — "what passes and does not pass in such a transformation, what remains irreducible and what flows" (ATP 147)?

- Generate new statements: "one could try to create new, as yet unknown statements for that proposition" (ATP 147).

- Order-word inventory of the field: which order-words govern one's own scene (funding formulas, jury vocabulary, curriculum language), and which incorporeal transformations do their rituals effect — who or what is instantaneously transformed into something else by vernissage, certificate, prize ceremony (textual in the concept, ATP 80–82; extrapolation in the application)?

- Search for the "pass-words" beneath the order-words (ATP 110).

*Application example:* A sound artist audits her grant sentence "My practice explores listening as resistance" and finds that it functions postsignifyingly (avowal to a funding face). The translation test into the presignifying — the sentence is replaced by a collective listening situation instead of being asserted — changes the project design itself; the untranslatable remainder ("resistance" as a pathos formula) is struck.

*Limits/misuse:* Regimes are functions, not ethnographies: "There is no reason to identify a regime or a semiotic system with a people or historical moment" (ATP 119), and all semiotics are mixed (ATP 136). A second misuse: passing surface findings off as regime change — "Crazy talk is not enough" (ATP 138); deleting the "I" proves nothing (ATP 138).

*Failure criterion:* The translation test changes only the wording, not the project design — the grant sentence becomes more elegant, the situation stays the same. An audit that ends as a style exercise has not changed the regime but served it ("Crazy talk is not enough," ATP 138).

### T4 Minor-Science Heuristic

*ATP grounding:* The four characteristics of nomad science (ATP 361–362), the distinction following/reproducing (ATP 372–373), the critique of hylomorphism together with the material rule — "surrendering to the wood, then following where it leads" (ATP 408); "this matter-flow can only be followed" (ATP 409) — and the licence for anexact, "vague yet rigorous" concepts (ATP 367; cf. ATP 20).

*Procedure:*

- Formulate the project as a problem, not a theorem: from a problem to the accidents "that condition and resolve it" (ATP 362) — not a hypothesis to be confirmed but a quadrature that first generates the square (ATP 362).

- Prospect the singularities of the material: where do its implicit topological forms lie, its intensive affects — hardness, porosity, elasticity, sonic behaviour (ATP 405–408)?

- Keep a "following-journal": an itinerant record that does not note results from the fixed bank ("watching the flow from the bank," ATP 372) but the movement of following itself — detours, abandonments, the material's changes of direction.

- Admit and cultivate anexact concepts: working concepts may remain vaguely precise so long as they designate "the exact passage of that which is under way" (ATP 20).

- Plan for the royal translation: document what is lost in formalisation (method chapter, documentation text) — "what intuition gets lost in it" (ATP 408–409).

*Application example:* The book itself supplies the pattern: the Gothic stonecutter cuts the stone not by template but so that "the cutting line propels the equation" (ATP 364) — the equation is generated by the material as a thrust, not imposed on it (ATP 364–365). Transferred: a sculptor working with grown wood records how grain direction and the singularities of knots generate the form of the work (ATP 408), instead of "realising" a design drawing; the following-journal becomes part of the exposition.

*Limits/misuse:* The anti-academic pose. Minor science is "Not better, just different" (ATP 372) and dependent on the field of interaction with royal formalisation (ATP 367, 373–374) — the collapsed cathedrals of Orléans and Beauvais (ATP 374) are a reminder that "approximate knowledge" raises real questions of safety. A following-journal is no substitute for structural engineering.

*Failure criterion:* The following-journal is written after the fact — reconstruction instead of record — or registers not a single deviation from plan across the project's duration. A journal without detours documents no following, only reproducing with a better conscience (ATP 372).

### T5 Refrain Journal

*ATP grounding:* The three aspects of the refrain (ATP 311–312), the classification of refrains from the territorial to the cosmic type (ATP 327), the "components of passage" that mediate between assemblages as "assemblage converter" (ATP 325), and the consolidation triad: intercalated elements, intervals, superposition (ATP 328–329). In addition, modernity's sobriety rule: "Sobriety, sobriety" (ATP 344).

*Procedure:*

- For each working phase, note which aspect of the refrain dominates: seeking a hold in chaos, organising the working-home, or opening the circle (ATP 311) — in the knowledge that all three coexist (ATP 312).

- Identify the project's "components of passage": which element (a motif, a device, a habit) functions as a converter between territories (ATP 325)?

- Test openings as to whether they occur at a self-created point or there "where the old forces of chaos press" (ATP 311).

- Apply the consolidation triad as a composition test: what is intercalated (densifications, injections), where are the holes ("make a hole in order to consolidate"), which disparate rhythms are superposed without a metre unifying them (ATP 328–329)?

- Sobriety balance: is the material "prodigiously simplified, creatively limited, selected" (ATP 344–345)?

*Application example:* Musically the instrument is applicable almost identically to the text: Schumann treats an "extremely sober kind of refrain" through a highly elaborate melodic line (ATP 297); music seizes the refrain, makes it "more and more sober, reduced to a few notes," and carries it forward on a creative line (ATP 302). A composition project documents in the refrain journal which minimal motif functions as territory, when it is deterritorialized, and whether the opening gains consistency or falls into the "black hole" of premature deterritorialization (ATP 334).

*Limits/misuse:* The journal is read as a phase model (first territory, then opening) — the text rules this out: "sometimes, sometimes, sometimes" (ATP 312). A second misuse: the cosmic dilettantism that puts "too much" into it and, instead of a sound machine, produces "a scribble effacing all sounds" (ATP 343–344); the antidote is built into the instrument itself (the sobriety balance).

*Failure criterion:* The entries sequentialise the three aspects after all — first hold, then home, then opening; the text rules exactly this out (ATP 312) — or the opening test has in no case shifted, prevented or differently placed an opening. A journal that never intervenes in the course of things is chronicle, not instrument.

### T6 Lines-and-Dangers Self-Analysis

*ATP grounding:* The three lines of the novella plateau — rigid segmentarity, molecular segmentation, line of flight (ATP 195–198, 200) —, the schizoanalytic question-catalogue (ATP 203), the compossibility test for shared lines (ATP 205–206) and the four dangers: Fear, Clarity, Power, Passion of abolition (ATP 227–231).

*Procedure:*

- List the project's rigid segments: institution, funding logic, genre, roles — the line on which "everything seems calculable and foreseen" (ATP 195).

- Register molecular cracks: "micro-cracks, as in a dish" (ATP 198) — shifts of interest, energy, relation that occur "when things are going well on the other side" (ATP 198).

- Name lines of flight and go through the question-catalogue: "What map are you in the process of making or rearranging? What abstract line will you draw, and at what price, for yourself and for others?" (ATP 203).

- Danger audit per line: Fear (hardening of formats, retreat into proven work-forms, ATP 227), Clarity (scene dogmatism, "self-appointed judge," ATP 228), Power (self-canonisation, arresting one's own mutation, ATP 228–229), Passion of abolition (expenditure without connection, ATP 229).

- In collaborations: test the compatibility of the lines — "one person's creative line is the other's imprisonment" (ATP 205).

*Application example:* D&G themselves demonstrate the instrument on F. Scott Fitzgerald's "The Crack-Up": the great ruptures from outside, the barely perceptible cracks, the "clean break" from which there is no return (ATP 198–199) — and, on the pair Fitzgerald/Zelda, the mixture of binary machine, double extraction and shared line of flight (ATP 206). For an artistic doctoral project this means concretely: record the three lines and four dangers every six months — not as self-mirroring but as a risk instrument that makes, say, a looming dramaturgy of exhaustion (fourth danger) visible early. But let the counter-case be constructed too: the same project produces, in three successive rounds, well-formed, nearly identical entries — the segments are still called "institution," the line of flight still "the next work," and no note has ever touched a decision. The failure criterion triggers; the instrument is struck for this project, and precisely that is documented: an instrument that got no purchase on this assemblage. For a toolkit won from a book about productive failures, this outcome is no operational accident but a datum.

*Limits/misuse:* Moralising: no line is in itself good or bad (ATP 227), and the molecular line is not "better" — it reconstitutes in miniature what it dissolves: "micro-Oedipuses, microformations of power, microfascisms" (ATP 205). Whoever uses "line of flight" as a seal of quality has inverted the instrument into its opposite.

*Failure criterion:* Several rounds note exclusively the dangers of others — the institution, the scene — and never one's own; or no audit ever had a consequence: no changed collaboration, no adjusted workload, no discarded plan. A risk instrument without a single risk consequence is self-mirroring at a safe distance.

### T7 Smooth/Striated Audit of Institutional Spaces

*ATP grounding:* The mixture thesis (ATP 474–475) with the operative test-question: "is a smooth space captured, enveloped by a striated space, or does a striated space dissolve into a smooth space […]?" (ATP 475); the Boulez formula of counting and occupying (ATP 477); the double-faced translation operation (ATP 486–487); the warnings against the occupied smooth (ATP 480, 492).

*Procedure:*

- Inventory the spaces of one's own working: studio, school, funding format, publication format, platform.

- For each space, ask the Boulez question: is one counting in order to occupy (credits, deadlines, evaluation grids, character budgets), or occupying without counting (ATP 477)?

- Determine the direction of the mixture (ATP 475): where is smoothing under way, where striating — and by whom?

- Balance translations both ways: where does writing-up overcode the practice, where does it give it "a milieu of propagation" without which it would perish (ATP 486–487)?

- Counter-check: which seemingly smooth spaces (open formats, "free" platforms) are occupied by powers of organisation (ATP 480, 492)?

*Application example:* A doctoral programme is audited: the curriculum striates (modules, ECTS — one counts in order to occupy), the studio seems smooth; but the audit shows that the grant prose, as a translation operation, joins the two — it overcodes the practice *and* procures it circulation (ATP 486–487). Instead of "institution bad, studio free" a differentiated map of directions of mixture emerges; ATP itself supplies the model case from the history of science: the masons' lodge, whose "ground-level plane" is subordinated to the "metric plane of the architect […] on paper and off site" (ATP 368).

*Limits/misuse:* The good/evil axis. The audit measures no degrees of freedom but ratios of mixture; its supreme rule is the plateau's closing warning: "Never believe that a smooth space will suffice to save us" (ATP 500). Whoever uses the audit to demand "smooth" formats because they are smooth repeats the mistake it is meant to expose.

*Failure criterion:* The audit issues in the demand for "more smooth spaces" — the good/evil axis, against the closing warning (ATP 500) — or the translation balance comes out one-sided across all spaces: only overcoding, never propagation. Consistent one-sidedness is a finding not about the institution but about the bias of the audit (ATP 486–487).

### T8 Plateau Format

*ATP grounding:* The concept of the plateau — "a continuous, self-vibrating region of intensities whose development avoids any orientation toward a culmination point or external end" (ATP 22) —, the readability rule "Each plateau can be read starting anywhere and can be related to any other plateau" (ATP 22), the middle rule — "A rhizome has no beginning or end; it is always in the middle, between things, interbeing, intermezzo" (ATP 25) — and the selection criterion of the increase in connections (ATP 508). The formal model is the book itself, up to and including plateau 15, which links every rule by marginal numbers to the plateaus from which it stems, and the opening of plateau 1, which sets a graphic score by Sylvano Bussotti in place of a beginning of text (ATP 3) — a non-discursive entryway as the first entryway.

*Procedure:*

- Organise the documentation (dissertation, exposition, catalogue) in plateaus rather than chapters: modules without a climax-economy, assessed by "intrinsic value" on a plane of consistency rather than by external ends (ATP 22).

- Build each plateau from the middle: do not begin with derivation, do not end with synthesis — "proceeding from the middle, through the middle, coming and going rather than starting and finishing" (ATP 25).

- Mark connection points explicitly: references showing with which other plateaus, works, discourses the module is connectable (model: the marginal numbers of plateau 15).

- Set non-discursive entryways on an equal footing (scores, maps, objects — cf. ATP 3).

- Final test against the criterion: does the form increase the number of really operating connections (ATP 508)?

*Application example:* Michelkevičius's monograph, which wants to be at once a study of and an example of artistic research (Michelkevičius 2018, p. 13), shows the direction and the limit of the format: the claim "form = content" is redeemed where the book argues diagrammatically, and missed where diagrams merely present results. The plateau format demands more: that each module be an entryway of its own into the research — just as the Bussotti score is the first entryway into the rhizome plateau (ATP 3).

*Limits/misuse:* "Technonarcissism": typographic and formal feats do not substitute for the construction of the multiple — "no typographical cleverness […] can substitute for it" (ATP 22), and D&G self-critically concede having missed their own ideal: "We ourselves were unable to do it" (ATP 22). A second trap: the sacralisation of one's own project as a total work — "The unique book, the total work […] all of these platitudes so dear to the avant-gardes" are "still Bibles" (ATP 127).

*Failure criterion:* The connectivity test of the last procedural step is answered with formal features instead of real connections — no actual re-uses, cross-references or entryways can be named that the plateau form generated and a chapter form would not have generated. Then the format is technonarcissism (ATP 22).

### Trial: Protocol and Failure Criterion

With all this, the practicability of the toolkit is rendered plausible, not proven; the constructed examples prove nothing, and this paper does not claim that they do. The proof — or the refutation — has the form of a trial protocol, laid down here in advance so that the trial cannot be reinterpreted after the fact as confirmation.

*Design.* The toolkit is not introduced as a whole but tried in running projects: per project at most two to three instruments according to the instrument economy, selected before the project begins and recorded in the project document. Each application records three things: which decision the instrument touched — or that it touched none; what would presumably have happened without the instrument (a counterfactual note, marked as an estimate); and whether the instrument's failure criterion triggered.

*Evaluation.* After a number of projects fixed in advance — not as needed — a balance is drawn per instrument: did it change a decision at least once? Did its failure criterion trigger? Instruments that across the whole trial only renamed what was happening anyway are struck, not defended (relabeling test, ch. 2). The balance is published regardless of outcome; a toolkit whose trial were documented only in case of success would have violated the caution balance (ch. 7) before it begins.

*Status.* This protocol is part of the toolkit, not its appendix: the building instruction of laying every copy back onto the map (ATP 13) demands a place where it can show itself that a copy has no business on a particular map. The trial is that place. With this, the toolkit takes on the burden of proof that chapter 3 held against the existing literature: the instruments presented here, too, are — until the trial shows otherwise — candidates, not achievements.

## 7. Quality Criteria: How Rhizomatic Research Can Be Tested

A model of artistic research that says nothing about quality would remain academically inconsequential; a model that translates quality into a points-grid would betray its own foundations. *A Thousand Plateaus* allows a third way: the book contains its own selection criteria — it is full of evaluative operations, only they are immanent rather than transcendent. Five criteria can be distilled.

*First, connectivity.* The explicit selection criterion of plateau 15: "What is retained and preserved, therefore created, what consists, is only that which increases the number of connections at each level of division or composition" (ATP 508). Assemblages are expressly selected "according to their ability to draw a plane of consistency with an increasing number of connections" (ATP 513). For a research project this means: not closure or fidelity-to-work counts, but whether the work has produced really operating connections — between materials, practices, discourses, persons — that did not exist before it.

*Second, consistency.* Consistency is "the 'holding together' of heterogeneous elements" (ATP 323): does the work hold the heterogeneous together *as* heterogeneous, without homogenising it? The formula "Never unifications, never totalizations, but rather consistencies or consolidations" (ATP 507) distinguishes this criterion sharply from classical coherence; it becomes testable via the consolidation triad (ATP 328–329): are there intercalated elements, load-bearing intervals, a superposition of disparate rhythms — or only one metre that brings everything into line?

*Third, function-testing instead of meaning-testing.* The question to a work is not what it means but what it functions with, "in connection with what other things it does or does not transmit intensities" (ATP 4). This coincides with de Assis's shift from "What does it mean?" to "How does it work?" (de Assis 2021, pp. 22–23) and makes assessment describable: a review in the sense of this criterion reconstructs wirings and transfers of intensity, not messages.

*Fourth, new-production.* The degree of deterritorialization: does the work bring forth something new, or does it trace? As a textual finding, the diagram supplies the measure — it "does not function to represent, even something real, but rather constructs a real that is yet to come" (ATP 142) — and the translation theory the test-question: is this "an adaptation of an old semiotic, a new variety of a particular mixed semiotic, or the process of creation of an as yet unknown regime" (ATP 138)? That translations *can* be creative (ATP 136) makes the criterion demanding: a transfer, too, can produce the new; the mere gesture of rule-violation can miss it.

*Fifth, the caution balance.* Was the experimentation dosed, was the stratum kept as a working base (ATP 160–161)? The question of the BwO plateau — "What happened? Were you cautious enough?" (ATP 150) — is to be taken seriously as an evaluative question, above all with risky self-experiments and collaborative formats. As negative indicators, plateau 15 even supplies an ironic set of measuring instruments: "conjunctions causing blockages (axiomatics), organizations forming strata (stratometers), reterritorializations forming black holes (segmentometers), and conversions into lines of death (deleometers)" (ATP 513).

Two qualifications. First, these criteria are formative, not merely summative. D&G insist that criteria must "not be used after the fact" but "applied in the course of events" — in the carrying-out of the experiment itself (ATP 251). The becoming plateau demonstrates this with an inconspicuous case: someone begins practising the piano again — Oedipal regression, or "a new borderline, an active line that will bring other becomings"? That is decidable neither in advance nor from outside, but only by whether the line gains consistency, whether the heterogeneous actually functions as symbiosis (ATP 250–251). Evaluation of artistic research would accordingly be primarily process-accompaniment, not final inspection. Second, the criteria are tensioned against one another: connectivity without consistency yields mere accumulation; new-production without the caution balance rewards the line of destruction; function-testing without the connectivity criterion rewards the frictionlessly running. Only in concert do the five criteria form what plateau 15 calls a selective construction rule (ATP 508).

These criteria are qualitative, and they require judgment — that is not a weakness but the point. There is no procedure that mechanically establishes an increase of connection or consistency; the selection is "active and temporary […] which must be renewed" (ATP 10). This is exactly where Roberts's proposal to replace transcendent judgment with the jurisprudence model takes hold: legitimacy and quality are generated case by case, through conceptual work on the individual case, not through subsumption under definitions (Roberts 2015, pp. 231–232). The five criteria are in this sense not a checklist but topoi of a case consultation: they structure the conversation of reviewers, juries and supervisors without replacing it. They thereby differ categorically from scientistic evaluation grids, which extract constants where continuous variation would have to be judged (cf. ATP 369–372) — but also from the mere assertion that artistic quality cannot be judged. That the criteria can themselves congeal into an axiomatic that "blocks all lines" (ATP 143) is their known occupational disease; the counter-medicine is built into criterion five: even evaluation needs a caution balance. And the criteria apply reflexively: the trial protocol at the end of chapter 6 is nothing other than their application to this work's own instruments — what produces no real connections is struck.

## 8. Critical Appraisal and Limits

A model that does not name its own dangers would have learned nothing from *A Thousand Plateaus* — few books build their warnings so systematically into their concepts. Seven problem zones are to be marked.

*(a) The institutionalisation paradox.* The model recommends to artistic research procedures of nomad science — within institutions that structurally operate as "State science." ATP describes precisely what then threatens: State science retains of nomad science only "what it can appropriate" and degrades the rest to formulas "without any real scientific status" (ATP 362); neutralisation often proceeds through promotion — Perronet is appointed director of the school, "a frequently used procedure that inhibited experimentation more than crowning its achievements" (ATP 365). Transposed: the war machine that becomes an administrative authority; the rhizome as module handbook. Structurally, the school can even be read as an apparatus of capture that combines a "general space of comparison" (grades, rankings, portfolios) with a "mobile center of appropriation" (certification, canonisation) (ATP 444) — where it must be expressly noted that this is a structural analogy, not an equation: D&G analyse State and economy, not art schools. The paradox is not solvable, only workable — for instance with the textual reminder that collective bodies always have "fringes or minorities" that reconstitute equivalents of the war machine *within* the institution (ATP 366). Where the appropriation tips, the hard diagnosis holds: at exactly the point where the State apparatus appropriates the war machine, "the line of flight […] turn[s] into a line of death and destruction" (ATP 513).

*(b) The jargon danger.* The Manifesto's critique of the "citation quarry" (*"Zitatsteinbruch"*; Henke/Mersch et al. 2020, p. 12) touches a real finding: Deleuzianism frequently functions in the art field as a rhetoric of legitimation in which "rhizomatic" is an adjective for "somehow networked." ATP knows this danger as its own: Kleist's and Artaud's texts themselves became "monuments, inspiring a model to be copied—a model far more insidious than the others—for the artificial stammerings and innumerable tracings that claim to be their equal" (ATP 378). Nothing guarantees the present toolkit fares better. The only safeguard is procedural: page-exact explication, marked extrapolation, and the readiness to strike concepts where they merely conjure. Whether that succeeds is decided by use, not by declaration — and the use has been made testable: the failure criterion from chapter 2 and the trial protocol from chapter 6 lay down when this safeguard counts as having failed.

*(c) The romanticisation of the nomadic.* The model's vocabulary — nomadic, smooth, minor — invites self-stylisation ("we artists as nomads"). The text forecloses this reading: "It is the nomads, rather, who remain an abstraction, an Idea, something real and nonactual" (ATP 420); "It is not the nomad who defines this constellation of characteristics; it is this constellation that defines the nomad" (ATP 423). The nomad is a functional concept, not an offer of identity. Just as little is there a revolutionary automatism of the smooth: "smooth space and the form of exteriority do not have an irresistible revolutionary calling but change meaning drastically depending on the interactions they are part of" (ATP 387) — up to the closing warning of plateau 14 (ATP 500). And against touristic nomadism kitsch stands the double warning: "It is certainly not enough to travel to escape phantasy" (ATP 379); "We will always be failures at playing African or Indian" (ATP 188).

*(d) D&G's own caveats.* The book does not exempt itself. It concedes its failure at its own ideal of form — "We ourselves were unable to do it" (ATP 22) — and imposes on formal radicality the verdict "Technonarcissism" (ATP 22). It insists that the line of flight carries the worst danger of its own: it can surrender its creative potentials and turn into "a line of destruction pure and simple (fascism)" (ATP 506); the passion for abolition is the fourth and gravest danger (ATP 229–231). And it relocates the analysis of fascism into the molecular: "It's too easy to be antifascist on the molar level, and not even see the fascist inside you" (ATP 215). For artistic research this means: the model is no good for self-reassurance. Whoever works with deterritorialization works with a concept whose tipping points — self-destruction, the microfascism of scenes, exhaustion — lie in the concept itself; the guerrilla clause of plateau 12 formulates the condition: one can only "make war" if one "simultaneously create[s] something else" (ATP 423).

To this is added a concept-hygienic limit of the transpositions themselves. D&G insist that their economic-political concepts are no metaphors — the axiomatic "is far from a metaphor" (ATP 455) — while the transpositions of this work onto the art field (the school as apparatus of capture, funding lines as axioms) are precisely that: structural analogies, which must remain marked as such if the emptying of concepts against which the text works is not to set in. The same holds for the concept of microfascism: it carries the historical signature "1933" and is bound to precise distinctions between fascism, totalitarianism and military dictatorship (ATP 214, 230); its inflationary application to art-world group dynamics would itself be a case of the jargon that point (b) criticises.

*(e) The objections of the field.* Against a Deleuzian toolkit stand six objections, to be taken seriously and not to be argued away — five documented in the field, and a sixth that is only half formulated there and is therefore raised here against this work itself. First, the medium-specificity objection: Van Tuinen and Zepke read Deleuze's logic of sensation as "a rather old-fashioned plea for medium specificity" and precisely not as "art as a conceptual or interdisciplinary research-based practice" (van Tuinen/Zepke 2017, p. 8) — Deleuze as chief witness *against* artistic research. The objection touches a real tension, but it is selective: it rests on the *Francis Bacon* aesthetics, whereas *A Thousand Plateaus* consistently dissolves the medium question into materiality and continuous variation (ch. 4.3). The present work appeals not to an ontology of the medium but to the pragmatics of the plateaus; it is therefore not the proper target of this objection — but it inherits the task of not sliding into a merely interdisciplinary thematic research, against which it warns.

Second, the most radical anti-toolkit thesis, Julian Klein's "The Mode is the Method": "there is no method within artistic research because to research artistically is in itself a method, or, better, a strategy of undertaking research" (Klein 2018, p. 78). If the mode is itself the method, every instrument seems superfluous. It is to be conceded here that Klein is right about the *core* of artistic knowing — no tool produces the "mode of artistic experience". But the toolkit does not claim this: it supplies no method for producing the artistic mode, only operators to map, test and pass on what happens in this mode. Klein's thesis and the toolkit contradict each other only if one equates "method" with "instrument" — an equation ATP itself undoes with its distinction between the striated method-path (ATP 377) and optional rules (ATP 93–94).

Third, the prohibition on application: Deleuze's ideas "cannot be simply applied to practice, as they intervene and disrupt processes of identification and representation" (Attiwill et al. 2017, p. 88); the authors offer lived exempla instead of transferable methods. This objection strikes any toolkit claim at a sensitive point, and it is not rejected here but taken into the construction: exactly for this reason the toolkit is conceived as a copy that, according to the rule of the rhizome plateau, is to be laid back onto the map of the given project (ATP 13), and not as a template. "Deform rather than apply" is the building instruction, not its violation — while it stays open whether a published instrument keeps faith with this self-commitment in use.

Fourth, and most uncomfortable, the irony from the field itself: the SHARE Handbook cites the "rhizomatic project description" as the exemplary case of an over-interpreted self-description that conceals prosaic method-work (Wilson/van Ruiten 2013, p. 278) — Deleuze-jargon as obfuscation, noted of all places in the very handbook that takes up Rajchman's "thinking in art" productively. This objection is justified without reservation and coincides with the jargon danger marked under (b). The only answer is the one already given: page-exact explication rather than incantation, and the readiness to strike "rhizomatic" wherever it merely means "somehow networked" — a readiness the relabeling test (ch. 2) makes testable.

Fifth, the fundamental philosophical critique of Josef Früchtl, who attests of artistic research as a whole that it "still fails in giving a sufficient explanation of itself" (Früchtl 2019, p. 124), and who holds the appeal to non-propositional knowledge to be too unspecific: "the assignation 'non-propositional knowledge' is not specific enough to distinguish artistic research from the impact of art in general" (p. 131). Accurate, too, is his observation that ATP occupies a special position within Deleuze's oeuvre — unlike in *What is Philosophy?*, science there does not proceed in propositions (p. 127). The objection touches the epistemology of the aesthetic harder than it touches the present model, which carries non-propositionality not negatively ("not statement-knowledge") but positively as the asignifying, constructive semiotics of the diagram (ch. 5). But the burden of specification remains: that a process is mappable does not yet say it is specified as *artistic* research — that distinction must be supplied by the aesthetic judgment the model presupposes but does not replace.

Sixth, the discursivisation problem — the objection to be raised against this work itself. All eight instruments produce texts: protocols, journals, audits, formats. A toolkit that defends the non-propositionality of artistic knowledge by producing documents seems to subject the aisthetic to exactly that textualisation which Lesage criticises as a compulsion to supplement (Lesage 2009, pp. 7–8) and Mersch as a re-import of the old hierarchies (Mersch 2022, pp. 158–159); pointedly: the instruments would be the "royal" translation in the house of the nomadic. Three answers, in descending strength. (i) The instruments do not map the work but the research situation: what they discursivise is the assemblage — spaces, lines, regimes, risks —, not the aesthetic event; the exemplar is Deligny's lines, which do not "mean" anything but are recorded (ATP 202–203): mapping without interpreting. (ii) The losses of translation are subject to mandatory accounting in the toolkit itself (T4, final procedural step), and the non-discursive entrances are given equal standing (T8). (iii) It remains to be conceded that this balance, too, is again text. The limit is real, and the model can only declare it, not annul it: the artistic enactment itself need pass through no instrument — the instruments stand beside it, not between it and its right. Where a supervision or review practice nonetheless makes them an obligatory passage, the toolkit is not refuted, but its use has tipped: from map back to copy.

*(f) The economy objection.* It has so far gone unformulated in the field, but it suggests itself: if the quality criteria and instruments are what is actually usable — why then the whole apparatus? Could connectivity, consistency and the caution balance not be had without seven hundred pages of Deleuze? Three answers. *First,* the criteria are defensible only as immanent ones: detached from their provenance, "connectivity" would become a metric and "consistency" a coherence formula — they would reoccupy exactly the transcendent reviewer position against which they are built; the derivation is not an ornament but the grounding of their status as topoi rather than indicators (ch. 7). *Second,* the vocabulary carries its warning layers with it: an "opening" without the distinction between consistency-forming deterritorialization and deterritorialization tipping into the black hole (ATP 334), a "connectivity" without the warning against the blocking axiomatic (ATP 143) would be more dangerous tools, not leaner ones. *Third,* the remainder is to be conceded: for researchers to whom Deleuze is a matter of indifference, the access remains expensive; the toolkit has an entry threshold, and this work lowers it through explication but does not remove it. To that extent the objection stands — as a price tag, not as a refutation.

*(g) What the model does not do.* It does not answer the format questions of doctoral bureaucracy — length requirements, assessment orders, grading scales — and it should not answer them: such struggles are struggles over axioms, and they remain, per ATP, "determining" (ATP 470–471), but they are fought on another level than that of the model. The friction between cartographic research and striated institution vanishes through no toolkit; the model at most makes it describable and negotiable (T7). At least institutionalisation is, following Almeida, no mere doom but an ambivalent, also productive terrain (Almeida 2015, p. 146) — an assessment that converges with D&G's finding that the interaction with "royal" formalisation is constitutive (ATP 367, 374). Just as little does the model replace aesthetic judgment (ch. 7) or the political work on the institutions. It is, in its own language, a map of limited scale — and the limit belongs to the map.

## 9. Conclusion

The opening question was whether a methodologically load-bearing model of artistic research, together with a toolkit, can be won from *A Thousand Plateaus* without betraying the book's anti-methodical character. The answer of this work: it can, if the transposition follows the rules the book itself sets for handling books. The tool-reading is book-internally legitimated — through the function-question (ATP 4), the spelled-out pragmatics (ATP 145–147) and the concrete rules of the closing chapter (ATP 503–510). From the explication of seven concept-complexes a model in six postulates was won — assemblages not works, map not copy, following not reproducing, refrain not phase-plan, becoming not standpoint, caution not transgression — and translated into eight instruments whose quality criteria (connectivity, consistency, function-testing, new-production, caution balance) stay qualitative and demand judgment in the sense of a jurisprudence of the individual case. New, compared with the mere presentation of an instrumentarium, is the status this work gives it: every instrument carries a testable failure criterion, the economy of use is regulated, and a trial protocol fixed in advance determines when instruments are to be struck. The practicability of the toolkit is thereby not asserted but put to the test — with failure named.

The toolkit itself is subject to its own logic: it is a map liable to revision, not a method in the sense of the striated path (ATP 377). The fitting attitude toward it is stated by the close of the refrain plateau, from which this work also takes its reservation: "we can never be sure we will be strong enough, for we have no system, only lines and movements" (ATP 350). A system of artistic research was not the aim; lines and movements that can be continued in concrete projects were.

The outlook is double. Practically, the trial is prefigured by the protocol at the end of chapter 6 — there where Almeida locates the proving ground: in ongoing doctoral and exhibition projects, on documentation and assessment realities — with an open outcome, for undecidability is, per ATP, no defect but a resource: "The undecidable is the germ and locus par excellence of revolutionary decisions" (ATP 473). Theoretically, the dialogue between the epistemology of the aesthetic and the pragmatics of the plateaus, only sketched here, would be worth continuing — as mutual correction, not as synthesis. That both remain risky belongs to the matter. The plateau on becoming ends with a closing chorus that holds fast the double face of every experiment — "WE ARE LOST!/WE ARE SAVED!" (ATP 309) — and perhaps that is the most exact short formula for the state of artistic research: lost and saved at once, and researching only so long as both stay open.

A second, narrower outlook concerns a follow-up project of the author under the title "Error as Method" (*Irrtum als Methode*). Artistic research has repeatedly asserted the productive rank of failure — the Manifesto counts it among the "intrinsic epistemic potential" (*"intrinsischen epistemischen Potenzial"*; Henke/Mersch et al. 2020, p. 40) — without always making it procedurally workable. Two connecting points offer themselves. On the one hand, the line of experimental systems worked out by Michael Schwab and Hans-Jörg Rheinberger, in which knowledge is grasped as a chain of transpositions and the experimental system as a "generator of surprises" (Schwab 2013; Tarasiewicz 2016, p. 345); "progressive" here names the orientation toward a future, not yet attained knowledge (de Assis 2023). On the other hand, the ATP figure of productive derailment: error, disturbance and abandonment are, in the language of the model, not defects but deterritorializations — points at which an assemblage leaves its territory. *A Thousand Plateaus* builds this insight into its concepts when it declares failure to belong to the nature of the plan(e) — "it is of the nature of the plan(e) that it fail" (ATP 269) — and distinguishes the premature deterritorialization tipping into the "black hole" from the consistency-forming one (ATP 334). The toolkit already provides instruments for such a project: the following-journal (T4), which records the material's detours and abandonments, and the lines-and-dangers self-analysis (T6), which makes the tipping of the productive line of flight into exhaustion and destruction visible early. A model that seeks to map error rather than avoid it finds its methodical grammar in the pragmatics of the plateaus — and must at the same time endure Mersch's objection that artistic experiments remain "amethodical" *"weil sie allererst ihre eigene Hypothese sind"* — "because they are, in the first instance, their own hypothesis" (Mersch 2014, typescript p. 22). The trial protocol of this work is already the first application case for this: a toolkit that makes its own failure documentable practises "error as method" on itself before it methodises anything else.

## Appendix: Editorial Note

The textual basis of the ATP quotations is an OCR digitisation of the Massumi edition (University of Minnesota Press 1987). All page numbers were verified against the running heads of the digitisation; quotations crossing a page break are marked with double references (e.g. ATP 12–13). Individual pages missing or truncated in the digitisation (pp. 309, 350, 423, 473, 500) were checked by the project lead directly against the PDF print master; the passages quoted from them count as verified. Obvious OCR artefacts were corrected tacitly (e.g. "black hoes" for "black holes", "deterritorializaton" for "deterritorialization"). Before any print publication, all quotations are to be collated against the printed edition; this applies in particular to passages that sit at a page break in the digitisation. Quotations from the secondary literature follow the sources documented in the reading notes; for Mersch 2015 the reservation on the typescript status noted above applies. The revision of the state of research (ch. 3) rests on a two-stage search: in a first step, around eighty titles from the author's collection were surveyed; in a second, the canonical texts carrying the argument were studied in depth — partly in full text, partly verified only bibliographically; in the bibliography, titles that could not be consulted in full text are given without page references and flagged with a note of uncertainty.
