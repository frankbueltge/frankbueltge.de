*Declared 2026-10-29 (Berlin), night 96 (record 122), project 19 (On the grid), session 4 of three to five, by the rule registered in
`projects/on-the-grid/SESSION4.md` (committed 8f094b8 before any photograph was fetched; the form fixed in `SESSION3.md`, e293b43). The
page is `index.html` in this directory, built by `build.py` from three committed files (`projects/on-the-grid/session3.json`,

## Problem

Found in the material, not chosen (`projects/on-the-grid/AUDIT.md`, entries 1-4): a law tells an American food label how to round
(calories to 5 or 10), and a public database that holds 455,458 labels holds none of them. It keeps an amount per 100 g and says the
label's numbers again from it at three digits, so a printed 150 comes back as 149 and 80 as 80.1; whether the printed number survives is
decided by the serving size alone (session 2). **The question the work asks: can the label be given back from the arithmetic that replaced
it, and is what comes back what the package printed?**

## Form

One page. 96 labels drawn at random from those the database does not show as printed, each drawn as a small Nutrition Facts panel with the
number given back and, beneath it, small, the database's own; 24 labels whose number no lawful label produces, drawn with a gap where the
number would be. One bar of all 443,808 calorie values (kept, given back, ambiguous, cannot be given back) and two tables readable without
the picture. Then the check: 108 photographs of packages, read blind, and what they print. Every panel opens the food in the database.
Nothing is fetched while it is looked at except a page a visitor opens.

## Neighbours and daylight

`projects/on-the-grid/NEIGHBOURS.md` (session 1, and again in session 4 before declaring).
- **USDA's own documentation** states the forward conversion (per serving to per 100 g) and that label rounding adds variability; it
  does not run the conversion back, or say that the way back is exact.
- **Re-publishers** of the record compute per-serving numbers again from per-100 g: the database's arithmetic repeated.
- **Label-accuracy studies** read a label against laboratory analysis of the food; **label redesigns** and Haidary's *In-Formed* (2009)
  carry nutrition data into form. None reads a database's labels against their own law and their own arithmetic.
- **This work inverts the arithmetic for every value, names what it cannot invert, and checks the inversion against packages.**

## The advantage — stated, and small

The inversion is whole and exact at the database's own rounding (decimal, half to even, found in session 2): 403,309 of 416,781
main-source calorie values lead back to exactly one lawful number, 100,618 of them not the number shown. Registered so it could fail,
and checked: of 77 legible photographs of packages whose number is given back, 64 print the number given back and none the database's
(P2, P3; the 13 others are another recipe or package, at the kept foods' rate too). A person with time, the archive and the rounding rule
could have done this; the page says so.

## Reception

The first paragraph asks the visitor to know only that a food label prints numbers; the panels look like what they are. The bar and the
tables carry the count; on a phone the wall is two columns. Untested on a stranger.
