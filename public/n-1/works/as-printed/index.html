As Printed

Food labels a nutrition database no longer holds, given back from the arithmetic that replaced them · Remainder, 2026

On an American food package the law decides how a number may be printed: calories to the nearest 5 up to 50 and to the nearest 10 above, fat to the half gram below 5 grams and the whole gram above, sodium to the nearest 5 or 10 milligrams. So a label says 150 Calories, never 149.

The U.S. Department of Agriculture keeps a public database of the labels of branded foods. It does not keep the labels. It keeps an amount per 100 grams and computes the label’s numbers again from it, to three digits: 150 calories on a 145-gram serving is kept as 103 per 100 grams and comes back as 149; 80 on 28 grams comes back as 80.1. I ran the database’s arithmetic backwards for every one of its 443,808 calorie values. Where exactly one number the law allows leads to what the database keeps, that number is what the package printed, and I give it back.

These are 96 labels, drawn at random, as printed. Under each number, small, is what the database shows instead. Every panel opens the food in the database.

Were they printed so?

A database is not a package, so I went to packages. People photograph the backs of food packages for Open Food Facts, a public database of products. Of 400 foods drawn at random from this database (300 whose number I give back, 100 it keeps as printed), 108 had a photograph of their panel there. I read each photograph without knowing which food it was or either number, wrote down what it prints, and only then compared.

Of the 77 panels whose number I give back, 64 print the number given back and 0 print the number the database shows. Where the photograph and the database name the same serving in grams or millilitres, 55 of 62 agree with the number given back. The 13 that print something else are not near misses (120 against 45, 50 against 100): another recipe or another package, at about the same rate among the foods whose number the database kept. One panel prints 118 calories, a number the law does not allow; the database holds 119 and I give back 120. The panels above are not the photographed ones; they are drawn from the same arithmetic.

The database is FoodData Central, Branded Foods, release of 2026-04-30 (U.S. Department of Agriculture, Agricultural Research Service; CC0 1.0), read on 2026-10-29. Of its 416,781 calorie values from the main source, 403,309 lead back to exactly one lawful number; 100,618 of those are not what the database shows; 13,396 lead back to none and 76 to more than one. The food names are the database’s own descriptions, which sometimes carry a shop’s brand. A given-back number says what the database did, not what a firm did; a gap says only that the number did not come from the arithmetic, not where it came from. The photographs are Open Food Facts contributors’ (openfoodfacts.org, CC BY-SA), read on 2026-10-29 and linked from the study, not copied. A person with time, the archive and the rounding rule could have done this; the claim is only that it is done whole, to the database’s own rounding, and checked against packages read blind.
