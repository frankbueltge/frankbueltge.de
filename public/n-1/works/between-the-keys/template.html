Between the Keys

{{RINGS}} rings of church bells, struck against the pitch orchestras tune to · Remainder, 2026

An orchestra tunes to one note, A = 440 vibrations a second. A ring of church bells tunes to nothing outside its tower. It was cast and tuned, once or a few times, to agree with itself, and it stands where its metal stands: usually somewhere in the hundred cents between two keys of the piano.

Here are {{RINGS}} rings listed in Dove’s Guide for Church Bell Ringers, each sounded by its heaviest bell. Every ring has been moved up or down by whole semitones so that it stands next to A; its place between the keys is kept. Under them all a steady tone sounds the orchestra’s A. A ring that stands on A blends with it. A ring between the keys beats against it, a wavering, faster the further away it stands: about five times a second at a fifth of a semitone, nearly thirteen at the midpoint.

The rings come decade by decade, from the oldest year the record gives to now, each decade in the same few seconds, so a crowded decade is a cloud and an empty one is almost silence. Listen for when the wavering stops, and for when it comes back.

What there is to hear

For six hundred years the rings scatter evenly across the gap: before 1900, half of them beat against the orchestra’s A faster than {{BEAT1900}} times a second, about what rings placed at random would do. In the 1930s, 40s and 50s they gather, but around A = 435, not 440. In the 1970s and 80s, for the only time, a large share stand on the orchestra’s A ({{S1970}} and {{S1980}}), and the wavering thins. Then it returns. Since 1990 most of the rings whose sound was made are old bells retuned ({{OLD1990}} of {{N1990}}, their heaviest bell at least fifty years older than its tuning), and a retuned ring keeps its tower’s own key: those beat a median {{BEATOLD}} times a second, the new ones {{BEATNEW}}.

What this is, and what it is not

Each ring is reduced to one number: where its heaviest bell’s measured nominal (the partial a bell is tuned by) stands between two keys, to the nearest cent. Within a decade the rings at each cent are struck at random moments. The sound is a plain tone with its octave, not a bell: a bell’s heard pitch is made by the ear from several partials and can sit a fraction of a semitone from any of them (Hibbert, 2008), so this is the record’s measurement made audible, not a recording of any tower. The year is a tuning year where the record gives one and the heaviest bell’s casting year otherwise; a bell tuned since without record is heard at its casting. No tower, place or bell can be found in the page: it holds only counts.

What a machine adds here, and it is small. No listener stands in more than one tower at a time, and no ringer hears their ring against an orchestra’s A. This holds {{RINGS}} towers’ pitches in one listening, in the order their sound was made, against one fork, and counts, beside each decade, how many stand on it. A person with time and the file could have done the same; the page says so rather than claim otherwise.

Neighbours

Dove’s Guide charts its bells by nominal, by weight and by year, per bell and per ring, in cents from the named note; it does not place rings against a fixed pitch over time, or sound them. William Hibbert’s studies measure the partials of chosen historic peals. Works of sound art have long made beating their material, slow interference between close fixed tones (Alvin Lucier, Phill Niblock; named from memory, not looked up tonight). The Atlas of Data Art holds sonifications of climate and weather and no work on bells. None found sounds many towers’ measured pitches against a standard.
