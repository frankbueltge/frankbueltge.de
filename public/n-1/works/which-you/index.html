Which You

Thirty English sentences and every translation people made of them · Remainder, 2026

English says you to one person or to many, to a friend or to a stranger, with the same word. Most of the languages below cannot: their translators had to choose. Here are thirty English sentences, drawn at random from Tatoeba, a collection of sentences translated by volunteers, and around each one every translation people made of it. On the left, you became one close person; on the right, a stranger, or more than one. A line is a link a person made between two of the translations.

Across the thirty

In 26 of the thirty sentences, the translators chose both ways. Of 333 pairs of translations of the same sentence that chose the same you, 51 have been linked to each other by someone. Of 284 pairs that chose differently, 2, both at one sentence in Lingua Franca Nova, whose tu says only “one person”, not “close”. Everything else that crosses the choice is joined only through the English.

No translation here is wrong. Each is a faithful answer to a question the English left open; two of them disagree only because the English never asked.

How it was made

The thirty sentences were drawn by a fixed seed from the 76,757 English sentences in Tatoeba’s export of 17 October 2026 that hold you and have between five and twelve translations into languages other than Esperanto, whose vi also does not choose. Who each of the 231 translations speaks to, in 39 languages, was decided by one reader, a machine, in one pass, without seeing the English sentence, which sentence a translation belonged to, or which had been linked. Those readings are estimates; 39 were made with little confidence in the language. Where a form could also be read as “he” or “she” (Italian Lei), it was read as the polite you; where a sentence shows politeness only by its tone and not by a you (Japanese, Tagalog po), it was read as not saying. On French and Russian, where word lists checked by hand decide, the reading agreed with them every time (52 of 52).

What this does that a person with time would not. One reader held thirty-nine languages at once and decided, for every translation, whom it speaks to, before seeing which ones people had joined. A team of readers could divide the sheet among them; the readings would then be many people’s, and the blindness would have to be arranged. That is the whole of the claim, and it is a small one.
