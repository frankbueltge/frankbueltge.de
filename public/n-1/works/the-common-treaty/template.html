The Common Treaty

Nine human rights treaties, and what in them binds every state that joined · Remainder, 2026

A state may join a treaty and, in the same act, hold back part of it. What is left that binds every state that joined is smaller than the treaty, and it changes each time a state joins with a reservation or withdraws one. Each row below is one of the nine core United Nations human rights treaties; each square is one of its obligations. A square is lit when, in the year shown, no party holds it back.

What changed

In 2014, {{C14}} of the {{NPROV}} obligations the file codes bound every party to their treaty. On the depositary pages of October 2026, read entry by entry, {{C26}} of those {{C14}} still do, and {{RET}} more are common now that were not in the file: in one, Oman’s withdrawal on the Child’s 9.4; in the other, the United Kingdom’s on Women’s 10(c), withdrawn in 1996 though the file still codes it. In all nine treaties at least one party holds a reservation that names no article at all: the treaty applied subject to a constitution, a law or a religion. Whether such a reservation covers any one square is a question of law; it is shown beside the row and not decided here.

No reservation on this page is judged lawful or unlawful, and no state is ranked. A square goes dark for a single party: in 2014, 160 of the 262 dark squares were held back by three parties or fewer. A declaration that only interprets an obligation darkens it as a reservation does, because the file counts both.

How it was made

The obligations, parties and reservations to 2014 are those coded by K. Zvobgo, B. Shannon and C. Eldredge in their dataset of reservations, understandings and declarations to the nine treaties (Harvard Dataverse, doi:10.7910/DVN/GURSZB, CC0). A treaty is shown from the first year it had 20 parties; before that, fewer states than that cannot stand for “every”. Only what the file codes is here: the clauses on disputes and on the treaty committees are not in it and not on the page. A party holds back an obligation when it has a reservation or a declaration on it, as the file counts them, not withdrawn by that year.

The last step is read from the record, not the file. On the United Nations Treaty Collection pages for the nine treaties, every entry that could break what was common in 2014 ({{READ}} entries) was read by one reader, a machine, in one sitting, and decided by hand against the obligations it could break; those decisions are estimates. A reservation that names an article without a paragraph is taken to hold back every paragraph of it, which accounts for many of the outlined squares; counting only entries that name the paragraph itself, and leaving out statements made only on signing, {{C26B}} of the {{C14}} would remain. The entries a filter set aside were checked by a blind draw of twenty; read by hand, none of the twenty held back a common square. Squares not common in 2014 were not read again, except where every holder had withdrawn; they stay as the file has them.

What this does that a person with time would not. An intersection is broken by one exception, so a sample cannot show it: every entry that could break it has to be read. One reader read all of them on nine pages in one sitting. A person with time could do the same in days. That is the whole of the claim, and it is a small one.

Nearest neighbours: the dataset’s authors, who code and model reservations and their withdrawal (Boyes, Eldredge, Shannon, Zvobgo, British Journal of Political Science 54(1), 2024, doi:10.1017/S0007123423000339), and the Human Rights Committee’s General Comment 24 (1994) on which obligations of the Covenant may not be reserved.
