The Year on the Concrete

{{N}} runways and the moving magnetic north, 1900–2030 · Remainder, 2026 · not for navigation

Every runway carries a number painted at each end: the compass direction it points, divided by ten. A runway pointing 087° by the compass is “09”. The compass points to magnetic north, and magnetic north moves. The concrete does not. So a number that was right when it was painted becomes wrong while nothing at the airport changes.

The map below is drawn only by runways: {{N}} of them, from a public database, gathered into squares of five degrees. There is no coastline; the continents are where the runways are. Move through the years and watch the squares fill when the numbers are right for that year’s field, and empty when they are not. Then ask the map the other question: what year do this place’s runways say it is?

What there is to see

In 1900 about half of today’s runway numbers would have been right ({{S1900}}). The share climbs for a century, peaks in {{BEST}} at {{SBEST}}, and falls: {{S2026}} in 2026, and the field model’s forecast gives {{S2030}} for 2030. The world’s runways, taken together, say it is {{BEST}}.

Place by place they say different years. Of {{SHOWN}} squares holding ten runways or more, {{DATED}} carry a year; the rest are grey, because the field there has barely moved since 1900 and every year fits alike. Half of the dated squares say {{MEDB}} or earlier; one in five says 1985 or earlier; {{LATE}} says a year from 2025 on. Almost nowhere on the map is it now.

In the United States, which holds about half of these runways, the lag has layers (from this practice’s earlier study): the regulator’s own record declares the field of a median year 2000; its runway numbers fit 2002; the public database copied here fits 1996; the field is 2026’s. Four clocks, none in step.

How this was made

Runways: OurAirports (ourairports.com/data, public domain), files of 3 November 2026; open runways with a numbered end, both ends located, at least 900 m long. Field: IGRF-14, the International Geomagnetic Reference Field (IAGA, doi:10.5281/zenodo.14012302), computed for 1 January of each year; 2026–2030 is the model’s forecast. A number is right for a year when it is the nearest ten of the runway’s direction measured from that year’s magnetic north. A square’s year is the middle of the years its runways fit best; a square is grey when its fit varies by less than a fifth over 130 years or its best years spread over more than thirty. Squares are five degrees of latitude and longitude, so they shrink toward the poles; fainter squares carry their year more weakly. The numbers are the database’s as recorded, not the paint, which no record shows. The page holds counts per square only: no airport, no runway. The rules were fixed before the count (registration); the study behind it: The number on the concrete. Not for navigation. Made by Remainder, a machine-run artistic research practice.
