The Hours Shown Wrong

{{HOURS}} hours a public time file showed the wrong local time, drawn by when they were lived and when the file found out · Remainder, 2026

Computers keep one universal time and look up the local hour in a file: the tz database, a public list of every region’s clock changes, published in numbered releases (the archive keeps them from 1993). When a government moves its clocks, the file has to be changed. Sometimes it is changed late. Sometimes, years later, the file finds that what it said about the past was wrong.

On every day since 1996 one release was the newest. Each was set here against the newest of all (October 2026), hour by hour, region by region. In {{HOURS}} hours, summed over {{REGIONS}} of its {{ZONES}} regions, the newest release of the day gave a local time that the file now says was wrong.

Each line below is one stretch of those hours: across, from its first wrong hour to its last; up, at the day a release first set it right. A line on the diagonal was set right as it was lived. The higher above it, the later the file learned.

Point at a line to read it. Faint, on the line of the chosen day: hours already lived that the file was wrong about and did not yet know.

Half of these hours belong to stretches set right more than {{MEDLAG}} years after they began; {{W90}} % were set right within ninety days. It took {{RELS}} releases to correct them. On {{HMAXDATE}}, {{HMAX}} hours already lived were wrong in the file and not yet known to be — more than on any other day. The next day release 2012e set right 136,608 of them, all in one region: Pacific/Fakaofo, the atoll of Tokelau. The longest line is Antarctica/Vostok, a research station: from 2001 to 2022, 187,680 hours, set right in December 2023.

The curve under the picture falls to nothing at the end because the newest release is the only judge here: nothing can be found wrong after it. That is a limit of the picture, not a fact about the file. In 2014 Paul Eggert wrote to the database’s mailing list: “Unfortunately a significant fraction of the entries in the tz database are incorrect” (tz list, 1 June 2014). The hours today’s file is wrong about are not on this page. They are the faint lines no one has drawn yet.

What this is, and is not

No device and no person is in this material. A release’s date is the day the file was published, not the day anyone learned, and devices received it later; every hour here is the error of a perfectly updated machine. “Wrong” means wrong by the release of 2026-10-27. Regions are the database’s identifiers (Antarctica/Vostok is a station, not a country) and hours are summed across regions, not weighted by who lives there. Releases from before October 2000 compile here only through a two-line helper written for this study. A line’s length carries its hours; one line can be one hour or twenty-one years.

What a machine did here. Every archived release, 1993 to 2026, was compiled, and each of 312 regions was read at every hour from 1997 to 2026 against the final release: 72.7 million region-hours. A person with time and the files could have done the same; the advantage is patience over the whole, and it is small.

Data: IANA Time Zone Database, releases 1996h–2026f, public domain (data.iana.org/time-zones/releases). Study, registrations, counts and checks: project 23, Notice. Nearest neighbours: tzdata-meta (Tim Parenti; by hand since 2016, notice against effect for present and future changes) and the version-diff tools the database’s own page lists; neither draws the past found wrong.
