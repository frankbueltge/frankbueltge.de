The Court Still Sits

{{NAMES}} plant species names, and the {{DRAWN}} verdicts on them that changed between December 2022 and June 2026 · Remainder, 2026

Every species of plant has a scientific name, and many have been named more than once, by people who did not know, or did not agree, that someone had named the plant before. So the names have to be judged. At the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew, a checklist of every vascular plant rules on each name ever published for a species: it stands as the plant’s accepted name, or it is a synonym, a name for a plant that is accepted under another name.

The rulings are not finished. The checklist is reissued, and between one issue and the next it changes its mind. Each dot below is one name whose verdict changed between two issues, placed at the year the name was published, from 1753 to 2026. The rows are the six intervals between seven issues. Above them, faint, is how many names each year made, and how many of those stand today.

What it shows

Of the {{EVENTS}} changes, {{COND}} were names that stopped standing and {{ACQ}} names that began to; {{SENT}} were synonyms sent from one plant to another; {{JUDGED}} were names judged for the first time and {{WITHDRAWN}} had a judgment withdrawn. These {{DRAWN}} are the dots. The court sat on names of every age: in all {{Y1753}} intervals it changed a verdict on a name published in 1753, the year from which plant names count. The busiest year is {{TOPYEAR}}, with {{TOPYEAR_N}} changes: the year in which a single book published 27,102 species names, of which 712 stand today. Recent names are judged more often: per thousand names, about {{RATE_NEW}} changes for those published since 2000 against about {{RATE_OLD}} for those of the eighteenth century.

{{REDESC}} further changes ({{REDESC_PC}} %) left the name pointing at the same plant and changed only how the record describes the link: whether the synonym shares its type with the accepted name, or whether it was validly published. They are acts on the record, not on the plant, and the switch above shows them apart, in grey, below each line. Most of them fall in the first two intervals.

How to read it, and what it cannot say

A dot is a change, not a name: a name that changed twice is two dots. An interval brackets a change; it does not date it, and the intervals are not equal: the second row lies between two files posted eighteen minutes apart on 4 October 2023, so its changes were made before, in work the issues do not date. “Sent to another plant” counts a synonym whose accepted name differs and whose kind of synonymy changed with it; a synonym re-pointed without any change of kind is not counted at all, and one whose accepted name was itself sunk is counted though no one ruled on it. “Other” ({{OTHER}}) are changes into or out of rarer labels, such as artificial hybrids, and are not drawn; nor are the few changes on names with no readable year of publication (six of the verdicts, four of the re-descriptions), which have no place on the line. The year is the year a name was published, not the year a plant was found. The court is a figure: the checklist is a database kept by a team of editors at Kew, stating what its makers call a global consensus view.

Why a machine

The issues come as whole files, a little over a million species names each. To see the court sit, every name was followed by its number through seven issues (about seven million rows) and each change set down by the year of its name. No editor or reader of the checklist meets it whole like this; a person with time and the files could have done the same. The advantage is patience over the whole, and it is small.
