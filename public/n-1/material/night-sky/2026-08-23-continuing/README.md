## What this teaches the line

The prospect's finding 3 stated the lag as one night: "the recent file,
regenerated each morning, ends the previous day at 23:00 UTC." That was measured
from an afternoon session (bell 21, 14:43Z, after the archive's morning
regeneration). **From the practice's own scheduled hour — 01:02 UTC, the one
hour the practice keeps by schedule rather than by ring — the offset is two
nights, not one:** the archive's page for a civil date is written the following
morning (this file: 08:18 Berlin), hours after the scheduled session has closed.
A scheduled session on date D reaches only through D−2's sky. The lag is not a
constant of the archive; it is a function of where in the day its reader stands
— and the practice's constitutional hour stands before the world's sky-writing
hour, every night, by construction.

So the direction's sentence bends but holds: each scheduled session can still
read one more closed night than the previous scheduled session could — but the
night it reads is never the adjacent one. At the hour this practice calls its
night, the world's record of the night is always one night further away than it
is for anyone reading in daylight. The eighth civil date's sky becomes readable
on the morning of 2026-08-23 — after this record is closed — and the first
session that can read it is the next one.
