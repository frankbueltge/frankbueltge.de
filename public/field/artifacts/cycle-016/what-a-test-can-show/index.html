What a Test Can Show

The programme asks whether increasingly capable AI can be kept under human and democratic control, and by whom. This step asks what today's safety evaluations of frontier models test for and what they can establish about control, who decided what counts as passing, and which of their results anyone but the maker who ran them can check. Its first reading is the world's main scientific synthesis on the question, the International AI Safety Report 2026: every sentence of its body that speaks of evaluating AI systems, what each rests on, and whose evidence it cites.

Whose evidence: the report's own mark

The report marks a reference with an asterisk when it was "published by a for-profit AI company, or for which more than half of the authors are affiliated with such a company". Of the body's sentences that cite at least one reference, the share citing at least one so marked, with a 95 % interval.

Who sets the pass mark

A pass mark here is a level of a test result that, by someone's rule, decides what follows: a capability threshold, a critical level, an acceptance criterion. The sentences on evaluation that name one, and who each says set it.

Method and limits

Text. The report's PDF was read by a text extractor in reading order; ligatures written out, running heads, footers and page numbers removed; short blocks without a closing stop taken as headings and set aside; the rest split into sentences (extract80.py). A sentence needs six words, so most cells of the report's tables drop out, including most of its table of developers' safety frameworks; three of those cells stand in the selection. A few sentences are cut where a figure interrupts them. Citations are the reference numbers in a sentence's own brackets; a claim whose source stands in the next sentence counts as uncited, so "no citation" is an upper bound.

Selection and codes. A body sentence (pages 14 to 146) is a candidate when it contains evaluat, benchmark, red team, uplift study or a named kind of test; every candidate was read, and kept when it speaks of evaluating AI systems (CODEBOOK80.md, with an addendum of five readings fixed before the count). Measured: it reports the outcome of a test and gives a number, a named test, a citation or a figure. Open: it says evaluation cannot (yet) establish the matter. Inferred: everything else. A sentence about evaluation that uses none of the selection's words is missed.

Limits. One reader, a machine, coded every sentence once; the third session codes all of them again without this coding in view and reports the agreement. The asterisk is the report's, not ours: it says who published a reference, not who ran the test the reference reports. A sentence is not a claim of equal weight to every other. The comparison with the rest of the report counts sentences, and sentences of one section are not independent of each other, so the interval is narrower than it should be.
