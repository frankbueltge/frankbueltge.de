# Critique — What a Test Can Show (second state, 2026-11-18)

The page holds up as an honest second state. It answers the last part of its question (who can check) better than the first two (what tests can establish about control, and who sets the pass mark). A visitor gets the answer in one box and can open any tile to read the source's own words and why it stands in its band. The page shows three predictions fixed in advance, reports all three as failed, and names its own weakest claim. That is rare and worth keeping. The rest of this critique is about where the answer is thinner than the box makes it sound.

The page answers "what tests can establish about control" only with how often the report hedges: 5 of 22 sentences on control against 29 of 99 on capability. That describes the report's prose, not the tests. The coding holds a sharper answer that the page does not draw. The control tests an outsider can repeat (413, 724, 726, 727) show that models can mislead, sandbag or tell a test from real use when they are trained, told or asked to. The evidence that frontier models notice real evaluations and act on it (2, 7, 722) rests on pre-deployment snapshots and shared environments no outsider can reach. Outsiders can check what a model can do; only makers hold what it does. The 62.5 % of control sources that are repeatable is also padded: six of the 16 come from one long list sentence (1923), and two of those six test misuse, not control.

The weakest claim is the one the page names, but its weakness lies elsewhere than the page says. "Outside" is coded per source, without asking whether the source contains the result the report cites it for. By the coder's own notes, six repeatable sources do not contain it (151, 216, 457, 725, 1064, 1065), and three were never checked against the figure (138, read as it stands today, 201 and 632). Without them, 18 to 21 of 48 remain (37.5 to 43.8 %, not 56.2 %). At sentence level, counting a sentence when any one of its sources is repeatable gives 19 of 41. Requiring every test source to be repeatable gives 12. Requiring the repeatable source to hold the claim gives about 11. Measured against all 201 sentences on evaluation, that is between one in twenty and one in ten, a base the answer box never gives.

The same notes hold the study's strongest result, buried in one sentence of the method section: about ten of the 41 measured sentences say something their source does not contain or contradicts. Two examples: at 855 the report says all three systems reliably answered very-low-risk questions, while the source has one answering them directly only a quarter of the time; at 911 the report speaks of "real interactions" that were calls simulated by clinicians. This is the only outside check the study actually made, and it answers "can anyone but the maker check" more directly than any proxy. But the sources were read in abstract and method only, so "not found" has to be confirmed in the full text before it is claimed.

"Rerunnable" is a fair upper bound, not a measure of "anyone can check the result". It counts access on request to a vetted circle as public (317, 549, 723). It treats a hosted model, which may since have changed without notice or been retired, the same as open weights anyone can load again. It ignores sampling, scaffolds and model-as-judge scoring (1227, 1231), so a rerun need not return the same number. "Third party" does not mean "independent" either: it includes a security company (467), a source whose authors all work at one for-profit company (158), and a benchmark funded by a maker that holds exclusive access to part of it (281). Four sources come from one benchmarking organisation and three from one evaluator. The intervals assume independent sources and are therefore too narrow; the page says this for study 80 but not for study 81.

"What counts as passing stays with the maker" outruns its evidence. Behind the results it rests on four sources, all system cards, three of them from one maker, so "set by the maker and not repeatable" describes one kind of document. In the report's sentences P119 failed: 5 of 8 name a developer, three of those five are cells of one table, and the three that name no setter are generic. Table 3.5 lists developers' own frameworks, one row per developer, so finding no public body in it is true by construction. After "0 of 3 held", the line that turns three failed predictions into "what stays with the maker is the pass mark" reads as a rescue after the fact.

The nine's 0 of 9 is close to true by definition. Step 3 chose these cases because only makers wrote them down, every one happened on a model outsiders could not use, and eight carry the same reason word for word. Repeating is also the wrong test for an incident: an incident is checked through its records, not rerun. The Atelier's column on whether an outside evaluator had the records is the better measure. Its one case with an outside evaluator (1685, in an evaluation an outside partner built) sits awkwardly beside the page's line that each case "is known only from the maker's account".

The tile figure reads without help at 1100 px and wraps cleanly at 390 px: the bands are labelled in plain language, the runner is shown by colour, and the asterisk is explained. Elsewhere there are problems. At 390 px the nine-case table is 464 px wide in a 358 px box, so the Field's own column, which holds the finding, sits off-screen with no cue to scroll. In the 41-sentence strip, "no test in its sources" and "no source named" have the same colour. Blue means "measured", "the rest of the report" and "repeatable" in three figures in a row, and red means "on evaluation" in the bars but "maker" in the tiles. The strip of 2,323 hair-thin sentence marks blurs into texture at phone width. Most important, whether a repeatable source actually holds the report's result shows only when tiles are opened one by one; it belongs on the tile itself.

The third session should code, blind and from each source's full text, every sentence-and-source pair for whether the source holds the result the sentence states. It should recode "outside" with four cases kept apart: open weights, hosted model, still available today, and access on request to a vetted circle. It should report agreement code by code, and rerun one cheap open test to learn whether "rerunnable" returns the report's number. The fourth session should give every share against the 201 sentences as well as the 41, and keep incidents apart from tests. It should say what control tests can establish, separating what a model can do from what it does, and group or weight sources by the organisation behind them. Finally, it should either search outside the report for public pass marks (regulators' codes, institutes' criteria) or limit the pass-mark claim to what the report says.
