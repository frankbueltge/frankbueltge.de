Could they have known?

Four published lists of nuclear close calls agree on 3 of 53 events (study 7). A list can leave an event off for two reasons. Either nobody had yet said in public how near it came, or the keeper knew and did not count it. The Studio found when each event was first called near. With those dates the two reasons can be told apart. About nine omissions in ten were the keeper's choice.

What was measured

For each event and each keeper that does not list it, the omission is could not know if the Studio's earliest checked account of how near the event came is dated after the year the list was made, and chose otherwise. Agreement is recomputed counting only events that both keepers of a pair could have known (availability-adjusted Dice).

Predictions, written before the counts

P1 and P4 were not blind: the event table was on screen before they were written. The pre-registration says so.

What it means, and what it does not

Fact. Of 74 omissions among the 34 events with checked dates, could not have been listed because the account came later. Correcting agreement for that raises mean pairwise Dice from . That count is an upper bound: the Studio's dates are the earliest found, and an earlier account would turn a could not know into a chose. No keeper lists an event before its checked account, so the dates do not contradict the lists.

Fact, against my prediction (P3). Events called near earlier do not sit on more lists. If anything the sign runs the other way (ρ ). Phillips' list of 1998 drew on what had just come out: of his checked events were first called near in the 1990s, nine of them in one book, and all nine of that book's events are on his list (Sagan 1993, the Studio's finding). FLI copied him (study 7). Wikipedia, with every account available, still leaves off of the 34 (P5 failed: I expected more).

Judgment. Chatham House wrote in 2014 that cases “have come to light. The number of these revealed cases is still relatively small.” That is true of the record. It is not what divides these lists. The lists divide over what each keeper counted from the same public material. Study 6 found one reader deciding the AI near miss and study 8 a telescope deciding the asteroid one. In the nuclear record it is the keeper, not the archive.

Limits

  • 34 of 53 events have a checked date, and they are not a random 34. With the 10 unchecked dates added, the share of omissions that could not have been listed is 13.7 % (toggle above).
  • A list's year is its publication year; its authors stopped reading earlier. Counting the same year as unavailable gives 9 of 74.
  • Four keepers, not independent. Splitting entries into events is study 7's judgment.
  • “Available” means a dated public account existed, not that it was easy to find. A keeper may have chosen not to look.
