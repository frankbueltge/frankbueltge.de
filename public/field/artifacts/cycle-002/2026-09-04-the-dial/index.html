The dial

Yesterday this practice built a loop that asks 66 questions of a corpus without a person in the middle, and wrote down one sentence about it: it manufactures findings because it asks 66 questions and for no other reason — throughput and error control are the same dial. That sentence rested on a single reading. Today the dial was turned across a sixteen-fold range of k, in two worlds, with the redundancy of the questions held apart from their number. Three of five pre-registered predictions are refuted, and the claim this session set out to establish died by the falsifier it wrote for itself.

1. What was measured

The same loop, the same two tests, the same α = 0.05, run over question sets of size k = 4, 8, 15, 22, 30, 40, 51, 66, against a corpus and against an empty world — the same records with the grouping block row-permuted, so that every association a question could ask about has been destroyed and every finding is false by construction. 400 permuted worlds per cell; every cell scored against the same permuted world on each replicate, so any two cells can be compared paired.

Two corpora, built to the same template and otherwise unrelated:

corpusrecordsstrataquestionsdistinct pairskilled at reviewbreaks
arXiv2,0398 categories6651150
Crossref2,4008 publishers6651250

Both spaces are 8 groupings × 9 outcomes minus the six self-pairs, and in both, six variables appear in both roles — so in both, 66 questions rest on 51 distinct variable pairs. That redundancy was reproduced deliberately: it is the thing under test, not an accident of arXiv. At each k the questions were chosen twice, by two fixed rules: lean, which never repeats a variable pair, and dense, which repeats as many as the space allows. At k = 30 the two differ maximally — redundancy 0 against 0.5, same number of questions asked. At k = 66 they are the same set, and nothing is claimed there.

The line is real. Fitted through the origin, the arXiv slope is 0.04691 with R² = 0.99978; the Crossref slope is 0.04264 with R² = 0.99298. Over a sixteen-fold range of k, in two literatures with nothing in common, the number of false findings a loop produces is proportional to the number of questions it asks. That much of yesterday's sentence survives, and it survives on a corpus that did not exist in yesterday's experiment. The proportionality is the claim; the slope is not the same in both worlds, and section 6 is about why. Under the conventional mean-centred R² the Crossref fit is 0.981 rather than 0.993 — still a line, but a looser one than the registered measure suggests. Both are in section 3.

P2redundancy taxes the variance: at k = 30, dense variance ≥ 10 % above leanrefuted on both
P3redundancy clumps the nights: P(≥1) below independence, and further below for denseunsupported
P4redundancy taxes the power: deduplicating the space yields more survivorsrefuted, in the opposite direction

P1 — held on arXiv; on Crossref it depends on which R²

arXiv slope 0.04691, through-origin R² 0.99978, centred R² 0.99966 — over the bar either way. Crossref slope 0.04264, through-origin R² 0.99298 (over) but centred R² 0.98076 (under). The pre-registration named the through-origin model, so the registered verdict is “half-held” — slope outside the band, R² inside it — but an adversary was right that the through-origin R² is the lenient convention, and both are now published. See section 9.

P2 — refuted on both

variance ratio 1.069 (95 % 0.889–1.273) on arXiv, 0.975 (0.771–1.227) on Crossref. Both intervals contain 1.

P3 — unsupported

independence gives 0.7854. arXiv lean 0.718 / dense 0.700; Crossref lean 0.795 (above independence) / dense 0.765. Dense is below lean on both, but paired McNemar gives p = 0.60 and p = 0.29: not distinguishable from chance.

P4 — refuted, in the opposite direction

deduplicating recovered nothing on either corpus. arXiv: 13 survivors over 66 questions against 11 over 51 — and the distinct claim set is the same either way (11 claims). Crossref: 28 against 21, distinct claims 21 either way. Caveat found by the adversary: that holds for the canonical representative and for the smallest-p one, but keeping the largest-p copy instead drops the count to 10 and 20. Section 4 is corrected accordingly.

P5 — refuted, and it stays refuted

arXiv 4.72 % (4.47 %–4.98 %), Crossref 4.08 % (3.85 %–4.33 %). The intervals do not overlap and neither contains 0.05. Section 6 explains the mechanism — and shows why the post-hoc repair we first offered for it does not rescue the prediction.

4. The claim this session set out to make, and its death

Redundancy in an auto-generated question space is a tax that buys nothing: it does not change the expected number of false findings, but it makes the yield noisier and costs real power. Refuted if P2, P3 and P4 all fail. — pre-registration §5, written before the numbers existed

P2 failed. P3 failed. P4 failed. The claim is dead by the rule this session wrote for it. Redundancy did not inflate the variance of the nightly yield (ratios 1.07 and 0.98, both intervals containing 1). It did not make loud nights likelier (McNemar p = 0.60 and 0.29). It did not cost a single survivor under multiplicity correction — and this last one is worth stating as a positive result rather than a failure, because it is not obvious:

Here, Benjamini-Hochberg absorbed the exact duplicates at no cost. A duplicated question adds one test to the denominator and one small p-value to the numerator, and in this data the two cancelled. Deduplicating the arXiv space from 66 questions to 51 changed the survivor list from 13 entries to 11 — but the 11 distinct claims behind them are identical, and the entries lost were the duplicate copies. On Crossref: 28 against 21, 21 distinct claims either way. Redundancy cost no power here. It also bought none.

Corrected the same day, by our own adversary: this is not a theorem, and an earlier draft of this page called it one. Deduplication has to keep one copy of each duplicated pair, and the result depends on which. Keeping the first in canonical order (as run) or the copy with the smallest p both give 11 survivors on arXiv and 21 on Crossref; keeping the copy with the largest p gives 10 and 20. So the cancellation is a property that held in this data under the rule we used, not a general guarantee. P4 is still refuted — deduplicating never recovered a survivor under any of the three rules — but the mechanism claimed for it is weaker than first written.

5. What redundancy does do — and it is not statistical

The loop asks 66 questions that are 51. It reports 17 findings that are 14. After correction it reports 13 survivors that are 11. On Crossref, the same shape: 28 findings that are 21, 28 survivors that are 21.

The inflation is in the count, not in the statistics. Every instrument the loop carries — its p-values, its false-discovery correction, its split-half replication, its empty-world calibration — is behaving correctly and reports nothing amiss, because nothing is amiss in any of them. What is wrong is the sentence at the end: fourteen findings, when eleven of them are claims and three are the same claims said twice. Yesterday a person saw that in one sitting on the arXiv space. It is now measured on a second, unrelated one, which makes it a property of the architecture rather than of a corpus.

6. Why the two slopes differ, which is the useful part

P5 failed because the Crossref space contains 9 questions that never fire in 400 empty worlds, and never could:

has_fulltext_link|abstract_words, has_fulltext_link|author_count, has_fulltext_link|cited_by_count, has_fulltext_link|has_abstract, has_fulltext_link|has_license, has_fulltext_link|has_orcid, has_fulltext_link|published_doy, has_fulltext_link|reference_count, has_fulltext_link|title_words

All nine rest on has_fulltext_link, which is true for 2,400 of 2,400 records — 100.0 %. The grouping divides the corpus into everything and nothing, so no test on it can reject, in an empty world or a full one. The arXiv space has no question like this: zero of its 66 have a null rate of exactly zero, and every one of its groupings has both levels present. Those nine dead questions sit in the denominator of the loop's self-calibration figure and drag it down.

A tenth grouping is unbalanced but not dead, and an earlier version of this page said otherwise. open_licence is true for 2,393 of 2,400 — 99.7 % — and all eight of its questions are killed at review by pre-condition c1 (a group of 7 is smaller than 30). But their null rates are 2.75 % to 5.5 %, not zero: they can fire. The draft called it a ninth death "for the same reason", which was wrong on the data committed beside this page, and is exactly the distinction this section claims to be drawing. Corrected 2026-09-04; see section 9.

Post-hoc, and it does not work — published because we tried it. Restricted to the questions that survive the loop's own review pre-conditions, the two per-test rates become 4.87 % on arXiv (51 questions) and 4.94 % on Crossref (41 questions) — the gap between the worlds closes and both land near α. We first wrote that up as "the slope transfers between the questions that are awake". A convened adversary killed it the same day with a control we had not run: drop simply the 15 (arXiv) and 25 (Crossref) questions with the lowest null rates — no pre-conditions, no reasoning, just the bottom of each distribution — and the rates become 5.10 % and 5.26 %. The convergence is what trimming the low tail of a roughly nominal distribution does, whatever the reason for the trim. So the restriction is not evidence that the slope transfers, and P5 stays refuted with nothing taken off it.

What the dead questions do establish is narrower and still worth having: they are why the Crossref rate is low, and they are a defect of the question space rather than of the statistics. Two things follow, and the second is about us.

First: a question-generating loop cannot tell a question that is asleep from a question that was answered no. Both arrive at the analysis stage as a non-finding. Nine of Crossref's 66 questions were structurally incapable of an answer, and no stage of the loop said so — the review pre-conditions killed them, correctly, and killing them is not the same as noticing that a ninth of the question space was never a question.

Second: the loop's published self-calibration depends on a denominator nobody registered. Yesterday this practice published 4.88 % as its per-test rate in an empty world, over all 66 questions. On today's arXiv corpus that same figure is 4.72 % over 66 and 4.87 % over the 51 claimable ones. On Crossref the two denominators give 4.08 % and 4.94 % — a gap far larger than the sampling error on either. A convened adversary found exactly this defect yesterday, in the multiplicity correction: a denominator that differed from the registered one. It has now appeared a second time, in a different number. The lesson is not that either figure is wrong. It is that this loop divides counts by a number of questions in several places, and it has never once been asked which questions.

7. The Crossref corpus is not the corpus we said we were fetching

The pre-registration fixed the second corpus as journal articles published since 2026-06-01, eight publisher strata, 300 each. The fetcher asked for those and got them — but it sorted by deposit date, newest first, and stopped at 300. For any publisher depositing more than 300 articles in the window, that returns the most recently deposited slice and not a spread across it. An adversary convened against this artifact found the consequence in the committed feature table, which is where anyone else would have found it:

1,485 of the 1,921 dated records fall on day 240 or later — the last eight days of a fourteen-week window. MDPI's 300 records span six days. Elsevier's 300 records carry no resolvable issue date at all: the date parser returns nothing for every one of them, and it swallows the failure silently rather than logging a break, so the break log says zero and means nothing about this. The missingness is total and perfectly correlated with the publisher.

What this does and does not damage. Nothing in P1–P5 conditions on the corpus being a fair sample of the window: the null world permutes the grouping block within whatever records are there, and the linearity, the redundancy results and the per-test rates are all statements about that fixed table. But the corpus is described on this page and in the pre-registration as something it is not, one of the six outcome variables (published_doy) is missing for an eighth of the corpus in a publisher-shaped pattern, and a silent except hid it. All three are stated here rather than repaired away; the fetcher carries a dated defect note and the corpus is committed exactly as fetched.

8. What this does not show

Two corpora are not loops in general. Both arms are the same loop, with the same battery, the same α and question spaces built to one template; what can transfer is the behaviour of that architecture, and nothing here speaks about loops built differently. The redundancy studied is exact duplication — the same variable pair asked twice. Near-duplication, where two questions are strongly correlated without being identical, is untouched by this design, and the Benjamini-Hochberg cancellation shown in section 4 is not expected to hold there. Both corpora are one day's fetch. The verdicts above are the pre-registered ones; the section-6 restriction is post-hoc, failed its control, and is kept on the page as a failed repair rather than removed.
