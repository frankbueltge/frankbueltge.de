A loop that finds things

Cycle 002 asks this practice to build end-to-end automation of research and measure where it breaks. So we built one: a loop that enumerates its own questions, fetches its own data, runs its own tests, writes its own claims and reviews itself — six stages, no person in the middle. It ran in about a hundred seconds and reported 14 findings. Then we ran it 500 more times on a world we had emptied of everything there was to find, and it reported 3.2 per run.

What was built

Six stages, in tools/autoloop/, each of which logs its own failures instead of raising them — a loop that dies on its first bad cell measures nothing about where loops break.

A world with nothing in it

The honest test of a machine that finds things is to give it nothing and see what it brings back. We permuted the grouping labels across records — every association destroyed, every distribution and every dependence among the groupings kept — and ran the whole battery 500 times.

Figure 2 · Findings per run in 500 empty worlds: mean 3.22, median 3, worst 11. Only 23 of 500 runs came back empty-handed. The real corpus returned 14.

The per-test rejection rate across all 33,000 null tests is 4.88 % (Wilson 95 % CI 4.66–5.12 %). That covers the nominal 5 %, which refutes our own prediction P2: the tests are not miscalibrated. Nothing is broken in the statistics. The loop manufactures findings because it asks 66 questions, and for no other reason.

Throughput and error control are the same dial. Everything automation adds to a research loop's speed, it takes out of the loop's credence, unless something else in the loop is paying it back.

Do not subtract. 14 minus 3.2 is not 11 real findings. The null-world mean is the average yield of this loop when there is nothing at all to find; it says how loud the machine is in silence, not which of the 14 are real. That question is answered — partly — by what follows.

Here the something else is Benjamini–Hochberg, and it works: 10 of 14 findings survive it and 7 survive Bonferroni. Both counts under the denominator the code actually used — the 51 tests that could become a claim. Our pre-registration had said "across all 66"; under that literal rule the counts are 12 and 9, and the claim set is the same, because the 2 tests it promotes were already killed at review. An adversary found the discrepancy; it is deviation 7 in METHOD.md. What multiplicity correction cannot repair is next.

What the loop could not see

It asked the same question twice, fifteen times over

Two of its findings carried the identical p-value to every printed digit (3.3 × 10−140). They are one 2 × 2 table, asked once with the DOI as the grouping and once with the journal reference as the grouping. An audit of the whole space — added after this was noticed, and therefore exploratory — found that the 66 questions rest on only 51 distinct pairs of variables: 15 questions are a second asking. Among the survivors, 10 findings rest on 8 associations.

No stage of the loop could notice this. The enumeration rule is correct, each test is correct, the correction is correctly applied to the number of tests actually run — and the count of findings is still inflated, because the generator does not know that two of its variables are two views of one thing.

It cannot tell a discovery from a plumbing fact

The ten survivors, read by hand against a rubric fixed before the data were seen. This table is judgment, not measurement, coded by the same practice that wrote the prediction it tests, with no blind second coder; the reasoning is given so it can be re-coded against us.

Half of what it found does not survive the same corpus split in two

Splitting the records by the parity of the last digit of their identifier and re-running everything: 7 of 14 findings come back significant with the same sign in both halves. This is not out-of-sample replication — it is the same corpus, the same day, cut in two.

But read it precisely, because 50 % invites the wrong conclusion: 13 of 14 keep the same sign in both halves. Only one finding actually points the other way in one half; the rest fail only because halving the corpus costs the power to clear p < 0.05 twice. Fragile at the threshold, mostly steady in direction. This distinction was pointed out by the adversary and measured afterwards.

What broke

  • EXPERIMENT · degenerate_test · has_comment|comment_pages@full — no p-value computable (zero variance or empty group)
  • EXPERIMENT · degenerate_test · has_comment|comment_pages@half-even — no p-value computable (zero variance or empty group)
  • EXPERIMENT · degenerate_test · has_comment|comment_pages@half-odd — no p-value computable (zero variance or empty group)

15 of 66 questions were killed by the review pre-conditions before any claim could be written — eight of them because one outcome, the page count stated in an author comment, is missing for most of the corpus. One test was degenerate: a stated page count can only be parsed out of a comment, so its comparison group was empty. The loop logged all of this and carried on.

The first thing the review stage found wrong was the review stage. Its first run reported five disagreements, all of them the string 0.0001 in a claim sentence that it could not re-derive — because p = <0.0001 is a threshold notation, not a measurement. The fault was the reviewer's tokeniser. That run is committed unrepaired at data/review-run1-unrepaired.json; the repair is dated in the file; the second run reports 586 checks and 0 disagreements.

What an adversary broke

An adversary was convened against this measurement before it shipped, given the pre-registration, the code, the data and the eight headline numbers, and told to break them. It returned twelve items. Three were defects and all three are repaired in the open:

  • The multiplicity denominator was not the one we registered — corrected above, both counts published, the claim set unchanged.
  • The review pass was less independent than we said. It had been taking the loop's own z on trust for every numeric claim, and had never re-applied the pre-conditions itself. Both now done from its own re-derivations: 586 checks, 0 disagreements.
  • The page this pre-registration promised did not exist yet when the adversary looked. It does now, and make_page.py --check rebuilds it from the data.

Six attacks failed, and a failed attack is evidence too: the tie-corrected Mann-Whitney variance, the pooled two-proportion z and the Benjamini–Hochberg step-up are textbook-correct (the last verified against a hand-built non-monotonic sequence); the permutation null preserves dependence among the groupings while destroying every association; the identifier-parity split shows no imbalance on nine features (all p > 0.14); the missing continuity correction moves the most marginal p from 0.043619 to 0.043623; and a cluster-robust interval for the null-world rate (0.0464–0.0513) sits almost exactly on our Wilson interval — which, the adversary noted, is the wrong estimator whose adequacy here is luck. Everything it found, and everything it failed to break, is in VERIFICATION.md.

What this licenses, and what it does not

It licenses this: a loop of this shape — enumerate, fetch, test, write, review — can be built in one sitting, runs unattended in about two minutes, and is now on a nightly schedule (.github/workflows/autoloop.yml) so that its yield becomes a series rather than an anecdote. Its statistics are calibrated. Its review stage catches numeric drift. It is a real instrument, and it is small.

It does not license this: any claim that the loop did research. It has no prior, no theory and no interest. It cannot tell that two of its questions are one question; it cannot tell that a DOI and a journal reference are stamped by the same event; and the only stage that noticed either was a person reading the output afterwards. What is missing between the two is not throughput and not statistical rigour — the machine has more of both than a person does. It is the standing of the question.

Limits, in the practice's own hand: one corpus, one day, one loop. The sampling frame is eight category queries and not arXiv, so several survivors could be properties of that frame — named per finding in the table above. The split half is the same day's records. The judgment column is judgment. And the loop's own author wrote both its questions and the predictions about what it would do, which is exactly the arrangement this practice has measured in others.
