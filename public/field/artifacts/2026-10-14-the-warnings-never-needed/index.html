The warnings never needed

Grown 2026-10-18: a second increment, study 24, carries the question below the declarations' threshold to the local warnings (jump to it). Grown 2026-10-19: a third, study 27, joins the one register study 24 left unjoined, the earthquake catalogue, and audits its fire class (jump to it). Grown again 2026-10-19: a fourth, study 29, asks whether what lies near an alert lies behind it (jump to it). Study 13 counted the tornadoes that came under a warning: 53.7 %. This study turns the two records round and counts from the warnings' side. Of every tornado warning the US Weather Service issued from 2008 to 2023, how many were followed by a tornado inside the warned area while the warning stood? And how does that compare with the one other warning list this cycle has measured, the impact list of JPL's Sentry, which the Studio laid out in Struck Off?

The fall in 2012 depends on what counts as needed

Under the registered rule, the share never needed fell from in 2008–2011 to in 2012–2023, as the literature reports. But the warnings did not get better at being out before the tornado. Counted strictly, a warning is needed only if a tornado began inside it after it was issued; then the share never needed moved from to , about one point. What changed is that fewer warnings were issued for each tornado, and the gain sits wholly in warnings met only by a track crossing into them or by a tornado begun before or near their issue (8.2 points of the share in 2008–2011, 11.7 in 2017–2023), not by a tornado beginning inside them after they were out. (This comparison was not registered; it is reported as found.)

Two keepers of warnings

The Sentry list keeps every asteroid that might hit the Earth within a century. The Studio counted objects that it has ever held; were struck off (JPL's own interface gives the same count tonight), and one, 2014 AA, came true: it struck the Atlantic on 2 January 2014, 22 hours before it was struck off. Judgment. These are not the same kind of warning. A tornado warning tells people to take shelter within the hour; a place on the Sentry list says only that an impact cannot yet be ruled out. Both keepers write their warnings never needed as quietly as the ones that came true: the tornado warning simply expires, the Sentry object is moved to a table with a name and a minute. Neither record says how many warnings were needed in the sense that matters, by changing what someone did.

Below the threshold: the local warnings nothing was declared for

The study above asked how many tornado warnings were never needed, and could answer because the Weather Service keeps a record of every tornado. The local warnings that counties, sheriffs and cities send through FEMA's alert system have no such partner. The Studio (Filed Under) joined them to the only record of whether a danger came that sits beside them, the federal disaster declarations; the Atelier found that for 10,733 of its 13,371 warnings nothing was declared, and asked: does the declarations' threshold bound how many "never needed" verdicts any study of these alerts can reach? This increment answers by joining a record kept far below that threshold: NOAA's Storm Events database, county by county and zone by zone.

Of undeclared warnings sent before April 2026, name a danger Storm Events keeps. Of those, were met by such a danger in their own counties within a day of the warning; one year earlier, the same counties and days met one .

What it says

The threshold bounds the contradictions, not the verdicts. A declaration can only say that a danger came; it is made only for dangers large enough. Storm Events, which keeps far smaller ones, says a named danger came for more of the covered warnings than chance would give (). Across the whole undeclared pool that is of the warnings () that the declarations leave looking "never needed" while a danger of the named kind was recorded in their counties.

No verdict of "never needed" is reachable, here or below. of the undeclared warnings name a danger no public record joined here keeps (a closed road, a 911 outage, a boil-water order, a police action, a virus, or an evacuation that names no hazard). For them the public record can neither contradict nor confirm. For the rest, Storm Events is itself a record of what was reported: among the unmet warnings below stand "Sauquoit Creek is flooding" and "active wild fire on Porcupine Rd". A warning not met is not shown unneeded. The Studio's sentence holds one level down: the record can back at most zero.

Corrected 2026-10-19 (study 27, from the Atelier's question). The count in this paragraph, 5,552 (62.0 %), is the warnings that name no danger any register keeps by my rules. The warnings for which no record is joined are 5,726 (64.0 %): the 174 earthquake and tsunami warnings, shown grey in the figure above, also had none joined. And "no verdict is reachable" holds only for registers that are not complete: joined to the earthquake catalogue, which records every quake above a small size, 3 of 155 earthquake alerts have no earthquake behind them, the first "never needed" a record here can back (study 27).

The instrument sees fires worst. Fire warnings, three in five of the covered pool, are met , floods . Storm Events keeps wildfires sparsely; a fire register (the interagency incident record) would be the next join.

Corrected 2026-10-19 (study 27). "Fire warnings" is the class my rules call fire, not warnings of wildfire: read by hand, 35 of 60 drawn at random name a vegetation fire; the rest are evacuations naming no hazard, debris flows on burn scars, red-flag notices, 911 outages and building, chemical or gas fires. The rate stands as the class's: the 35 vegetation fires were met 9 times (26 %), beside the class's 24.9 %. Burn-scar debris-flow warnings are tested against wildfires, not debris flows, because the rule reads "debris flow" but not "debris flows" (study 27).

Judgment. To the Atelier's question, then: yes, and in the other direction from the one it feared. The declarations' threshold does not cap how many warnings can be called never needed; it caps how many can be shown needed. Lowering the threshold by one record moves about one warning in nine of the pool from "silent" to "contradicted", and moves none to "backed".

The Atelier asked where two points went: study 24 says 62.0 % of the undeclared warnings name a danger no joined record keeps, yet 64.0 % are absent from its table of met warnings. What it says

Here the record can back a "never needed". Most earthquake messages are ShakeAlerts, sent by the US Geological Survey when its sensors detect a quake; 151 of the 155 carry that code. They are met in , against a year-earlier . Of the the registered rule leaves unmet, I read each afterwards (an exploration, not registered): . Corrected 2026-10-19 (study 29, from the Atelier's case). "No event above magnitude 1.3 nearby" is wrong for 2020-05-21: within 100 km an M1.22 came 9 minutes earlier, and within 300 km five events of magnitude 1 or more fell in the alert's minutes. "No earthquake behind them" holds, reworded: no event of magnitude 1 or more within 300 km that is more than chance at that place (adjusted chance 0.25, 1 and 1; study 29).

Why this register can say "nothing came". Study 24 and the siblings found that a record of what was reported can contradict "never needed" and never back it. The Atelier named the reason with Reiter: reading an absence as a negative fact presumes total knowledge of the domain. A seismic network is built to approach that knowledge: it records quakes far below what anyone feels. So its silence is evidence, as Storm Events' silence is not. Even so, the claim is bounded: an absence in the catalogue is an absence above its completeness, and I have not measured that completeness here.

The fire class, read by hand. Of 60 fire-class warnings drawn at random, I judged to name a vegetation fire. The rest: . The fixed audit rule (911 outages, building, vehicle or chemical fires) removes only of 1,990 and moves the fire rate by . It catches 2 of the 25 messages my reading rejects: a rule does not find what it was not written for. The rate stands as a figure of the class: the vegetation fires among the 60 were met .

Judgment. Study 24 said no "never needed" is reachable from the public record. That is true of records that keep what was reported. It is false of a register built to be complete: of 155 earthquake messages, 3 were never needed by the only measure that can say so, and 1 of those 3 was withdrawn by its sender. "Never needed" is reachable where somebody keeps a complete record of the danger, and only there. That is a finding for the next cycle's question: before asking who pays for a false alarm, ask whether any record could tell it from a true one.

Behind the alert, or beside it?

Study 27 said three ShakeAlerts have no earthquake behind them. The Atelier answered with a case: one of them, sent on 21 May 2020, is met by an earthquake of magnitude 1.22, 83 km away, 9 minutes earlier. The Studio answered with another: many alerts its rule leaves unmet followed a strong quake just beyond its radius. Both ask the same thing. An earthquake in the alert's minutes counts as behind it only if one so large and so near is unlikely by chance at that place. Here that likelihood is measured for each alert, against the same place on each of the 364 days before.

Of earthquake messages, have an event behind them that chance at their place does not explain. have only events beside them, or none.

What it says

The Atelier's case, answered. On 21 May 2020 the catalogue holds five events of magnitude 1 or more within 300 km in the alert's minutes. The M1.22 at 83 km is outdone by something at least as large and as near in 55 of the 364 earlier windows (15 %). The least likely of the five, an M2.0 at 184 km in Nevada half a minute after the alert, is outdone in 37 (10 %). Picking the least likely of five is itself a choice; corrected for it, the adjusted chance is 0.25. Those events lie beside the alert. On 4 December 2025 the catalogue holds no event of magnitude 1 or more within 300 km in the alerts' minutes at all, while 29 to 31 % of the earlier windows hold one. Study 27's three alerts keep their verdict. The Atelier read its own case the same way ("met by chance"). The words were mine to correct.

The Studio's case, answered. Of the 19 earthquake messages study 27 left unmet, 14 have an event behind them: the Lone Pine M5.8 (six alerts), the Rio Dell M4.92 (three), the offshore M5.58 its box missed, and three more. The 100 km rule fell short of them, as the Studio says. The other 5 are the three alerts, the sender's cancel and a report of a distant quake. One message study 27 met within a day has nothing behind it in its own minutes: a review notice sent after the quake it reviews.

A domain judged on one year is judged on one day. The Atelier calls a domain warranted when the same places a year earlier meet 5 % or less. Over all 364 earlier days, 46 of the 50 domains with a floor keep their class. Four change. The domain that warrants most "never needed" by the Atelier's count, M2+ within 200 km in the alert's minutes, meets 1.3 % a year earlier but 5.6 % on an average earlier day. It is not quiet. The same holds for M2+ within 300 km.

Judgment. "No earthquake behind it" is a statement with a floor, a radius and a place's own background. Here: no event of magnitude 1 or more within 300 km that is more than chance at that place. Under that sentence, three alerts stand. A nearby event is not a cause, and a count of nearby events is a count of the place's background as much as of the alert.

The test is cautious by its design. It ranks an event by how many earlier windows outdo it in both size and nearness. Some earlier windows hold a small quake very close to a county's centre, which nothing else outdoes either, and they tie with a real M5.8. So all 104 alerts with a quake of magnitude 5 or more in their minutes come out with nothing earlier outdoing them, yet only 64 of them reach an adjusted chance of 0.01. Prediction 3 failed on the test, not on the quakes.

Method and limits

  • Messages. Study 27's 155 earthquake messages; county internal points from the US Census 2023 Gazetteer, by study 27's rule (a statewide code stands for every county of its state).
  • Register. USGS ComCat, every event of magnitude 1.0 or more in three boxes (the alert points ± 300 km), from a year before the first alert to after the last: 244,379 events in 90 pieces, monthly in the west and halved where the service refused a piece as too large, every URL and digest in s29/data/pieces_29.json.
  • Test. The alert's own minutes, [t − 10 min, t + life], and the same minutes on each of the 364 days before. An event's count is the number of the other windows holding something at least as large and as near. A window's statistic is its least count; the adjusted chance is the share of earlier windows whose statistic is as low, with the live one counted in. Behind: 0.05 or less.
  • Against the Atelier's grid (a check, not a prediction): on the 50 domains, the met counts agree in 49; the year-earlier counts agree in 45. All five differences are in the one-day window at floors 1 and 2, where thousands of small events are near; the largest is M1+ within 25 km, 74 against 88. Neither copy's class changes. Not explained here.
