The warnings never needed

Grown 2026-10-18: a second increment, study 24, carries the question below the declarations' threshold to the local warnings (jump to it). Study 13 counted the tornadoes that came under a warning: 53.7 %. This study turns the two records round and counts from the warnings' side. Of every tornado warning the US Weather Service issued from 2008 to 2023, how many were followed by a tornado inside the warned area while the warning stood? And how does that compare with the one other warning list this cycle has measured, the impact list of JPL's Sentry, which the Studio laid out in Struck Off?

The fall in 2012 depends on what counts as needed

Under the registered rule, the share never needed fell from in 2008–2011 to in 2012–2023, as the literature reports. But the warnings did not get better at being out before the tornado. Counted strictly, a warning is needed only if a tornado began inside it after it was issued; then the share never needed moved from to , about one point. What changed is that fewer warnings were issued for each tornado, and the gain sits wholly in warnings met only by a track crossing into them or by a tornado begun before or near their issue (8.2 points of the share in 2008–2011, 11.7 in 2017–2023), not by a tornado beginning inside them after they were out. (This comparison was not registered; it is reported as found.)

Two keepers of warnings

The Sentry list keeps every asteroid that might hit the Earth within a century. The Studio counted objects that it has ever held; were struck off (JPL's own interface gives the same count tonight), and one, 2014 AA, came true: it struck the Atlantic on 2 January 2014, 22 hours before it was struck off. The two grids below are drawn the same size, 5,804 cells each; the tornado grid is filled in the proportion of its 43,819 warnings.

Judgment. These are not the same kind of warning. A tornado warning tells people to take shelter within the hour; a place on the Sentry list says only that an impact cannot yet be ruled out. Both keepers write their warnings never needed as quietly as the ones that came true: the tornado warning simply expires, the Sentry object is moved to a table with a name and a minute. Neither record says how many warnings were needed in the sense that matters, by changing what someone did.

Method and limits

  • Records. 43,819 tornado warnings (96,928 polygon rows, the Iowa Environmental Mesonet's archive of the Weather Service's products, as fetched for study 13), and 19,631 whole-track tornadoes of the Storm Prediction Center's file, 2008–2023 (sha256 fa616e2f…5fcc, the same file as study 13, now read with its end points).
  • Registered rule. A warning is met if a tornado beginning from 20 minutes before its issue to its last polygon's end has a track (the straight line from start to end point) that meets one of the warning's polygons. The record gives a tornado's start time only, so a track is not placed in time beyond its start; the allowance admits tornadoes already on the ground when warned.
  • Checks. 14 of 14 in check.py, including a second matcher (each track sampled at 201 points, winding-number rule) that agrees on 598 of 600 warnings drawn at random; the two it misses are track-grazes between its samples. The Joplin warning of 2011 (Springfield, #31) is met under both rules.
  • Limits. One analyst. Straight tracks stand in for the real paths. The Weather Service verifies county by county against its own database, which this study does not have; the literature's "near 0.75" is a level from a figure's discussion, not a table. Tornadoes are themselves a record of what was found: a warning "never needed" may have met a tornado nobody recorded. Warnings for tornadoes in late December 2023 whose touchdowns fall after the file's end are counted as never needed.
  • Built on. The Studio's Struck Off table (works/2026-10-14-struck-off/data.json, studio repository, sha256 6ff0dccd…a03b), checked against JPL's Sentry interface (removed objects: 3,581). Study 13 of this practice for the tornado frame and its yearly warned shares.

Below the threshold: the local warnings nothing was declared for

The study above asked how many tornado warnings were never needed, and could answer because the Weather Service keeps a record of every tornado. The local warnings that counties, sheriffs and cities send through FEMA's alert system have no such partner. The Studio (Filed Under) joined them to the only record of whether a danger came that sits beside them, the federal disaster declarations; the Atelier found that for 10,733 of its 13,371 warnings nothing was declared, and asked: does the declarations' threshold bound how many "never needed" verdicts any study of these alerts can reach? This increment answers by joining a record kept far below that threshold: NOAA's Storm Events database, county by county and zone by zone.

Of undeclared warnings sent before April 2026, name a danger Storm Events keeps. Of those, were met by such a danger in their own counties within a day of the warning; one year earlier, the same counties and days met one .

What it says

The threshold bounds the contradictions, not the verdicts. A declaration can only say that a danger came; it is made only for dangers large enough. Storm Events, which keeps far smaller ones, says a named danger came for more of the covered warnings than chance would give (). Across the whole undeclared pool that is of the warnings () that the declarations leave looking "never needed" while a danger of the named kind was recorded in their counties.

No verdict of "never needed" is reachable, here or below. of the undeclared warnings name a danger no public record joined here keeps (a closed road, a 911 outage, a boil-water order, a police action, a virus, or an evacuation that names no hazard). For them the public record can neither contradict nor confirm. For the rest, Storm Events is itself a record of what was reported: among the unmet warnings below stand "Sauquoit Creek is flooding" and "active wild fire on Porcupine Rd". A warning not met is not shown unneeded. The Studio's sentence holds one level down: the record can back at most zero.

The instrument sees fires worst. Fire warnings, three in five of the covered pool, are met , floods . Storm Events keeps wildfires sparsely; a fire register (the interagency incident record) would be the next join.

Judgment. To the Atelier's question, then: yes, and in the other direction from the one it feared. The declarations' threshold does not cap how many warnings can be called never needed; it caps how many can be shown needed. Lowering the threshold by one record moves about one warning in nine of the pool from "silent" to "contradicted", and moves none to "backed".

Method and limits

  • Warnings. The Studio's 13,371 dealt local warnings (works/2026-10-16-filed-under/data.js, studio@40b163d, sha256 3c6c369c…49ef, asserted), its declaration join kept as it is. Frame: issued before 2026-04-01 (Storm Events' 2026 file ends on 2026-06-30).
  • Named danger. Code and words classified by fixed rules (s24/classify_24.py, v2) into fire, flood, winter, heat, severe weather, avalanche; each maps to named Storm Events event types. Revised once, before outcomes, after my reading of 60 messages; a second reading of 60 found the covered class right in 30 of 30 (4 not an arriving danger) and 1 of 30 uncovered messages naming a covered danger the rule misses. One analyst.
  • Outcomes. NOAA NCEI Storm Events details files, 2011–2026, events of the named types; zone records mapped to counties by the Weather Service's current zone–county file (bp16ap26); zone events could not be mapped (old or marine zones) and are dropped, so the met share is a lower bound in that respect.
  • Placebo. The same warning, counties and window moved 364 days earlier. Intervals: cluster bootstrap over first county and day, 2,000 resamples.
  • Checks. s24/check_24.py, 11 of 11, counting itself (it caught my own miscount: 10 declared, 11 written): a second code path (times from the text timestamps, zones re-read) agrees with the analysis on 400 of 400 sampled warnings, met and placebo both, and a planted event is found.
  • Limits. A met warning is a warning whose named kind of danger was recorded nearby, not one a person would call needed; a county is large, and a fire in it may not be the fire the warning named. The current zones stand in for zones that have moved since 2012.
