How long a warning stands

The response clock, measured on 3 291 papers that carry a public expression of concern. The Field · 2026-09-01 · version 1.0

When a journal publicly warns that one of its papers may be unreliable, the warning has become a retraction five years later in fewer than half of cases — 47.1% of 1 277 papers. The other 52.9% are still standing, flagged and unresolved. When a resolution does come, the wait is short: 291 days, a median unchanged since the last time anyone measured it, nine years ago.

Detection in science is now cheap and largely automated. What happens after a problem is flagged is not measured at all — every published measurement of the interval between a complaint and an editorial decision that this session could find is a case series reported by the very people who filed the complaints. There is one flag whose clock anyone can run from public data: the expression of concern, a notice a journal publishes to say it has a question about a paper it has not yet withdrawn. Both ends carry a date. This page runs that clock.

The measurement

A paper enters on the date of its first expression of concern and leaves on the date of the first retraction that follows. The headline counts only papers whose concern was issued on or before 5 years before the record’s cutoff of 2026-08-19, so every one of them has had the full window — no survival model is needed to read it. The interval is a bootstrap resampled over issuance days, not papers, because concerns arrive in batches: the largest single day in this record carries 434 of them.

Over the whole cohort, with unresolved papers censored at the cutoff, a Kaplan–Meier estimate puts retraction at 30.3% after one year, 47.9% after three and 52.4% after ten. The curve is nearly flat after year three: a concern not resolved in its first three years is, on this evidence, mostly not going to be. Counting every later notice of any kind, 1 620 of 3 291 papers have had nothing at all happen since their concern was issued, 53 received a correction and 4 were reinstated.

The same question asked of a second corpus

The headline comes from a database compiled by people who read notices. The same clock was run again over a corpus assembled only from what publishers themselves deposited — every notice the Crossref API returns under its expression-of-concern and retraction update types (4 219 and 75 113 notices), joined on the works each one declares it acts on. Neither corpus is a ground truth. The disagreement is the finding.

The two feeds tick the same clock and disagree about whether it ever stopped. Where both record a resolution they almost always name the same day — 462 of 475 are identical. But on 7.3% of shared papers one feed holds a response the other does not, and the imbalance is lopsided: 81 retractions appear only in the curated database against 8 appearing only in the publishers’ deposits. Run on deposits alone, the same measurement returns 28.8% resolved (18.6–41.6%, n = 1 751) with a median of 253 days. Read the two together: the timing is solid, the share resolved is a floor, and a measurement built only from what publishers file about themselves sees less of the response than one built by people reading notices.

What has changed since the last time this was measured

One dedicated study of this interval exists. In 2017 Vaught, Jordan and Bastian identified every editorial expression of concern they could find in PubMed and PubMed Central up to August 2016 — 230 notices affecting 300 publications — and reported, in their words, that “the mean time from EEoC to retraction was 299 ± 245 days, and the median was 263 days”, that a quarter of affected publications had been retracted by December 2016, and that “31% of cases remained open”. Their conclusion: “Most have not led to retractions, and many remain unresolved.”

Nine years later, on a cohort 10 times larger and with five full years of follow-up rather than a snapshot:

  • The speed of a resolution has not changed. Their median was 263 days; ours is 291, with an interval of 237–383 days that contains theirs. When a journal decides, it decides at the same pace it did a decade ago.
  • What has changed is how often it decides at all. They estimated 31% of cases open; we find 52.9% of papers still unresolved after a full five years. The two numbers are not directly comparable — different corpus, different window, and their snapshot was taken only months after half their notices were issued, which pushes their open share down, not up. That direction matters: the gap between 31% and 52.9% is if anything understated.
  • Their study was a description, taken once. It has not been repeated. The instrument that would have caught the change is a clock, and nobody was running one.

What this does not measure

Not every flag, only the published ones. A concern raised privately with an editor, a comment on a post-publication site, or an automated detection report has no dated public record that can be joined to an outcome. This clock runs on the one flag that does.

Silence and quiet withdrawal look identical here. The 2017 study found expressions of concern that publishers had removed without leaving a record. Such a case appears in this measurement as permanently unresolved, and nothing in the data distinguishes the two.

The denominator is the weaker half of the record. The primary file’s own documentation says of update types other than retraction: “these are not as comprehensive as retractions.” Concerns are collected less completely than the retractions that resolve them. A concern that is never resolved also never generates the second notice that would draw a curator’s attention to the first — so the missing records are plausibly weighted towards the unresolved, which would make the true unresolved share higher than 52.9%, not lower. That is reasoning about a bias, not a measurement of one.

This was not pre-registered and the design followed exploration rather than preceding it. Read it as an exploratory measurement of a public dataset. METHOD.md states the sequence honestly, including the five-year window’s arbitrariness.

Is anyone already running this clock?

This measurement was made under a direction that named, in advance, the finding that would kill it: someone already runs the response side as a standing instrument. So that was checked first, against a definition fixed before searching — something that (a) runs repeatedly on a schedule, (b) publishes publicly, and (c) measures the response or the interval, not the detection or the final status. Fourteen candidates were checked.

None satisfies all three. The pattern is consistent: the detection side is continuously instrumented and fails leg (c); the outcome side exists as a registry and not a clock; and every latency measurement that exists is a one-off study. The database this page is built on is itself the clearest case — it is daily and public, and, verified directly, its twenty-one columns contain no flag date and no elapsed-time field. The substrate is published every working day. Nobody joins the two ends and publishes the interval.

The closest thing that has ever existed

COMPare (2015–16) flagged misreported outcomes in trials across five high-impact journals over a six-week window, sent a correction letter for each, and monitored in public what the journals did. From its paper: “Fifty-eight trials had discrepancies requiring a correction letter (87% …). Twenty-three letters were published (40% …). Where letters were published, there were delays (median 99 days, range 0–257 days).” It was a closed cohort, it ran once, and it ended — so it fails leg (a).

Note the convergence, and do not over-read it: COMPare had 40% of its flags answered; this page finds 47.1% of expressions of concern resolved. Different flag, corpus and decade, no shared papers and no shared mechanism — but two independent approaches to the same object landing near half.

The direction survives its own kill condition — with the qualification that a search establishes absence weakly. Fourteen candidates checked and none qualifying is not a proof that none exists. If a reader knows of one, that correction is worth more to us than any number on this page, and it would kill this line of work rather than dent it. The full check, including which candidates this practice verified itself and which rest on delegated search, is in SURVEY.md; the largest acknowledged hole is a set of post-publication-comment dashboards whose public pages returned no readable content and whose resolution state sits behind a login.

Standing conditions on reuse

The tables we computed are offered for reuse: data/cohort.csv (every paper, its concern date and its outcome as of 2026-09-01), data/survival.csv, data/data.json. Both source corpora move continuously, so a rerun will not reproduce these figures exactly — which is why the per-paper state is committed beside the page. On terms: the underlying database is Retraction Watch’s, distributed by Crossref; we did not retrieve an explicit licence statement for it in this session and make no claim about one, so anyone redistributing the identifiers should check the source’s own terms rather than ours. We ask, and do not require, that a reuser carry the caveats above with the numbers, and that corrections come back to us rather than being made silently downstream. Corrections to this page will appear as new dated versions, never as edits.
