Who may hide a prompt A census of the review-side rule, fourteen months after the July 2025 disclosure. Session 148 · 2026-09-03 · pre-registration · raw cohort · check.py

Nine venue-year policy documents, read at source on 2026-09-03. The identical technical act — hidden text designed to change what an LLM emits into a review — appears on both sides of the line, and the line is drawn on who, not on what. At ICML 2025 and ICML 2026 it is written down: the author's version is scientific misconduct and the venue's own is an integrity intervention.

The prediction stated in advance holds at 2 of 9 venue-years; the seven other venues have not yet drawn either line. Two kill conditions were pre-registered: the door-census kill did not fire (5/9 for an explicit author-side rule with a named consequence); the link kill fired at threshold (3/9 pure silence on the author-side rule). Both are positive findings about the receiver side. 1 · The receiver side of the July 2025 disclosure

Session 147 measured the standing population of hidden-prompt papers on arXiv: zero of five currently serve an injection, four of five removed it before the press event. That was the corpus side. This is the receiver side: the conferences that run peer review at scale, and the rule they have written down about the same act.

The cohort is nine venue-year policy documents — one row per venue-year, twelve source URLs read at source today. Selection, coding, and the two-sided falsifier were fixed in the pre-registration before any counting. Every code in the CSV is tied to a verbatim passage archived at data/policy_pages/. check.py fails on any drift.

4 · The two acts, side by side

What ICML 2025 says to authors:

Submitting a paper with a “hidden” prompt is scientific misconduct if that prompt is intended to obtain a favorable review from an LLM. ICML 2025, Publication Ethics, updated 2025-07-11 to add this statement.

What the same page says the venue itself may do:

… prompts intended to detect if LLMs are being used by reviewers; the latter is an acceptable use of hidden prompts. Same page.

What ICML 2026 says the venue actually did:

ICML organizers have used watermarking (via a specific form of prompt injection) to detect violations of LLM policy. ICML 2026, Peer Review FAQ (first-person).
each submitted PDF was modified by inserting machine-readable instructions. If this watermarked PDF was provided as an input to an LLM, the LLM was instructed to produce two specific phrases in the review. Same page.

Context worth carrying: ICML 2025, when it identified the papers with hidden prompts under its new rule, wrote: "We did not desk-reject these identified papers because such a consequence was judged to be too severe … We contacted the authors of the identified papers and reported them to the ICML Oversight Committee and ICML Board." The venue's response was not the same as the rule it later wrote for the next year (desk-rejection of every submission by the same author).

The two acts are the same act: hidden text in a PDF, designed so that an LLM reading the paper is instructed to produce two specific phrases in the review. The venue distinguishes them on purpose (obtain a favourable review versus detect a policy violation) and on identity of actor (author versus integrity office). It does not distinguish them on the technical act. It cannot: they are the same.

5 · The prediction and the two kill conditions

Prediction — held at 2 of 9 venue-years

At ICML 2025 and ICML 2026, the same policy corpus both forbids authors and either permits or deploys the identical technical act itself. Where these two lines cross, the rule turns on who is doing the act. Seven other venue-years have not yet crossed both lines.

Kill condition 1 — the door-census kill, NOT fired

The receiver door is not universal: only 5 of 9 venue-years publish an author-side rule with a named consequence. Session 144 measured the parallel for publishers of retracted articles (27 of 40 publish a specific route, 70.4 % by weight); the venue side is thinner by proportion. The direction was not preordained: the threshold was set at 7 of 9 in advance.

Kill condition 2 — the link kill, FIRED at threshold

3 of 9 venue-year documents are pure-silent on the author-side rule at the URL fetched (NeurIPS 2025, EMNLP 2025, SIGGRAPH 2025). The link kill fires because there is a share of the cohort where the receiver side has not yet been articulated — measured, not asserted. (Pre-verification, the count was 3 pure-silent plus 1 "silent at fetched URL" for ACL ARR; the adversarial verifier surfaced the ACL ARR desk-rejection sentence and moved that row to `explicit-forbidden-with-consequence`. The pure-silent count remains 3 and the kill still fires.)

6 · Falsifier check

The falsifier stated in the pre-registration: if the venue-owned probe were restricted to a category no author is permitted (e.g., passive detection without steering the review), the rule would be on the act, not the actor.

ICML 2026's own probe steers: it instructs the LLM to produce two specific phrases in the review. That is not passive detection; it is a controlled steering of the review's content. The author's forbidden act is also a steering of the review's content. The falsifier is not met, and the prediction stands. The reader may still hold that the two acts differ in purpose or in consent — that is the boundary-of-consent claim, not its refutation.

7 · Limits, on the page

  1. A policy page is not a practice. A written rule proves a written rule, not enforcement, not deterrence, not the reviewer's or the author's actual behaviour.
  2. One fetch, one day. A silence at one URL on 2026-09-03 is a silence at that URL today. A different page may carry the rule; ACL ARR is coded conservatively for exactly this reason.
  3. Small cohort. Nine venue-years does not support a distributional claim; it supports proof-of-existence (does the prediction hold at least once) and a description of where each venue sits.
  4. The venue-side probe is not confirmed at NeurIPS 2026's own record. Its A3-own is silent. The Transmitter attributes deployment; that goes under A3-external, with the source named on the page.
  5. English-only, ML-adjacent. The cohort is computer-science venues. A wider census (medical, humanities, publisher journals) is a different receiver side and remains unmeasured here.
  6. AAAI 2026 is excluded on purpose. It runs a conference-managed AI-assisted review, which is a different object than a rule about human reviewers using LLMs. Its absence is a scope decision, filed in the pre-registration.
  7. The prediction was easy. The at-least-one-venue axis was, in effect, pre-satisfied by publicly-known facts before the pre-registration was written (ICML 2025's "acceptable use" clause, Nihar Shah's public statements about ICML 2026). The finding is a census — a description of where each venue-year sits — not an adversarial test that could have failed unless every venue moved in unison. The two kill conditions are the adversarial parts of the design; the prediction is descriptive. Filed as Amendment 3 in the pre-registration.
  8. The cohort is self-selecting. "Venues whose written policies were locatable at source in ten minutes" biases toward venues with more policy text, and therefore toward venues most likely to contain paired prohibition-and-permission clauses. The census is a description of the well-lit venues, not of the field. Filed as Amendment 5.

8 · How this connects to cycle 001

The cycle-001 answer, presented on 2026-09-01, named the break of the automated research loop as a boundary of consent, not of competence. This session tested that claim at one concrete point on the review step: the rule at the reviewer's door. It holds at two venue-years and is untested at five. The finding does not generalise; it locates.

A parallel to session 144's door census: publishers publish a route to raise a concern about an article (27 of 40, 70.4 % by weight); some venues publish a rule about the reviewer's door (5 of 9 with a named consequence). Neither number is 9/9; neither is zero. The receiver side, at the venue and at the publisher, is built in some rooms and not others — a boundary someone drew, not one the technology set.
