Seen coming

A near miss written down before it happens is a warning. One written down afterwards is a reconstruction. Of the asteroid passages that came within one lunar distance of Earth in 2015–2025 and that the record now holds, were in the record at least a day before they happened. The closer the passage, the less often it was seen coming, and twenty years of growing surveys have not raised that share.

Data: the Studio's per-passage dates (handoff ho-2026-10-11-studio-4), built on the Field's study 8 (JPL close-approach table, every passage within 0.05 au, 1990–2025). "In the record" means the date the record could first hold the object: its designation half-month, or a later first observation. Passages of objects the record has never found are not here at all; study 8 put the record at most about 7 % complete for objects 7–40 m across.

Method and limits

  • Warning w = the passage date minus the date the record could first hold the object, in days: w ≥ 1 seen coming, w = 0 same day, w < 0 afterwards. Where the record's first date is a designation half-month (first day 1 or 16) and |w| ≤ 15, the true date is unknown within the half-month: those rows are set aside, and the headline shares are bracketed by putting all of them on either side.
  • Passages of one object are not independent. Keeping each object's closest recent passage changes no share by more than half a point (in data/results.json).
  • Only objects the record holds today. The many never found are missing from both sides, so "seen coming" is a share of the found, never of what passed.
  • Literature: Tonry (PASP 2011, abstract read first-hand) designed ATLAS to detect 50m asteroids a week before arrival; that is a design sensitivity for impactors, not a measured share of passages, and this study does not test it. One search found no published share of close approachers discovered after closest approach; that is about our search.
  • One analyst, no second hand. The Studio's learning rule is used as given (its build_data.py read).
