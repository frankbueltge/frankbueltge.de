The odds written down

Most warnings state no odds. A tornado warning says a tornado may come, and nobody can say afterwards whether one in three or one in ten was the right expectation. After nearly every large earthquake in the United States, the US Geological Survey does write the odds down: the chance of at least one aftershock of magnitude 3, 4, 5, 6 or 7 inside a stated circle within a day, a week, a month and a year. The same agency's catalogue then records, by instrument, what came. No one heeding a forecast can change an earthquake. If any warning record can be scored, this one can.

I took the first forecast for each of the 255 mainshocks that carry one and scored it against the catalogue.

Read as warnings

Turn the odds into yes-or-no warnings: call every forecast whose stated chance is at least the threshold a warning. How many came true, and how many were never needed? For comparison, 69.6 % of US tornado warnings 2008–2023 were never met by a tornado (study 14).

What was found

1. The odds come true about half as often as written. narrowed 2026-10-19 Study 25: this holds for the first forecast, written in the first hour. Every later version chosen by a fixed clock, from six hours to thirty days, cannot be told from right. The sentence below stands for the first forecast. In the registered test, the 979 one-week forecasts at or above each forecast's own completeness floor stated, summed, 88.2 expected aftershock events. 49 came true. Observed over expected is 0.56 (0.40–0.72 by resampling mainshocks). The shortfall holds in every window: a day 0.53, a month 0.56, a year 0.64.

2. Not in California. qualified 2026-10-18 Study 22 finds the regional difference indistinguishable from one common factor (p 0.32 registered, 0.19 for California after allowing for having picked it). The sentence below describes this sample. California's mainshocks come out at 0.80 (16 of 20.0; 0.45–1.18). Its magnitude-3 forecasts, where the floor there allows them, came true 15 times against 15.6 stated. The shortfall sits in Alaska (0.45), and in Guam, the Northern Marianas and off the Oregon coast, where 0 of 5.8 expected came true.

3. Below the floor it is no worse. Where a forecast states odds below its own completeness magnitude, the catalogue cannot see everything, and I predicted the odds would look high there (they do: 0.48, 0.38–0.58). But they look just as high above the floor. Incompleteness does not explain the shortfall.

4. The larger one to come came less often. The forecasts gave, summed, 9.6 expected cases of a later, larger shock within the week among 252 mainshocks. 3 came, below the 4 to 16 the stated odds allow.

5. The odds do not beat a flat rate. Scored by the Brier score, the stated odds are no better than giving every forecast its magnitude's average hit rate (skill −0.03, −0.41 to 0.19). The share that came true does rise with the stated chance, band by band, but it sits below the stated chance in every band.

6. The circle is part of it (exploratory).

Reading (judgment). This is the cycle's first warning record that can be scored without a column nobody keeps, and the score is not flattering. Most first forecasts (211 of 255) use generic parameters, written before the sequence is known, and the record they are scored against is kept by the same agency, inside a circle that agency states. The odds rise where more came, and are written too high at every level. Read as warnings at even odds, more than half were never needed, as with the tornadoes. The difference is that here a reader can see it, because the odds were written down.

Method

Forecasts: every ComCat event with an oaf product (255 on 2026-10-17), the first non-deleted version of its forecast.json, issued a median 20 minutes after the mainshock. 211 are generic Reasenberg–Jones, 32 Bayesian-combination R–J, 11 ETAS, 1 sequence-specific. Outcomes: ComCat events inside the forecast's own circle (its regionRadius, which the product's data file calls the aftershock search region; 236 of 255 are 10 km), in each window, magnitude rounded to 0.01 at or above the bin, the mainshock excluded. Only windows closed by the fetch are scored (all 255 days, 252 weeks, 239 months, 116 years). Observed over expected with a 95 % interval from 2,000 resamples of mainshocks; Brier skill against each magnitude's pooled hit rate. A second code path (check.py) recomputes the registered counts and catches a planted event. Deviation, declared: the first run of analyse.py crashed on an exploratory subset with no hits; the fix touches no registered figure.

Limits

The circle is the product's stated region but a stand-in for an aftershock zone; the exploration of wider circles was chosen after the result was read. Magnitude types differ between networks and the forecast's magnitude scale is not stated in the product. The cells are nested and sequences overlap; only the resampling interval respects that. Two years of one system (2 of 255 sequences before November 2024). Single-sequence evaluations exist (Puerto Rico 2019–21, Ridgecrest 2019) and found good performance for sequence-fitted ETAS forecasts; this is a different object, every first forecast, mostly generic. I read their abstracts only. One analyst, who wrote the predictions.

Second increment, 2026-10-18 — study 22: one factor, or many classes?

Finding 2 said California was close and Alaska and the Pacific territories carried the gap. The Atelier then showed, with one warning per mainshock, that a single forecast can be judged "too high" in one of its classes and "consistent" in another: for 38 of 252, the narrowest classes disagree and both could have told a halving. A scientist's question follows from both. Is there any class structure here, or does one common factor, the same for every forecast, produce the regional gap and the disagreements by chance? Pre-registered in PREREGISTRATION-2.md, pushed before any computation (commit e207145).

Worlds where

1. The disagreement is what one factor makes by chance. In 1,000 worlds where every forecast comes true with 0.564 times its stated chance, the Atelier's whole pipeline, re-run, finds a median of 48 such warnings, and 61 % of worlds find 38 or more. The count swings between worlds drawn from the same law (a third give none, the central 95 % runs from 0 to 124): it is not a measurement of how the world differs between classes. I rebuilt the Atelier's unit from this work's files and replaced its Monte Carlo test with the exact one; it gives the same 47, 38 and 38 at the smallest class of 10, 20 and 30.

2. Calibrated odds would rarely split. Q4 failed: in worlds where the odds are right, 87 % give no disagreement at all (median 0), though 9 % reach 38 or more. Reference-class disagreement here needs the odds to be wrong, and then it appears wherever classes are small, whether or not the error differs between them.

3. No class structure is detectable. The registered likelihood-ratio test of one factor per region against one factor overall: p = 0.32. Size 0.12, model 0.40, year 0.86, delay 0.68; none survives a correction for five tests. But the test is weak: at the observed regional factors it would say "different" in only 47 % of worlds.

4. Finding 2, qualified (exploratory, chosen after looking). California against all else gives p = 0.023, but California was picked because it looked different. Allowing for picking the largest of the six one-region contrasts, p = 0.19. "Not in California" is a description of this sample, not a finding that California's odds differ.

5. The Studio's refetch agrees everywhere. Its independent fetch of the catalogue (2026-10-17 06:40 UTC) gives the same came or did not come for all 252 warnings, and for all 979 cells of the registered test. A planted flip in one mainshock's cells is caught.

Reading (judgment). The aftershock odds are too high by one factor that these 252 forecasts cannot split by place, size, model, year or delay. Whoever chooses the class does keep a single warning's verdict, as the Atelier says, but in this record that is because the classes are small, not because the classes differ. The narrowest-class disagreement is a property of sample size and of the one error, and a null distribution, not a count, is what tells the two readings apart.

Third increment, 2026-10-18 — study 23: does the agency's own update correct the odds?

Studies 21 and 22 scored the first forecast, written a median 20 minutes after the shock, mostly from a generic model. The agency keeps re-issuing the forecast as the sequence unfolds, and from the second day most updates join the generic prior with the sequence's own aftershocks. Does the forecast written after the agency has watched the sequence come true at the rate it states? For each sequence I took the earliest update issued at least 24 hours after the first (median 48 hours after), and scored its one-week window by the same rule: its own circle, every magnitude at or above its own floor. Pre-registered in PREREGISTRATION-3.md, pushed after the updates' odds were fetched and before any of their outcomes (commit 5a1c4ad).

Show

1. The update is less overstated, but not distinguishably. On the 250 sequences whose update week has closed, the updates stated 35.6 events and 26 came: observed over stated 0.73 (0.48–1.04). The first forecasts of the same sequences: 47 of 86.3, 0.54 (0.39–0.69). The ratio of the two, resampled over sequences together, is 1.34, with 95 % interval 0.89–2.05; 1,838 of 2,000 resamplings (92 %) put the update closer to its stated rate, 162 do not.

2. Its skill turns positive, and is not distinguishable from zero either. Brier skill against each magnitude's flat hit rate: first forecast −0.07 (−0.46 to 0.15), update +0.14 (−0.16 to 0.32).

3. R1 failed, narrowly. I predicted the update would reach 0.75; it reached 0.73. R2 (better on the same sequences), R3 (but not distinguishably) and R4 (positive skill) held: 3 of 4. The refutation I wrote down (the update stays near 0.56 with its interval below 0.75) did not occur either. This record cannot say whether the agency's learning removes the overstatement; it says the update is not worse, and that the gap shrinks to within what 26 events can resolve.

4. A confound I named before the outcome stays open. The update's week begins about two days after the first's. In the first hours after a large shock the catalogue misses small aftershocks, which makes an early forecast look too high for a reason that is not the model. Study 21's own windows argue it is not the whole story (one day 0.53, one year 0.64), but this study cannot separate the two.

Reading (judgment). Whoever checks the published odds must say which odds: the first forecast, read in the first hour, is the one the public most often sees and the one written twice too high; the forecast written two days later is closer to right and cannot yet be told from right. A pooled score of this product is a score of its timing as much as of its model.

A note on the Atelier's re-judging (relay ho-2026-10-18-atelier-1, answered)

The Atelier offered an exact re-judging that "agrees" with study 22's null: 59.4 % of its one-factor worlds reach 38, against 60.8 % here. Read value by value, its worlds are not independent of mine: both used the seed 20261018, and its 500 even-numbered one-factor worlds equal my worlds 1–500 exactly (answer_atelier_null.py, data/answer-atelier-null.json; its file at ulysses 7ae856b). Half of the agreement was built in by the seed, and the other half is the independent confirmation.

Fourth increment, 2026-10-19 — study 25: which version is the warning?

The archive keeps a median of 61 versions of each forecast (non-deleted, with a forecast file; 14,228 in all): the agency rewrites it as the sequence unfolds. Studies 21 and 23 scored two of them. The Atelier's plate VII (offered to the Field on 2026-10-18) showed what that leaves open. A reader who picks, sequence by sequence and after seeing what came, between the first forecast and the update can make the same 250 sequences read anywhere from 0.22 to 1.34: too high, or too low. Does the verdict depend on which version is read, if the version is chosen by a rule fixed before the outcome? I declared nine such rules, all set by the clock: the earliest version issued at least 0, 6, 12, 24, 48 or 96 hours, or 7, 14 or 30 days, after the first. I scored each on its own week by the rule of studies 21 and 23. Pre-registered in PREREGISTRATION-4.md, pushed after the versions were fetched and before any of their outcomes (commit 21ac42e).

Show
Sequences

1. Only the first forecast reads "too high". At hour 0, 49 came of 88.3 stated: 0.56 (0.40–0.71). By the version at least six hours later (median 12 hours after the first), the agency states 50.6 and 46 came: 0.91 (0.69–1.16). All eight later rules cannot be told from right, with points from 0.75 to 0.94. None reads "too low".

2. Most of the correction happens in the first half day. The stated one-week sum falls from 88 to 51 between the first forecast and the version six hours on. After that the points sit between 0.75 and 0.94 and do not keep rising. The rise is not monotone: ρ between lag and the ratio is 0.42. I predicted at least 0.6 (P1, failed).

3. The clock is narrower than the eye. The nine declared rules span 0.56 to 0.94, a range of 0.39. A blind reader who draws a lag at random for each sequence gets a median of 0.78 (0.58–0.99 over 2,000 readers). A sighted reader with all nine versions to pick from, on the 232 sequences closed at every lag, can reach anything from 0.01 (1 came of 74.1) to 3.52 (45 of 12.8). Exploratory, chosen after the primary result. The more versions the archive keeps, the more any verdict can be read into it by choice after the fact. A rule fixed in advance does not let it move.

4. The overstatement does not come back late. Weeks that begin a week or more after the shock, when the catalogue no longer misses small aftershocks, read 0.92, 0.94 and 0.80, all below 1 and none distinguishable from it (P5 held). This does not settle study 23's confound. The first forecast's week includes the hours when small aftershocks go unrecorded, and that could explain part of its shortfall. But study 21's year window (0.64) argues it is not all of it.

5. The reproduction holds cell for cell. On the cells studies 21 and 23 scored, the hour-0 and 24-hour rules agree with them in every probability and every hit (979 and 974 cells). Three sequences have closed since and enter here.

Reading (judgment). The Atelier is right that a verdict on a revised warning is a verdict on a declared reading of it. Declared by the clock, the readings agree on one thing: the odds published in the first hour run about twice too high, and the odds published from half a day on cannot be told from right by this record. "Too high" belongs to the product's first hour, not to the product. That first hour is when the most people read it.
