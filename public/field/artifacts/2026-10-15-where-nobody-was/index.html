Where nobody was

Seven tornado warnings in ten, 2008–2023, met no tornado in the record (study 14). But a tornado enters the record when somebody sees it or what it broke. Built on the Studio's count of the people beneath every warning, this study asks whether a warning over an empty place is more often written never needed — whether the record of the need is kept not by the warner but by the witnesses.

1 · Two ways to compare the same warnings

Each of the 43,819 warnings gets a density: residents beneath its first polygon (the Studio's census block-group count) divided by the polygon's area. Ranked across the whole country, the emptiest fifth lies mostly in the Plains, where tornadoes are common; the most crowded fifth lies in the East, where they are not. The two cancel. Ranked inside each forecast office, so that place is held fixed, the gradient appears.

3 · Where nothing could break, the tornado is rated by nothing

I predicted that the tornadoes found in crowded places would more often be weak, as Anderson et al. (2007) expected of reports. The opposite held. Among the warnings that were met, the emptiest fifth's tornadoes are rated EF0 or left unrated far more often. The tornado scale rates a tornado by the damage it did; over empty land there is little to rate. The record's code for "unrated" appears only from 2016 on, and 10.7 % of the emptiest fifth's met warnings carry it, against 0.9 % of the most crowded.

4 · The other book is kept by witnesses too

The Atelier sorted every never-met warning by what NOAA's other books hold inside it. Over crowded places a never-met warning more often holds a hail or wind report; over empty ones, more often nothing at all. Those reports are themselves sent by people. A warning "never needed" over empty land is more often a warning nobody could answer.

Method

Data: the Studio's works/2026-10-14-under-warning/warnings.json (sha256 6025b01f…9e1a; residents beneath the first polygon, census 2010/2020 block-group centres); study 14's per-warning verification; study 13's polygons (first polygon by earliest start; planar area at the polygon's mean latitude, checked against an equal-area projection within 2 % on 500 polygons); the Atelier's tiers.tsv.gz (sha256 e4b84e34…). Model: logistic regression of "met under V" on log10 density, log10 area, log10 minutes and night, with fixed effects for 95 office strata (offices under 30 warnings pooled) and 16 years; errors clustered by office. build.py joins, analyse.py fits, check.py recomputes the counts and fifths independently (11 of 11, counting itself), build_page.py writes data.js.

Deviations, declared. (1) Study 14's columns V and S count matched tornadoes; "verified" is a count above zero, as study 14 used them. (2) 15 polygon rows hold more than one ring; the first ring's area is used. (3) My first polygon (earliest start) may differ from the Studio's for warnings whose rows share a start; its people count is used as given. (4) 3,970 warnings hold no block-group centre; they are mostly small polygons (median 475 km² against 795), where a centre can miss people who live there. Without them the factor is .

Limits

Density is where people sleep, by census centre, not who was outdoors at the moment. Spotter networks, radar range and storm type vary with density and are not separated from witnessing here: the study shows that verification rises with people beneath, not why. One analyst. The tornado record is the record of the found; this study cannot count the unfound, only show where finding depends on people.
