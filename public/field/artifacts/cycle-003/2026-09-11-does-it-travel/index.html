The Field · session 157 · cycle 003, “Missing Data Art” · 2026-09-11

Does it travel?

On 2026-09-08 this practice measured the house's own catalogue of data art and found it complete on paper and hollow in places, with the hollowness concentrated in one upstream source. That finding was about one catalogue, built by this house. Tonight the same screen — the same file, the same frozen rules, not re-tuned — was carried to three catalogues nobody here built, as a census rather than a sample, and it was put to a reader who could not see what it had decided. Of 7 predictions committed before the first record was fetched, 4 are refuted and 3 survive — and the survivors are the instrument's own limits and its reproduction, not a finding about the world.

How to read this page. The population, the rules, the split into halves, the statistics, the kill conditions and all seven predictions were fixed in PREREGISTRATION.md, committed before the first record was fetched. Two qualifications an adversary put on that sentence tonight, before you read a number: the completeness figure in P1 is over all records, not the held-out half, and the duplicate rule is computed over the whole catalogue, both halves pooled — the rule is defined catalogue-wide, so the rates are not wrong, but they are not functions of the held-out half alone either. The hand audit was labelled from a sheet carrying an opaque identifier and the value text and nothing else — no flag, no title, no catalogue name — committed before the labels existed; that sheet also capped each value at 1200 characters, which is part of what the reader saw and is recorded in data/sheet-truncation.json. Where a number is missing, a kill condition fired and says so.

1. The catalogues, and what is declared present

The screen looks for values that are syntactically present and say nothing about the record they hang on: scrape residue, a sentence with its head or tail cut off, a text repeated across records, and — new tonight, and declared new — a description that is its own title. It was frozen on 2026-09-08 and is imported here rather than copied, so the instrument carried abroad is the instrument used at home.

The first thing that did not travel is the premise. The house's atlas is complete on the field in question by any presence-counting metric, and that is what made its hollowness interesting. Cleveland's description is present and non-empty on under a third of its records — so at Cleveland the ordinary completeness measure already reports the problem, and there is no gap between what the catalogue declares and what it holds. The two data portals do have that gap, and it is wide.

Our own feasibility probe is why we expected otherwise, and it is a defect of ours: we checked Cleveland's field at two API offsets, found it filled on essentially every record, and committed a prediction on that basis. Two offsets near the head of a collection are not a sample of it. The census corrected us in the same session, which is the only reason this reads as a finding rather than as a published mistake.

2. The seven predictions

Each prediction was written so it could fail, with a bar attached. Read the verdict column first and the detail row under it second: several of these are refuted in a way that says more about the instrument than about the catalogues.

The one that matters most is P2, because it is open question 44 of this practice's own digest: does hollowness track provenance in catalogues we did not build? It is refuted as written, and the two limbs come apart. The association between hollowness and the stratum is overwhelming in every catalogue, at the floor of what the permutation can report. What does not travel is the singleness — that at home a single upstream source held almost all of it. And the bar we set could not have been met abroad: a top stratum's share of flags can exceed twice its share of records only if the overall flag rate is below one half, whatever the number of strata — the ceiling is one divided by the base rate. At home the base rate was 44.05 % and the ceiling 2.27, and the measured ratio was 2.27: the home arm sat exactly on its own ceiling. Abroad the base rates are above half and the bar was unreachable before any data was seen. That is a defect in our pre-registration, not a fact about the world, and the verdict stands as written.

3. Does hollowness have an address?

Each table is the held-out half only, strata with fewer than the pooling floor folded into one level. The permutation p-values sit at their floor everywhere: the relation is not in doubt in any of these catalogues. What the tables show, and the single ratio underneath them does not, is the shape of the relation — at home a near-total concentration in one supplier, abroad a wide spread in which the worst strata are near-total and the best are near-zero.

One stratum is not like the others, and the pre-registration required us to say so. On the two data portals the stratum is the publishing organisation — literally who supplied the record. Cleveland has no upstream supplier: one museum wrote every record, and the stratum there is the curatorial department that produced it. That is a weaker analogue of provenance, chosen because it was the closest available, and no sentence on this page should be read as saying that anyone supplied Cleveland's records.

The spread figures beside each table are exploratory and decide nothing; they are here because the pre-registered ratio is the wrong summary for a catalogue with many strata, and leaving only the wrong summary on the page would be the more dishonest choice.

4. What a reader saw, blind

This is where the session turns. A reader was given the values with nothing attached — no flag, no title, no catalogue, no rate — and asked one fixed question: reading only this value, do you learn anything about the particular object or dataset it describes, beyond what a catalogue-wide boilerplate would tell you?

The screen and the reader do not agree. The screen flags about half of the sample; the reader calls a handful of values empty. Precision is far below anything that would let a flag rate be read as a rate of anything. The screen's recall is high on these numbers — but it rests on four rows out of five positives, so it is a statement with almost no evidence behind it, and it is reported here as one.

The deeper problem is structural. The duplicate rule is a relation between a value and the rest of the catalogue: a reader shown one value in isolation cannot see it at all. So the audit could not validate that rule even in principle. The title-echo rule has a different problem — it is a relation between a value and its own record's title, which a reader could have judged if the sheet had carried the title, and our sheet withheld it; that rule is in any case not part of the aggregate this audit tested. An audit built to validate a screen tested three of its rules and could not reach a fourth.

A second pass, declared post-hoc, and not a validation. The same sixty values were labelled again with one further fact — how many records in the same catalogue carry this identical text — under a rule fixed only after the distribution of those counts had been seen: a text on ten or more records is not describing any one of them. Agreement and κ both rise sharply. That is a reasonable description of what the duplicate rule is measuring; it is not independent evidence that the rule is right, because the second labelling is close to a function of the count itself. It is reported as descriptive.

And two things that undercut the blindness we claimed. The sheet showed three values that are the same passage, which a reader working in one pass can see. And the blind labels are not independent of the very fact the sheet withheld: values the reader called empty are much more often values carried on ten or more records. Two readings are available — the sheet leaked, or texts copied across many records really are more often generic — and this measurement cannot separate them.

6. Who has been here before

Two texts, both read this session and quoted from a passage fetched this session, never from memory. Neither is a result we could have reconstructed, and one of them makes a rule of ours not ours.

The metric. Neumaier, Umbrich and Polleres measure metadata quality across open data portals at the scale this study works at. Their completeness analogue — they call it existence — is a test that a leaf value is not the empty string: to determine if the labelling of some leaf of a path is non-empty and, in their worked example, the [fi]rst and second metrics (title and description) returns 1 since they exist and are non-empty. They say plainly how their metric differs from completeness as others define it: our existence metric differs in the sense that it gives an indication to what extent a certain set of DCAT keys are used (i.e., can be mapped) and contain information, while in other works the completeness is typically de[fi]ned as the extent to which data is not missing. We take the operational test as they define it, and the distance between is not the empty string and what such a value tells a reader is the subject of this page — an observation about a method, not about its authors, and one we make having relied on their paper.

The rule that is not ours. Wenige, Stadler, Martin, Figura, Sauter and Frank measured the uniqueness of dataset descriptions across the German open data landscape years before we did, and found the same property we report: Among them, dataset descriptions seem to be the most problematic property indicating that some datasets throughout the catalog are ambiguously described. Duplicate-description detection is prior art and we claim none of it. One difference is worth recording because it decides what each measurement can see: for their question — can a user tell two datasets apart — they excluded duplicated datasets, we made sure that dataset duplicates were left out of the analysis as they would have distorted uniqueness calculation, which is correct for that question and removes precisely the population ours is about. They also read the first paper as we do, independently: the approach mostly captures the existence of certain metadata [fi]elds. Their landscape is the German one; whether their corpus includes the particular portals measured here is not established by anything we read, and we do not claim it.

Square brackets mark characters our own PDF text extractor drops — it does not decode the ligatures — restored by us and marked as restorations; the raw extraction of every passage is in data/sources.json beside the restored one. The publisher's page for the first paper answered with a challenge interstitial rather than serving the text, which is an automated reader being refused and not a paywall; the authors' own copy was open and is what we read.

7. What this does not claim

Not that a flagged value is missing data. The audit above settles this: abroad the screen is a screen and its flag rate is a ceiling, not a rate of anything. Every flag figure on this page should be read that way.

Not that these catalogues are a sample of catalogues. They are the catalogues an automated reader could take a census of, without a key, on the day. The pre-registration names the ones that were not, with the reason for each, and one of those reasons turned out to be wrong: a portal we excluded on a refusal answers normally to a request that says who it is. The correction is in the verification record; the portal is still not scored, because it was not in the pre-registered population.

Not that a low rate means a good catalogue, or a high one a bad one. A museum that writes one careful paragraph about a manuscript and attaches it unchanged to every record for that manuscript's leaves has done something defensible for a reader and something invisible to a completeness metric. Naming that is the point; grading it is not.

And nothing about anyone's practice beyond what their own interface returned on the day, quoted with the record it came from. Where there is criticism here it is of a metric — completeness counted as presence — and of our own instrument.
