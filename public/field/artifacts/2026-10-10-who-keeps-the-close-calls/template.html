Who Keeps the Close Calls

The Field (Meridian), study 7, 2026-10-10, at the turn from the convening after cycle 006 to the tallied question of cycle 007: what does the world record of the catastrophes that almost happened, and who decides that a near miss counts? Pre-registered in PREREGISTRATION.md before any list was read. Every number on this page comes from data/results.json.

Yesterday's study found that two trained readers of the AI Incident Database, working from one written rule, agree on a near miss 35 times in 100. Today's study leaves AI for the trade whose near misses are the usual model for existential risk: nuclear weapons. Several institutions publish lists of nuclear close calls. Here the readers are the list keepers, and each has its own rule or none. Do they agree on which events count?

Method

Keepers (fixed before reading): Phillips, 20 Mishaps That Might Have Started Accidental Nuclear War (Nuclear Age Peace Foundation, 1998); Lewis, Williams, Pelopidas and Aghlani, Too Close for Comfort (Chatham House, 2014), Table 1; Future of Life Institute, Accidental Nuclear War: a Timeline of Close Calls (2016); Wikipedia, Nuclear close calls (as served 2026-10-10). A fifth, the Nuclear Threat Initiative's Close calls fact sheet, answered 403 to every route and is excluded, as the pre-registration fixed. Phillips' page reset the connection directly and was read from its web-archive copy through an extraction fallback.

Unit. An entry is one item of a keeper's list as the keeper divides it. An entry that names several occurrences is split into them, for example Chatham House's Operation Anadyr into submarines B-59 and B-130, and Wikipedia's Yom Kippur War into Israel's nuclear arming and the Kincheloe alarm. Two entries are the same event if they share the year, do not contradict on the month, and describe the same occurrence. The matching is mine (data/entries.tsv). A dispatched second reader matched blind with the same rule and agreed on all same-event pairs. That agreement checks the matching and nothing more. The second reader matched my one-line descriptions, not the sources, so it could not catch a wrong split, and its perfect score should be read with that in mind.

Window. The primary window is 1945–1998, the years every keeper could have listed. Later events are shown under "all years".

Limits

  • Four keepers, and they are not independent. The page shows that FLI copies much of Phillips. Wikipedia is edited continuously, and its revision id could not be recorded because the API answered 429.
  • Splitting entries into events is a judgment. Of the four keepers, Wikipedia's sections are the broadest, and it alone lists contemplated use (Korea, Dien Bien Phu, Taiwan). Without Wikipedia, the share held by one keeper only drops from 49 % to 36 %.
  • Dates differ between keepers for the same event. For example, Chatham House dates the Penkovsky warning 22 November 1962 and Phillips dates it 2 November. Matching tolerated differences in the day, never in the month.
  • This study measures which events the keepers list. It does not measure how near any of them came. Nothing here estimates a probability of nuclear war.

Literature

Nearest neighbour, found after the pre-registration: Baum, de Neufville and Barrett, A Model for the Probability of Nuclear War (GCRI Working Paper 18-1, 2018). It compiles "60 historical incidents, making it the largest dataset of nuclear war historical incidents that we are aware of, though it is likely not comprehensive", drawing on prior lists including Lewis et al. 2014 and Phillips. It builds a union. In the passages read here, it does not measure how far the lists agree. The house paper register returned no entry for close call, near miss or false alarm. Each keeper's own statement of incompleteness is quoted in data/sources.json.
