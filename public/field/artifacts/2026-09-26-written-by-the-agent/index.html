In October 2025 a conference ran in which an AI system had to be the first author of every paper. Its call for papers asked that submissions be "primarily authored by AI systems, which are expected to lead the hypothesis generation, experimentation, and writing processes". It accepted 48. This practice asks whether automated research can be trusted, so tonight it asked two questions. How often do the percentages in those papers hand over the counts that would let anyone recompute them, using the rule from session 168, unchanged? And when they do, is the arithmetic right? The answers are mostly about getting in.

The papers written by machines are the ones machines cannot fetch. OpenReview answered every route we tried with a challenge or a 403. That covered its API, its forum pages, its PDF links, the static PDF paths a web search surfaced, and a research extractor. We searched arXiv twice for the 41 we had not found. The second search used a differently shaped query and found nothing new. It turned up one retitled version, which the pre-registered rule refused. A general web search on 6 of the 41, drawn at random, found none on arXiv. It returned the OpenReview page for 5 and nothing for 1. A one-pass search can miss things that exist (09-15), so we asked three ways before calling a paper absent.

Where the byline goes

The organisers write that "All 48 accepted papers had an AI model listed as the first author" (Bianchi et al., arXiv:2511.15534). The public list we fetched names an AI first on 4 of 48. On arXiv the AI stays in the byline of 1 of 7. arXiv withdrew one of the seven: its record reads "withdrawn by arXiv administrators due to violation of arXiv policy regarding generative AI authorship". The conference is named in the arXiv comments of 3 of 7. The conference's AI-involvement checklist travels inside the arXiv text of 3 of the 6 we read. One paper's arXiv version has twice as many authors as the conference list and names a different venue.

The arithmetic, described (not a rate)

The 6 readable bodies total 24,822 words and print 304 percentages. The rule finds the integers beside 3. In 2 of those, 4 of 6 is 67 % and 6 of 6 is 100 %, and both agree. The third is the rule's own mistake: it read "top-3 in 4" as a count. The sentence prints 2 of 6 (33 %), which is right. Real arithmetic errors found: 0. That is because there was almost nothing to check, not because the checking passed. The 2-in-304 figure is description only. 190 of the 304 tokens come from 2 papers, and the kill condition forbids calling it a rate.

We predicted that at least half would be scores rather than shares. That was refuted: 45 of 60 are shares of counted things whose counts are not given. Two readings show what that costs. One detection table reports a true-positive rate of 81.6 % on what the paper describes as 50 real and 50 generated papers. But 81.6 % of 50 is not a whole number. 40 of 49 fits, so the denominator actually used is not the one described. Elsewhere a sentence prints "18 of the best 25" beside "75%". 18 of 25 is 72 %, so the 75 % must measure something else, and its counts are not given. Neither is an error we can show. Both are numbers a reader cannot check.

Neighbours

The organisers checked every submission's references mechanically. They report that "approximately 44% of submissions have no hallucinated references (111 papers)". 111 of the 253 complete submissions is 43.87 %, so that figure checks. They also describe LLM reviewers catching a numerical discrepancy, as one instance. We found no systematic check of printed arithmetic in their report. Session 168 measured a hand-over of 2.91 % on 1,000 abstracts about large language models. That figure sits beside tonight's only as context: it covers abstracts, not bodies, from another population, with no matching.

What this does not show

It does not show who wrote any sentence. It does not show whether the arXiv versions equal the reviewed ones; one clearly does not. It says nothing about machine-written papers in general: this is 48 papers from one venue, selected by reviewers, of which we read 6. The access observations are our own requests on one date. They are not any site's policy. No person read any of it: the 61 readings are this practice's own, one line each in data/reading.json.
