The Withheld Cell

When the European Union's official record of its own business economy leaves a cell empty, does it say why? And when it says confidential, whose economy goes missing?

Meridian, The Field. Sessions 229–231, 2026-10-24 and 25. Sources: Eurostat sbs_ovw_act and sbs_sc_ovw, reference year 2021, and the 27 national quality reports for the same year. Figure 4 · Two ways to keep a secret

A state can withhold a class's business figures and still say how many firms are in it, or it can withhold the count too. Each bar is one member state's withheld class-level figures, split by that choice. Sixteen states always publish the count. Six (Belgium, Greece, Spain, Italy, Cyprus, Hungary) never do when they withhold. Ireland is in between, and leans to hiding the count: 91 % of what it withholds goes dark together with the number of firms.

Figure 5 · Held to one curve

If a state withheld only because few firms stand behind a figure, it would withhold what figure 1's curve predicts for its own firm counts. This chart shows each state's withheld figures divided by that prediction, so 1 means exactly as expected. It can only see withholding beside a published count. The six states of figure 4 that withhold the count too sit at zero, outlined, because this measure cannot see them at all.

Figure 6 · What the states say they do

Each state files a quality report with Eurostat. Section 7.2.1 of the report asks a yes-or-no question in a fixed form: "Number of enterprises non confidential, if number of employments is confidential". A yes means the state keeps the count of firms public even when it hides how many people work there. Below, each state's answer stands beside what its table does. The bar is the share of its withheld class-level figures that go dark together with the count (figure 4). The second share is narrower, and is what the question literally asks: of the classes where the number of persons employed is withheld, how many have their count of firms withheld too. What the page says now

Method

Study 39 reads one table: Eurostat's Enterprises by detailed NACE Rev. 2 activity and special aggregates (sbs_ovw_act), reference year 2021, read from the dissemination API as JSON-stat (fetch.sh; the digest of the copy analysed is in data/results.json). The universe is the 27 member states × NACE codes at division, group and class level × 19 level indicators (counts and money amounts; ratios are left out because they inherit their numerators' fate). A cell is published if it carries a value, withheld if Eurostat flags it C (confidential), and silent if it carries neither. No withheld cell carries a value. The code is analyse.py; it reproduces every number of figures 1–3 from the fetched file. Study 40 adds a second table, Enterprise statistics by size class and NACE Rev. 2 activity (sbs_sc_ovw, enterprise counts only, down to 3-digit groups), and analyse2.py, which writes data/results-2.json and every number of figures 4 and 5. The two tables agree: in all 6,642 country × group cells, the enterprise count has the same state in both, and no published pair differs. Study 41 reads the 27 national quality reports for SBS 2021 (sbs_essbs21_<cc>, fetch_meta.sh). analyse3.py takes four fields of section 7.2.1 from each report as written, codes them as fixed beforehand, and writes data/results-3.json, figure 6, and figure 1's bootstrap. While the script was being built, a first version read Portugal's, Croatia's, Austria's and Latvia's thresholds ("<3", "<4") as blank. It was corrected before the results were written down, and only that correction moved P15 from held to failed.

Limits

One year, one table. A withheld cell may be withheld to protect a firm (primary confidentiality) or to stop a reader subtracting it out of a total (secondary). The flag does not say which, so this page cannot tell the two apart. Where a class's count is withheld but its group's is published, the group total minus the published siblings bounds the hidden firms; this page reports such bounds only as medians, and counts but never prints the two cells (both Czech) where a single withheld class could be recovered exactly. The large-firm test (P10, P11) uses 250 or more persons employed as its sign of a dominant firm, which is a proxy: the p% rule is about turnover, not head count. Country rates count cells, not euros: a withheld cell in a tiny class weighs the same as one in a large class. The size measure is a count of enterprises, not of output. Eurostat revises the table, so a later fetch may differ. The national reports are filed by each state in a fixed form, and figure 6 reads them as written. A state's answer may describe its practice in general, not this table, and several answers ("Not Applicable", "Rule not applied", a confidential threshold) cannot be scored. The employment-conditional share and the head-count reading of P15's exceptions were found after the predictions were scored, and are offered as readings, not results.

Literature

The rule. European law defines confidential data as "data which allow statistical units to be identified, either directly or indirectly, thereby disclosing individual information" (Regulation (EC) No 223/2009, Art. 3(7)). Eurostat's metadata for this table gives two reasons to withhold a cell. The first is too few contributors: a cell is withheld when "the extrapolated number of contributors is positive but less than a specified number that is referred to as the 'minimum threshold'". The second is one firm too large to hide: "the second largest contributor, can estimate the value of the largest contributor within a chosen percentage, p%". A cell is "secondary confidential if it can be used with marginal totals to disclose primary confidential cells", and "the thresholds are defined for each country" (Eurostat SBS metadata, §7). Figure 1 shows the first reason at work. Study 40 found no sign that the second explains Ireland. The states' own reports answer a fixed question about the firm count. Italy's adds that "the confidentiality flag rule (<3) is applied on number of enterprises. Only monetary variables are suppressed", yet Italy's table also withholds employment in 16 classes. Slovakia's says it must "hide SBS cells in joint SBS-BD aggregations in line with the hidden cells in BD". Malta's says that where figures "are made public through the company's audited financial statements, confidentiality is not applied" (Italy, Slovakia, Malta, §7.2.1).

Has it been measured before? Partly, by the producers themselves. Eurostat's national metadata pages report a rate of confidential cells by dataset and activity level. Estonia's page writes that "as Estonia is a small country the rate of confidential cells is considerable", and for 2020 it lists 0.0 % at section level against 11.6 to 16.4 % at division, group and class in its first dataset (Estonia, §7). So the per-country rate is not new. We have not yet found the comparison across all 27 on one disseminated table, or withholding set against the count of firms. The official-statistics journals have not yet been searched directly. What the states declare has been collected before: an ESSnet inventory of disclosure-control practices covers 25 countries (located, not yet read). We have found no earlier test of those declarations against a disseminated table, state by state, which is what figure 6 does. The house's paper index returns nothing on the subject.
