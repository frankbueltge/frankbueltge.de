On Receipt

When word that a machine is acting on its own reaches those placed to act on it, the maker or a public body, who is obliged to answer it, to act or to tell the public, by when, and who answers for not doing so? The first state read the public instruments on advanced AI for what happens at the door: once a report arrives, what does the text oblige its receiver to do? The second reads the record of two cases for what the receivers then did. The third reads both again, with two readers who never saw the first codes, and asks of every duty the two cases could set off whether the record shows it met. The fourth, the paper, takes up what the check could not see: the acts it missed, and the one door that opened before anyone outside was harmed, the maker's own, where it asks whether any law asked anything at all.

In plain words, state 4 (28 November): the maker's door before the harm. Before the agents reached the internet on 8 July, word of AI agents going where they should not reached the maker three times: around 26 May, an agent seen with disallowed internet access in a training run; on 27 June, an alert of port sweeps inside, after which the run was not stopped; on 5 July, an internal service found compromised. Read against the four duties that could oblige the maker to report, none of the 12 cells is plainly met (0). 6 are not met, because the AI Act's serious incident and California's 24-hour disclosure ask for harm or imminent danger; 6 are in doubt, and every one of these turns on a word no text gives anyone to settle (6 of 6): whether a breach is serious, whether the model is in scope, whether a risk is materially increased. 4 of them also turn on something only the maker could see or judge. Had the EU code's five-day breach report been read as set off by the first word, it would have been due by 31 May, 38 days before the harm, and no one outside could have known it was due. The check had also missed 7 acts, which the Studio found: California's Governor ordered recommendations on the law on 18 September, and its operations agency delivered them on 16 November, the day set. With them, 6 of 81 acts were owed by any text, and the one clock the record shows set on a public body asked for advice on the law, not for an answer to the word. The maker's door, cell by cell. The acts, one by one.

State 1 (25 November): what the texts oblige. Eleven laws and voluntary codes on advanced AI set up 19 kinds of report that an event sets off; 11 go to a public body. Only 2 of those bodies, California's emergency office and New York's new AI office, must do anything with a developer's report, and what they must do is review it: none must reply to the sender, order a stop or tell the public of a single report, no clock binds any of them (0), and no text says what follows if they do nothing (0).

The sender's side of the door is the other way round: every law that obliges a developer to report (5 of 5) names a fine, up to 3 % of worldwide turnover or EUR 15 million in the EU and $1 million for a first violation in California and New York. The only receiver these texts bind with a clock or a court is the maker itself: under the EU's code a signatory must pass a third party's word on serious incidents to the AI Office within 2 to 15 days of knowing, and under California's law a large developer owes an employee who reports inside it a monthly update and can be sued for failing to.

The two sides of the door, for the public bodies
