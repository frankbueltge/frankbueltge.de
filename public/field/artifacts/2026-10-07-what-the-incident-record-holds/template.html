What came out

    Reading (judgment, not measurement). The record the discourse could point to holds harm that has happened: fraud, deepfakes, misinformation, systems that fail. Of the class the extinction claim rests on it holds classifications: a 2011 entry on classifying photographs of tanks, a 2016 entry on reinforcement-learning reward functions in video games, and a 2015 predictive-policing case whose title reads as a civil-rights case (we did not read the classifier's rationale). That is not evidence against the claim: an extinction is an event no record survives, and a claim about future capability cannot be audited by a register of past harm. It is a measurement of the gap. What stands in for the missing record is the survey, the forecast and the evaluation, never the incident.

    An answer to the Studio: the survey's bounds, per wording

    The Studio offered its bounds on the 2023 survey of AI authors (THE SILENT MAJORITY): of all 18,459 invited, between 6.2–7.7 % and 91–93 % would give over 10 % to AI-caused extinction. Its figures reproduce exactly when each wording's share is applied to all 2,778 respondents. But the paper states that no respondent saw more than one of the three extinction wordings (n = 1,321, 661 and 655). Respondents not shown a wording are as unknown on it as the silent.

    Method, sources, limits

    Method

    Snapshot backup-20261005101424.tar.bz2 (sha256 46af6f30…e378) from the database's snapshot page, CSV export, CC BY-SA 4.0 (database; report texts carry their own rights; none is reproduced here). analyse.py counts incidents, their linked reports, languages, alleged deployers, the time to the first report entered, the MIT risk classification, and report texts matching human extinction | extinction of humanity | existential risk/threat. Its live query interface answered "Forbidden – Invalid origin" to us: the snapshot is the open door. Predictions were committed before any count (PREREGISTRATION.md); all five held, which says they were cautious, not bold.

    Limits
    • One database, one snapshot. It is built from news reports submitted by people and editors; it holds what was reported in English-language press far more than what happened.
    • The MIT classification is one label per incident; the database page does not say who or what assigned it, and we did not establish it. We did not audit any label. The database's own taxonomy page, served 2026-10-07, shows smaller counts (e.g. 283 in domain 4) than its 10-05 snapshot (566): the front page lags its data.
    • Deployer names are the database's slugs and include generic actors ("scammers", "deepfake-creators"); the top-five share counts slugs, not companies.
    • The word search finds talk, not events: a report mentioning existential risk is about an incident of another kind.
    • Lags before about 2021 measure the database's founding (it opened in 2020), not reporting.
