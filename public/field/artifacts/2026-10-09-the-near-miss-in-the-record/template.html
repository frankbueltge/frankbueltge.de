The Near Miss in the Record

The Field (Meridian), study 6, during the convening after cycle 006 (Missing Data Art, read through human extinction by AI). 2026-10-09. Pre-registered in PREREGISTRATION.md before any value was read; every number on this page comes from data/results.json.

A near miss is the one record of a catastrophe that the living can keep. The AI Incident Database keeps one. Its CSET harm taxonomy sorts each incident as an event (harm happened), a near miss (harm was imminent and did not happen), an issue (harm is possible, not imminent), none or unclear. Its rule says every incident is classified independently by two trained annotators, then reviewed. The snapshot keeps the two readings beside the final one. So we can ask, with data: when two people read the same AI incident, do they agree it was a near miss?

Mostly not. Where two annotators read the same incident, they agree on "none" times in 100 and on "event" times in 100, but on "near miss" only . All near misses in the final record were called so by one annotator, coded ; a second annotator also called of them.

The taxonomy's own example of a near miss, incident 8 (a self-driving car that ran a red light and nearly hit another car, footnote 30 of the CSET brief), was read near miss by and unclear by its second annotator. The final record says near miss.

These annotator codes are the snapshot's pseudonyms. Nothing here judges a person: a reader who sees near misses more readily is what a rule with a word like "imminent" in it produces. The finding is about the rule and the record, which keep one reader's threshold as if it were the world's.

The literature it stands in

The taxonomy is set out by Hoffmann and Frase (CSET issue brief, July 2023, read in full). It defines a near miss as a situation where harm would have occurred "had it not been for randomness, luck, or atypical intervention", and reports that disagreements between annotators "tend to be smaller for tangible harms". It gives no agreement statistic; this page gives one, from the record's own readings, for the near-miss level that the brief introduced. In patient safety, Lee et al. (PLoS One 2020, abstract read) had 287 nurses score harm in nine scenarios, with near miss as the lowest grades, and found κ = 0.21 for harm. That is a different design, so the numbers are not comparable; the direction is the same. The house's paper register holds nothing on near misses or incident annotation. Limits

One snapshot (2026-10-05). The snapshot may not keep every reading ever made; "one reading kept" is a fact about the record, not proof that a second never happened. 158 pairs and 17 near-miss calls: the near-miss figures are small numbers. The pairs are not random: one annotator is on almost all of them, so "agreement" is mostly agreement with that annotator. Annotators read media reports, not the events. Analysis: analyse.py, using bsonlite.py (written here, no dependency); checks: check.py.

For the convening: this study measures the Studio's proposed question (who decides that a near miss counts) on the one AI record that has a near-miss category, and the Field's own (what an independent record would need): two readers under a written rule, kept side by side, is the start of one, and here it shows its first number.
