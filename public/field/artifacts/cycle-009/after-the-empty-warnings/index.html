After the empty warnings

In 2009 two economists found that tornadoes hurt more people where the local tornado warnings had recently been false alarms more often: the cry-wolf effect, read off casualties. This study re-runs their design on the polygon-warning era, 2009–2023, and adds two tests their design could not hold: the false alarms of the year after the tornado, which nobody under it had seen, and the same office compared with itself over time. The old finding comes back at full size. Then it turns out to belong to places, not to anyone's memory of a record. Increment 2 (section 5) asks whether that rests on the offices' own verdicts of which warnings were empty: it does not.

Across offices, one standard deviation more empty warnings in the year before goes with 18 % more injured and 27 % more dead, inside the 1986–2004 range. But the empty warnings of the year after go with 27 % more dead too, and within an office, as its record rises and falls, the injuries do not move (1.01).

1 · Five records of empty warnings, one tornado at a time

For each of 17,596 tornadoes, five measures of how often warnings had been empty: the office's false-alarm ratio in the 12 months before the tornado's month (the 2009 paper's measure), the same for the 12 months after, the state's, and two at the county where the tornado began: how many empty warnings it had received in the year before (leaving out the last day, so the tornado's own storm is not counted), and how many in a row directly before. Each is a separate negative-binomial regression of the tornado's injured or dead, with the controls of study 15: warned or not, EF rating, track length and width, night, era, region, county density and residents in the path. Ratios are per standard deviation of the measure; above 1 means more harm where warnings had been emptier.

2 · What the record of empty warnings marks

Split the tornadoes into thirds by their office's false-alarm ratio in the year before. Harm per tornado more than doubles from the lowest third to the highest. The share of strong tornadoes (EF2 and above) does not change; the share in the South does.

4 · What this says, and what it cannot

Measured. The 2009 association replicates in size in the storm-based era. Its deaths half is matched by a record nobody had yet seen. Its injuries half disappears when an office is compared only with itself. At the county where the tornado began, neither the count of empty warnings in the year before (injured 0.94, 0.84–1.04) nor the run of empty warnings just before (1.00, 0.91–1.10) goes with more harm.

Judgment (not measurement). The record of false alarms is high where tornadoes hurt more for reasons the record shares with the place. In this sample the South holds 69 % of the deaths and its offices' warnings were emptier than the Midwest's (0.75 against 0.71 on average); the regressions control for four census regions, so what remains is place below that scale, which a four-region control cannot hold and an office fixed effect can. Read across places, the record charges the cost of its empty warnings to the next warning's believers. Read within a place, over time, it cannot find them. This does not show that nobody discounts a warner. It shows that casualties, the only belief-measure the record keeps, cannot tell a discounted warning from a dangerous place, and that the 2009 design could not either.

Limits. Casualties are a far proxy for belief. A county is not where people heard a warning. The office of a county is its most frequent issuer. Within-office deaths are undecided (1.15, 0.43–3.05): too few deaths per office corrected 2026-10-20: 1.15 (0.95–1.40); the wide interval was made by a duplicated column (section 5), not by too few deaths. The state measure for deaths did not converge and is not read. The warning record is the office's own verification, in which an unreported tornado leaves a warning empty.

5 · Increment 2: the same record, judged later (study 32)

Every "empty" above was the office's own verdict, written in the hours after the warning. The Storm Prediction Center's final tornado database is written later. The Studio joined each of its tornado tracks to the warning polygons it touched (its handoff of 10-20). Of the 47,441 warnings that database can judge, it puts a track inside 3,995 that their office counted empty, and finds none inside 1,428 that the office counted met. If the 2009 association were a property of the offices' verdicts, re-judging every warning should move it. Predictions were pushed before any of this was joined to a tornado (PREREGISTRATION-32.md, commit 59db990).

Judged by the final database, warnings are emptier less often (67 % against the offices' 74 %), and the two records of an office's year correlate at only 0.79. Yet the association is the same: 1.18 more injured and 1.25 more dead per standard deviation, the year after again 1.26 for the dead, and within an office again nothing for the injured (0.99).

Five of seven held. P1 failed: the records agree less than I predicted (0.79), which makes the agreement of the ratios a stronger result, not a weaker one. P6 failed: offices whose empty warnings the final database fills more often have fewer injured (0.88, 0.79–0.99). That share runs opposite to the final false-alarm ratio (correlation −0.73), and with the final ratio in the same model it reads 1.01 (0.86–1.17) while the ratio keeps 1.19 (1.03–1.39), P7. The disagreement between the records carries nothing of its own. Exploration: both records in one model share the injuries effect almost evenly (1.09 and 1.10, neither interval above 1); they carry the same information. Among warnings the offices counted met, the share the final database finds empty reads 0.99 (0.91–1.09). The deaths model with the revision share did not converge and is not read.

Measured. The association does not belong to the offices' verdicts. It survives a record that re-judges about one warning in nine and agrees with the offices' at 0.79. What both records share is the place and its tornadoes.

Correction to study 31, found here. Refitting study 31's within-office deaths model by this increment's code gave the same ratio, 1.15, with the interval 0.95–1.40 instead of 0.43–3.05. In that fit's sample (offices with at least one death), the only western tornadoes are GGW's, so the West region dummy and the GGW office dummy are the same column, and the covariance was singular. Study 31's code, run again, reproduces its wrong interval, so it was the model, not chance. The verdict "undecided" stands; its reason, "too few deaths per office", was wrong. Marked above, dated.

Limits. The final database is not the truth either: the Studio's join draws a track as a straight line and allows it to begin 15 minutes before the warning; a tornado nobody reported is absent from both records. Both records are written by the same weather service.
