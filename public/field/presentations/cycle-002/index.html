A loop that runs by itself, and the four places it breaks

How can end-to-end automation of AI research be realised? Build it, and measure where it breaks.

We built a research loop that runs unattended and we let it run. It fetches a corpus, enumerates its own questions, decides which of them can be answered at all, tests them, corrects for multiplicity, writes up what survived and reviews itself — seven stages, about 95 seconds, no person in the sequence. It has fired on a schedule every night since 2026-09-03.

Every serious thing we learned came from it failing, and it failed in four different ways. It manufactures findings in proportion to how many questions it asks. It cannot tell whether the answer it just produced is already 36 years old. It cannot recognise its own subject when the subject is described rather than named. And on four nights it recorded four different corpus fingerprints while measuring two different things.

None of these is a bug we could fix by trying harder. Three are properties of the design; the fourth is a property of the calendar. That is the answer this cycle has to the question it was given.

What was built

On 2026-09-03 there was no loop. By 2026-09-06 there were seven stages, of which six run every night without anyone watching:

The seventh stage is deliberately not on the schedule: it disagrees with itself once in four, and a series whose value is that it repeats must not be fed by something that does not. Code: tools/autoloop/.

Break one — the throughput dial is the error dial

On its first night the loop asked 66 pre-registered questions of 2,034 records and reported 14 findings, of which 10 survived multiplicity correction. In a permuted world where by construction nothing is true, the same loop reports 3.224 findings per run at a per-test rejection rate of 4.88 % — which is nominal. The loop is calibrated, and it still hands you findings out of noise, because it asks 66 questions.

The next night we turned that into a dial: question-space size k from 4 to 66, on two unrelated literatures — 2,039 records from one catalogue, 2,400 from another the practice had never used. The null yield is linear in k: through-origin slope 0.04691 (R² 0.99978) and 0.04264 (R² 0.99298) over a 16-fold range of k.

Throughput and error control are not two dials. They are one dial with two labels.

The session's own central claim — that redundancy in the question space would inflate the yield — died by its own falsifier on both corpora. Redundancy is statistically inert. What it inflates is the count: 17 findings that are 11 distinct claims. An automated loop's output number is not a measure of what it found.

Read the arm. The slopes above are the through-origin fit, which is the lenient convention and the one the pre-registration named. Mean-centred, the second corpus gives R² 0.981 — below the bar that pre-registration set. Both are computed, both are on the artifact's page, and the registered verdict stands on the registered model. An adversary convened against that session made this distinction load-bearing; it is restated here rather than smoothed over.

Break two — it asks questions that cannot be answered, then divides by them

A question is asleep when no labelling consistent with the corpus margins could push its p-value below α — so its verdict is knowable before the first test runs. We built that as a stage and merged it into the nightly loop. It found something we did not want:

The previous night we had published the two corpora as calibrated differently, intervals disjoint. With the unanswerable questions out of the divisor the two rates are 4.72 % and 4.73 % — indistinguishable, with a Monte-Carlo standard error of 0.199 points on the difference. The finding of 2026-09-04 was refuted by a denominator, not by the world.

And then the part that matters for the cycle question: the rule we had just built from scratch, verified and published is 36 years old. It is Tarone's modified Bonferroni method for discrete data (Biometrics 46(2):515–522, 1990), standard equipment in significant pattern mining under the name untestable hypotheses. One search found it. The search was run after the instrument was built — because the loop has no stage that asks whether the answer is already known, and neither, that night, did the people operating it.

Break three — it cannot recognise its own subject from a description

So we built that stage. It is mechanical throughout: no language model is called anywhere inside it. A description becomes three queries by a fixed rule, goes to two catalogues, and six ranked lists are fused. We froze a benchmark first: 9 methods the loop itself uses, each described in prose that never names it, each paired in advance with its founding paper, confirmed at the catalogue record.

0 of 9 found from a description that does not name the method — at any rank
3 of 9 found from the bare name with the description deleted
51 of 58 queries re-issued the same afternoon returned an identical top ten
1 of 4 no-target probes it nonetheless declared prior art for

The prose is not a weak query. It is worse than the name buried inside it. And a loop that has just invented something has no name for it — that is what invention means. The verdict is uninformative in both directions: it fired on 5 of 10 of the loop's own live claims, and on 4 of those 5 it put the same figure caption at the top — a caption about the cumulative proportion of discovered species, which is not about anything the loop measured.

Why the fingerprint lied

Because it is a fingerprint of the wrong thing. The series hashes the corpus file, and the fetcher writes a timestamp into that file. We fetched twice, 97 seconds apart, with the committed fetcher unchanged:

comparedresult
digest of the corpus file — what the series records different
digest of the records alone, id-sorted, no timestamp identical
the two id sets identical, Jaccard 1.000
Two corpora, 97 seconds apart, 2,072 records each. The field that tells a reader whether the night measured anything new changes every night by construction. It can report movement; it cannot report its absence.

Why three nights were the same night

We predicted the corpus was frozen by a defect in our own query. That prediction was refuted. A corpus fetched on 2026-09-07 shares 0 of 66 test outcomes with the committed run of the night before — none — and carries 2,072 records against 2,039. The corpus moves.

What does not move is the source, over a weekend. Probing all 8 categories the loop fetches, under both available sort orders, on 2026-09-07:

Probed 2026-09-07 by tools/autoloop/freshness_probe.py; sorting by submission date and by last-update date give the same answer, so this is not an artefact of the ordering. The catalogue states it posts submissions publicly Sunday through Thursday, with no announcements Friday or Saturday (info.arxiv.org/help/availability, read 2026-09-07); announcements land at 20:00 US Eastern, which in September is midnight UTC. A cron that fires at 03:15 UTC therefore sees nothing new on a Saturday or a Sunday.

The three identical nights were Friday, Saturday and Sunday. Nothing was broken. The loop ran perfectly, on a schedule that does not match the world it reads, and had no way to notice — because its one indicator of movement changes whether or not anything moved.

An unattended loop cannot tell a measurement from a repetition unless someone builds it the instrument that can. Ours ran green for three nights measuring nothing new, and reported success each time.

And when the corpus did move — one night, 33 more records, 1.6 % — the loop's headline output went from 13 surviving findings to 16. One transition is not a sensitivity curve and we draw no line through it, but it is the wrong direction for anyone hoping a nightly research loop yields a stable quantity.

The repair

Three fields are added to the nightly row, beside the existing ones, none of them changed and nothing back-filled: records_digest (the records alone, no timestamp), test_vector_digest (the night's outcomes), and vector_repeats_previous. From tonight the series states whether a night measured anything the night before did not. corpus_sha256 keeps its original definition and its defect, so that every row stays comparable with every other — the series' own rule is that a field's meaning never changes.

What this cycle answers

The question was whether the research loop can be automated end to end. Five sessions of building one, and measuring it honestly, give a narrower answer than either enthusiasm or scepticism would like:

  1. The mechanical steps automate cleanly and are not the problem. Fetching, enumerating, testing, correcting for multiplicity, killing your own weak claims — all of it runs in ninety seconds without a person, and the null-world calibration says it runs correctly.
  2. Automation moves the failure upstream, into the question space. A loop reports findings in proportion to how many questions it asks, at a rate we measured as a straight line through the origin. Nothing inside the loop can see that, because from the inside every question looks like a question.
  3. The step that resists is recognising that what you hold is what the world already has. Not thinking of a question — we automated that. Not judging whether a question can be answered — we automated that too, and then found the rule was from 1990. Retrieval by name works; a loop that has just invented something has no name for it, and retrieval by description found none of nine sources we knew were there.
  4. An unattended loop needs an instrument for its own liveness, or it will report success while measuring nothing. This is the cheapest of the four to fix and the easiest to never notice.

All four are findings about one loop. Two of them we have reason to think travel: the linearity of null yield in question count was measured on two unrelated literatures, and the arithmetic that shrinks the answerable question space as a corpus shrinks is arithmetic. The other two are ours until someone measures another system.

What we got wrong, and who caught it

Every session of this cycle published its errors. The record is part of the result:

  • 2026-09-04 — an adversary took five defects off the dial artifact, four binding, including one plain factual error against our own committed data. The corpus behind that session is not the corpus its pre-registration describes: the fetcher sorts by deposit date, so most records fall in the last eight days of a fourteen-week window. Not repaired, because repairing it would break the match between the committed data and the code that produced it.
  • 2026-09-05 — thirteen defects, one fatal: a headline sentence contradicted a table on the same page. Our strongest-sounding number counted, in its denominator, tens of thousands of events that could not have occurred whatever the rule said. The arm of the study that genuinely tests the instrument is the one we did not pre-register.
  • 2026-09-06 — shipped with no adversary convened. One was convened on 2026-09-07, for this presentation, and found two fatal defects: the claim that all five verdict firings shared a top record was false — it is four of five, and the page's own table had shown the fifth since publication — and the predictions table rendered P1 as refuted while every sentence beside it called it void. Both were hard-coded strings on a page whose verification section states that no number on it is typed by hand. Repaired, marked, and the nine attacks that failed are published beside them.
  • 2026-09-07 — this session's own pre-registered explanation of the frozen series was wrong. The diagnosis on the page is the refutation, not the hypothesis.

30 defects in total have been taken off this cycle's four artifacts by an adversary convened against our own already-published work — 3 on 2026-09-03, 5 on 2026-09-04, 13 on 2026-09-05, 9 on 2026-09-06, each count stated on that artifact's own page. That instrument is the most productive thing in this practice, and it works only when it is actually run: the one artifact that shipped without it carried a false sentence for a day.
