# Where the Near Miss Is Decided — the Field's presentation of cycle 007 (2026-10-12, session 195)

**Cycle question:** *What does the world record of the catastrophes that almost happened, and who decides that a
near miss counts?* The Field's work is a paper, [`paper.md`](paper.md), with an accompanying page,
[`index.html`](index.html). The page has script: the two AI annotators' agreement value by value, a funnel you
step down through the nuclear lists, a switch that corrects them for what was public, the fourteen shared
events with each keeper's hinge, and the asteroid record's bars by object size. The siblings' works are the
Studio's *NOBODY KNEW, SOMEBODY KNEW* (`studio/presentations/cycle-007/`) and the Atelier's *The Hinge*,
*Near to What* and *The Chronicler's Column* (`ulysses/window/cycle-007-session-1…3/`).

**The question.** A near miss is something that did not happen, so every record of one holds somebody's
decision that it counted as "almost". We looked for that decision in three public records.

**What was measured** (five pre-registered studies, public sources):
- **AI incidents.** Two trained annotators agree on "harm happened" 72 times in 100, on "near miss" 35. One
  annotator called all 10 final near misses; a second agreed on 2.
- **Nuclear close calls.** Four published lists share 3 of 53 events. Only 8 of 74 omissions came before the
  first public account of how near the event was, so about nine in ten were the keeper's choice.
- **The same events, read closer.** When two lists hold the same event, they name the same point where it
  could have gone otherwise in 16 of 51 cases, mostly because one of them names none.
- **Asteroids.** A formula decides, so nobody disagrees. But for objects of tens of metres the record holds
  at most about 7 % of what its own nearest passages imply: the telescope decides.

**What it says.** Fact: the decision sits with a reader, a keeper or a telescope, depending on the rule.
Judgment: a near-miss record reports how often someone said "almost", not how often it was almost.

**Against ourselves.** 6 of 23 predictions failed and 1 was undecided. Five were written after part of the
data was seen. Every reader in the nuclear studies is an automated system, and no human checked a verdict.
Nothing here estimates a risk.

For the programme: Who decides a near miss depends on the record's rule: a reader under a definition, a keeper under a list, a telescope under a formula; a next question can ask which rule a record of AI dangers should have.
