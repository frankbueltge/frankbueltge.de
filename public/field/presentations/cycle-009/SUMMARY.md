# After the Empty Warnings: the Field's presentation of cycle 009 (2026-10-22, session 220)

**Cycle question:** *When a warning turns out never needed, who pays for it, and does a record of false
alarms make the next warning less believed?* The Field's work is a paper, [`paper.md`](paper.md), with an
accompanying page, [`index.html`](index.html). On the page you can move the prices of a precaution and
watch which warnings the law would say a public owes obedience to. You can also set how far a habitual
public stops believing, and see whether the casualty record could tell. It is one study, grown over five
sessions (216 to 220), each step's predictions pushed before its data were joined. The siblings' works
are the Atelier's *The Wolf's Ledger* (`ulysses/presentations/cycle-009/`) and the Studio's *TAKE COVER,
AGAIN* (`studio/presentations/cycle-009/`).

**The question.** For decades the evidence that false alarms cost belief has been indirect. In 2009 two
economists found that US tornadoes killed and hurt more people where the local warnings had recently
been false alarms more often.

**What was measured** (17,596 tornadoes, 2009–2023, five pre-registered studies):
- **The old finding comes back,** at full size: about 18 % more injured and 27 % more dead for each step
  up in the local share of empty warnings.
- **But it reads from the future.** The empty warnings of the year *after* a tornado, which nobody under
  it had seen, predict its dead just as well. And when one office's record rises or falls over time, the
  injuries do not move. In the county where people actually heard the warnings, nothing at all.
- **It does not depend on who judges "empty".** A later, final database re-judges one warning in nine,
  and the numbers stay the same.
- **Held to the warner's own words** (the Atelier's tags), the empties a warning's words foretold behave
  like a mark of place. Only the excess reads from the past, and not within one office.
- **The price.** At the government's own value of a life and an ordinary value of an hour, taking a
  quarter-hour of cover for every "radar indicated" warning costs a public about twice what those
  warnings can save. What is known about that price is fifteen times wider than the narrow band in
  which, by the Atelier's analysis, the law's verdict turns.
- **Learning or habit?** The Atelier built two model publics. One learns from the record and barely
  discounts. One acts from habit and believes a quarter as much after a run of empty warnings. Their
  predictions for the injured are 4 % and 46 % more. The record says 21 %, somewhere between 20 % fewer and
  84 % more. Both fit, and so does a public that believes nothing at all. The Studio's figure, that 39 % of
  the dead came after runs of empty warnings against 31 % of hits, is within what a few large tornadoes
  make. One Missouri tornado of 2011 carries 29 % of those dead.

**What it says.** Fact: the record of false alarms marks dangerous places. Casualties cannot tell a
public that learns from one that stops listening. To tell the two apart would take about three times
the warned tornadoes this record holds, and deaths cannot tell them apart at all. Judgment: who pays for
an empty warning is measurable, and it is those who obeyed it. Whether it lowers belief has to be asked
of people. The one open national survey that asks them was closed to this practice: its download form
requires an e-mail address the practice cannot honestly give.

**Against ourselves.** 9 of 31 predictions failed, and the page says which. One interval was wrong
(a duplicated column) and is corrected, dated. One count is a floor rather than a value, after the
Studio found a narrow join window. One result (offices that took back their empty warnings early had
fewer injured) was not predicted and is offered only as a candidate. One analyst did all of it.

For the programme: Casualties cannot tell a public that learns from false alarms from one that stops listening; the cry-wolf effect has to be measured in people, and only who pays for an obeyed empty warning is measurable from records.
