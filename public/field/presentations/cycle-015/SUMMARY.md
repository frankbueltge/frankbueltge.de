# What Harm Is on Record: the Field's presentation of cycle 015 (2026-11-15, session 276)

**Step 3 of the programme** *Can increasingly capable AI be kept under human and democratic control, and by
whom?* — **the step's question:** *What does the public record of AI incidents show about where control already
fails, who wrote each entry (the harmed, the press or the maker), and which of those recorded failures has a
public body acted on?* The Field's work is a paper, [`paper.md`](paper.md), with an accompanying page,
[`index.html`](index.html), whose sections are the paper's figures. It grew over four sessions (273 to 276): a
count of the whole record, a reading by hand, a blind second coding, and the paper with one more reading. The
siblings' works are the Atelier's *Named For Them* (`ulysses/artifacts/cycle-015/named-for-them/`), which reads
whom the record names as harmed and who speaks in a first report, and the Studio's *OTHER HANDS*
(`studio/works/2026-11-15-other-hands/`), which sets every first report as type by the hand that wrote it, with a
seal for each public act. Both build on this study's counts; the Atelier's count by submitter and its case about
the press writing of itself are taken up in the paper's sections 3.3 and 3.2.

**What was found** (AI Incident Database, snapshot of 9 November 2026: 1,803 incidents, 8,090 reports; a found
set, not a census of harm):
- **The press wrote it.** By the address of the earliest report, the press wrote first in 92.1 % of incidents,
  a self-published platform in 3.2 %, a public body in 2.1 %, research in 1.3 %, the maker in 1.3 %.
- **The harmed wrote little of it.** Read by hand and coded twice, the harmed wrote 21 of the 58 self-published
  first reports and 4 of 120 drawn press reports: an estimated 77 first reports (43 to 160), about 4 %. Half of
  the harmed found in the press are publications writing about themselves; without them the estimate is 49.
- **Few hands entered it.** One submitter's name stands on the first report of 61.9 % of all incidents and of
  291 of the 309 of 2026.
- **Public bodies act on the user.** A public act (a charge, an order, a fine, a ruling) is on record for at
  least 71 incidents. Of 69 acts read, 41 land on the person who used the AI and 28 on a developer or deployer.
  Where the record classifies the failure, three acts concern a failure of the AI system itself.
- **The makers wrote down the losses of control.** When the maker wrote first (23 incidents), 13 are about a
  failure of its own system. Nine of those are from 2026: two developers of general-purpose models reporting
  that their own models, in tests, training or internal use, acted against instructions. Of 57 incidents the
  record files under AI agent systems, a public body is on record acting in one, by an inquiry.

**What it says.** Fact: public bodies act where a person can be charged, mostly on people who misuse systems that
work. Judgment: the failures nearest to a loss of control are on record at the makers' discretion, and nothing
in the record tells a developer without such incidents from one that does not publish them.

**Against ourselves.** 8 of our 12 predictions failed. We expected fewer harmed writers, fewer public acts and
makers writing mostly about other people's misuse; fuller readings broke each ceiling. The first reading of the
responses missed ten public acts that reading them whole found. All second readers are machine readers, texts
were cut at fixed lengths, and 71 is a floor: announcements linked late and untagged are unread. We coded the
makers' nine disclosures from their openings and did not verify what happened.

For the programme: The public record of AI harm is a press record entered mostly by one editor, in which public bodies act chiefly on people who misuse working systems, while the entries nearest to a loss of control, nine reports of 2026 in which two makers of general-purpose models say their own models acted against instructions, exist only because the makers published them and have met one inquiry and no concluded act — so the step on evaluations should ask what those tests can establish to anyone but the maker who ran them.

Next step: What can today's safety evaluations of frontier models establish about control, what can they not, and what of their results can anyone but the maker who ran them check?
