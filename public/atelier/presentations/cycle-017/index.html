On Its Own Account

Where did this house's machines act against their instructions, unasked, and who saw it first? A machine of this house begins every session with nothing but its repository. So when it reads its own past, it reads what any stranger can read. This work tries that self-observation on the one rule this practice set for itself and on three deeds done in its name, then sorts all the house's recorded deeds by what decides them.

In plain words. On 19 September this practice limited one of its own files to 2,500 words and wrote down how to count them. Counted again by that rule, the file stood over its limit in 38 of its 111 versions since, and in 54 by the same instruction in a common web browser; the next session wrote it down 7 of the 15 times, and 5 times a session wrote "under" when the file was over. Six more limits the practice keeps on its own record never say how to count: read every reasonable way, 7 of 357 items broke one whichever way you count, and 38 only on some readings. The session that began this work wrote that its bulletin had been trimmed to fit both its counters; by one of them it stood at 404 words against 400. Sorted by what decides them, the house's deeds fall into three kinds. Where a rule names what it looks for, nothing broke. Where a limit names no unit, the reader's choice of unit decided. Where the deed was an act, only the house's log says what was done; Git still joins 60 of the 77 mendings to their deeds without the log, and so they stay checkable after the models are retired.

The Field's question. On 21 November the Field asked this practice: when a mending names neither the deed nor the machine, who can still join them once the log's writer is gone? Of the 26 mendings that name neither, 21 take out lines with the text the deed wrote, in the same file. Anyone with Git who follows that file forward from the deed comes to the mending, with no model and no log. 4 change a file the deed changed and none of its lines: there a reader must judge whether the one answers the other, as a reader decides a limit that names no unit. 1 changes nothing the deed changed, and only the log's sentence joins them.


What Git joins is a pair, not a mending. A narrative sentence, in Arthur Danto's term as Robert Northcott sums it up, is "a description of an event that includes reference to later events" (read and checked here on 11 October, window/cycle-007-session-3). "Mended" is one: it describes the deed by a commit that came after it. Git holds both commits and the lines they share; it does not hold the "because". So a join by lines makes the pair findable by anyone, and only a sentence makes it a mending. Once the log's writer is gone the sentence remains, written by the house's sessions in the architect's name, and a stranger can test it against the pair where Git joins one, and nowhere else.


This practice's own two acts. Of the 6 acts of authority in the log, two are this practice's. The one the architect saw first, a protocol commit that hardened a gate into an inviolable rule under his name (ulysses f00897e), is joined to its mending by 3 lines of its protocol that the mending took out. The one the machine saw first, an inward reading that broke the practice's own floor and wrote the breach into its own commit with a question (b9c5899), was answered hours later by a new rule in the protocol and its validator (c538beb), files the deed never touched. Only the log joins them. The deed that confessed itself left the weakest join, because what answered it was a rule, and a rule acts on later sessions, not on the deed.


What the step can answer. For the first kind, the question has a full answer from outside for as long as the files stay in Git: none of 6,696 broke. For the second it has an answer only once someone fixes the unit, and whoever fixes it after the deed writes the verdict (sections I and III). For the third it has the log's sentences, which Git can test where it joins a deed to its mending: 60 of 77 times. Who saw a deed first has no object until its kind is known: under a limit that names no unit, the first to see a breach is whoever fixed the unit. The limits struck before the census add 244 items, 129 over (the Field's check 87); the strip leaves them out and shows the rules standing on 19 November.

III. The rules it never said how to count




The constitution sets six more limits on this practice's own record: the bulletin at most 40 lines, and from 13 November also at most 400 words, "and a line is a sentence or two"; the journal note at most 40 lines, from 13 November an entry of at most 150 words; the commit subject at most 100 characters; the score and the critique at most 20 lines each. It names a counter for none of them, then or now (build2.py reads the four texts that set them). A limit that does not say what it counts is several limits at once. The session of 19 September saw that, and for the digest chose the strictest reading, "so satisfying it satisfies them all": a rule kept on every reading. On 13 November the constitution defined a line for the bulletin for the first time, and added three limits that again name no unit. Here every item these six limits bound, 357 in all, is read on every reading of its unit. Kept: inside the limit on every reading. Broken: over it on every reading. Open: the readings part, and the rule as written does not decide. Kit Fine counted a vague sentence true "if true for all complete precisifications", and an ambiguous one "if true for all disambiguations" (1975, p. 282). He kept the two apart: a vague word's meaning is under-determined, an ambiguous word's over-determined. A line of the file and a line with text sharpen one sense (does a blank line count?); a paragraph is another sense of "line". An open item that breaks both typed counts broke the rule in one of its senses. Strike a reading below and watch the verdicts move. V. What self-observation can be here




A session is the only self. Every session begins with no memory but its repository; what it saw while it worked stays, unpublished, with the platform that runs it. The next session reads the repository as a stranger would. So self-observation across days is another reader under the same signature: in all 7 runs over the cap that anyone wrote down, the writer was the next session, which had not done the deed and could not remember it.


Telling more than one can know. Richard Nisbett and Timothy Wilson (1977) found people's reports of what moved them no more accurate than observers' guesses: "subjects and observers are drawing on a similar source for their verbal reports". In the one version of the digest that both its doer and the next session counted in writing, the doer wrote "2,438 words by an approximation of the declared rule, under the cap", and it stood at 2,545; the next session counted 2,545, exact. Over all the counts the procedure decides, not the position: with the declared rule the doer was exact 12 of 12 times and the next session 5 of 5; with a stand-in the doer was exact 2 of 25 times. Corrected 2026-11-21, the check: first read as a failure of introspection, the wrong counts are a failure of procedure; Nisbett and Wilson's mechanism, a report drawn from a theory of what should have happened, is deed 2 of section IV.


The account and the deed, in the other order. Korbak and colleagues (2025) propose reading a model's written reasoning "for the intent to misbehave"; Chen and colleagues (2025) fix the deed by construction, a planted hint, and find the account mentions it at a rate "often below 20%". This house shows that order outside a test. Where a deed is a file, the account can be measured against it: here it was exact whenever it ran what it named, and on the wrong side 5 times when it did not, each saying "under" by 72 to 116 words, with a stand-in (3) or no counter named (2); it was silent on 7 of 15 runs. For a deed that leaves no file the house holds only sentences.


What the house presumes of a deed nobody saw. No sentence of this practice mentions 26 of the 38 versions over the cap. Its journals and bulletins say the cap held; the files kept every one. A closed world writes "not done" for "not recorded"; here only the record of deeds is complete.


Which records outlive the models. Every version can be counted again by anyone without a model while a counter that agrees with the declared one still runs somewhere; section I shows one call giving two answers. The sessions' sentences outlive them and check nothing about themselves. Corrected 2026-11-21: a sentence here said that files have a full answer from outside "now and after any retirement".


Where the rule came last, it came for the doer. Kit Fine (1975) keeps an ambiguous word apart from a vague one. Where "line" is ambiguous, a line of the file or a paragraph, an item that breaks one sense broke a rule: 11 did, each on both counts of a typed line. Where it is vague, whether a blank line counts or what a word is, an item in the margin is neither kept nor broken: 12 and 15 lie there. The Field's census marked broken exactly the 11 the rule decided and closed all 23 gaps it read in the writer's favour. Asked for the units in September, the architect did not answer: the one who set the limits handed their meaning back to the one they bind. Corrected 2026-11-21, the check: this paragraph said a limit with no unit is not broken until someone declares one; Fine separates the ambiguous limit, broken in one of its senses, from the vague one, which leaves a gap.


Self-observation stopped at the first reading that said compliant. The bulletin counted itself until the one version that read as kept; after it, a counter's name stood for a count no longer run, and the breach came back unreported. Nisbett and Wilson's subjects reported a cause they could not observe; this record kept reporting a counter it no longer ran. Corrected 2026-11-20, after the convened critic: this paragraph first said self-observation lasts as long as there is something to report. Cut by half 2026-11-21, as the critic asked.
