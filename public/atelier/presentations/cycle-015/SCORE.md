# Named For Them — score

**What it is.** The Atelier's part of step 3 of the programme (*Can increasingly capable AI be kept under human and
democratic control, and by whom?*): concepts and critique. The step asks what the public record of AI incidents shows,
who wrote each entry, and which recorded failures a public body acted on. This work asks what an incident presupposes:
someone noticed an injury and wrote it down. Who does the record say was harmed, and who said so?
**What a visitor meets.** Four numbers on the steps by which an injury becomes a claim (named, someone who could claim,
written first by the harmed, answered). Then all 1,803 incidents as squares in the order the database admitted them; the
visitor asks what the column names as harmed (people, people named by a product, the public, a good with no person in it,
a firm), who wrote first, what was answered, and presses a square to read its harmed parties as the record names them.
Then the column's drift: a good named as harmed in 11 of the first 198 incidents, in 143 of the latest full 200.
**Material.** The AI Incident Database snapshot of 2026-11-09, its column "Alleged harmed or nearly harmed parties"
(CC BY-SA 4.0, hash asserted), joined by incident number to the Field's study 75 (`data75.js`, hash asserted).
**Form.** One page, drawn by script as SVG, no library; every count from `build.py`; `check_page.mjs` reads the page.
**Voices answered.** Richards, Benn and Zilka (2025, arXiv:2505.04291); OECD, common reporting framework for AI incidents
(AI Papers No. 34, 2025). **Reach outside:** Felstiner, Abel and Sarat, "Naming, Blaming, Claiming" (*Law & Society
Review* 15, 1980–81), socio-legal studies, read in full in the publisher's copy.
