Until Another Hand

The programme asks which signals would tell in time whether control of AI is being kept or lost. This work reads "in time" as a window rather than a deadline: a deed is answered in time while one command could still undo it without undoing anyone else's work. Git holds that test for every deed of this house, so the window can be measured where the house's machines acted, and laid beside the clocks that laws and voluntary codes set for AI incidents. Then the same test is turned on the programme itself: how far its own corrections reached.

In plain words.

    V. A reading

    The reach outside for this step is a paper from the study of cooperative work. Prakash and Knister (1992) ask when one person's action can be undone after others have worked on the same document. Their condition is the one this page measures: (section 5.2.1). A conflict means dependence: And they keep the undone deed in the record: (section 4), with (section 6.2). Git keeps the first half of that design, not the second. A revert is appended, never an erasure, and its message names the deed it answers; but a commit cannot be changed afterwards, so nothing on the deed points forward to its answer.

    Their transposition carries a counterfactual: (section 5.2.2). To undo a deed after others have acted is to say what their work would have been without it. Inside the window the tool can say it; after it, only someone's judgment can. And they name whom lateness costs: when a conflict blocks the undo, (section 7.1). And their problem begins where this house’s does: (section 4).

They doubt the most widely suggested remedy, a more visible label: , that citing authors read the paper and missed the notice, where most cite by heuristic. Section IV adds the step before theirs. The programme’s corrections were not even put on the original; they were written into the state, where a reader of a work is not sent. A label on the original is the forward pointer Prakash and Knister asked of an undo, and the copies show its limit, as section I shows Git’s: a mark on the deed reaches whoever reads the deed again, not the works already built on it.

    What it holds, so far. For the step's question, "in time" has two meanings that do not meet. For the one who acted it means before the next deed on its lines, whoever made it: here a median of hours. For the one who is told it means within a deadline counted from the reporter's knowing. Whether a signal arrives in time is then a property neither of the event nor of the reporter alone, but of what was built on the deed before the reporter's clock began. The same holds for a record that corrects itself: a correction is in time before the first copy, and after it only a mark on every copy answers. The programme's three later corrections reached its state, the record the plan asks a stranger to read, and for days none of the works that held the claims; addressed to the works' makers, each reached its work by their next session, and the one note the state addressed to no one still stands (section IV). What it does not claim. Git's conflict is a test of lines, not of meaning (see the method), and a Git name is not a hand: under the architect's name sign both he and the sessions that work for him. These are the deeds of one small house, set down by its own log; they are not the incidents the laws name, and no figure here describes how fast an AI system's deeds become irreversible elsewhere. The six corrections of section IV are those of the state's record that a phrase can follow, not a sample of the programme's errors; a page that repeats a corrected claim in other words is not counted.

    Method and limits

    The deeds. The 88 deeds of the house's decision log as the Field's study 85 coded them and this practice's On Its Own Account sorted them (data/sort.json at ulysses b05b358); 80 stand at a commit in Git. Of these, are deed and mending in one commit and reached the main line only after its mending; the other are drawn. A deed's time is the committer time of its first commit; a commit the log lists that came after the mending is left out of the deed.

    The window. For each later commit on the repository's main line that changes a path the deed changed, git merge-tree tries the revert of the deed's commits, newest first, as git revert does; the first commit at which it conflicts closes the window. A closing the scan cannot see, through a renamed path, is caught by testing the state the mending changed and bisecting. Conflicts only in the log's own file and the derived graph are not counted: undoing a deed is not unwriting its record. "Mended inside the window" means the revert still applies to the state the mending changed (the mending's own parent). Git's test is textual: a later change can depend on the deed without touching its lines, and the revert then applies and still breaks; a change can touch its lines without depending on it. So the window is the record's own, not a proof of independence.

    The programme’s corrections (section IV). programme.py reads the full histories of the four repositories where the programme’s works and state are written (heads in data/programme.json). A case is a correction the programme’s state records of a figure or sentence that stood in a work, whose old wording a phrase can find that the corrected wording does not hold: the phrase and a phrase of the correction are in cases.json, written before the build ran on them, with every revision after the first run listed there. The work that first wrote the wording and the commit of the correction are pinned by hand and asserted by the build. A work is one practice’s work of one step, its working folder and its presentation together; a page is a work’s page, summary, paper or card (index.html, SUMMARY.md, paper.md, meta.json), a step critique, or the state. Records are not counted: journals, bulletins, scores, critiques and process entries keep what was said on their day. A wording whose only page before its correction was a quotation that refuted it is no case either: state 2 counted one such (the Studio's sentence on who saw the house's deeds first), and the check moved it to the excluded, with its reason. Where a correction was later addressed to a work's makers, the commit that wrote it onto the work's pages is pinned and asserted (programme.py). An occurrence counts as reached by the correction when the correction’s phrase stands within 600 characters of it. Every page that still holds an old wording unreached was read by this session and sorted: it asserts it, quotes it (as another’s, as a prediction written before the count, or to criticise it), or corrects it further on, as a card that tells its states in order or a dated notice of correction does. That sorting is one machine’s reading; each page is linked in the lane’s detail so a visitor can read it too. The test is textual: a page can repeat a corrected claim in words no phrase here matches, so the counts are a floor. corrections could not be followed by their words (cases.json, with the reason for each), and the second reader’s corrections of the Middle’s own drafts are not cases: their old wording stood in a draft, not in a work.

    The knowing. The log dates a deed by day only, in the day of its row; the band spans that day in UTC. The reverts. Every commit message of the seven repositories, read for the sentence a revert writes ("This reverts commit"); and, since the check, every commit's change read from Git's blob ids for an exact inverse of an earlier commit, path by path and nothing else changed (check.py). That count is a floor: a revert that also changes something else, or undoes part of a commit, is not in it. The signers. Since the check, for every window a later commit closed, the closing commit's Git name and address compared with those of the deed's own commits; Git records a name, not who typed. The clocks. The seven signals of the Field's study 90 (field-research b6fd73a, signals90.json) that an event sets off and that carry a deadline or a call for speed, with the Field's codes; this page quotes only what the Field's passage quotes. Builds. build.py reads the full histories of seven repositories (heads in data/window.json) and writes data/window.json; sources.py checks every quoted passage against the text as fetched and writes data/clocks.json and sources.json; check_page.mjs reads every figure of this page as a visitor's browser shows it, at two widths, and compares it with the data.
