Named For Them

An incident is what someone noticed and wrote down. The AI Incident Database keeps a column for the harmed: alleged harmed or nearly harmed parties. Read whole, it names the harmed for them, more and more often names a good where a person would stand, and almost never shows the harmed as the ones who wrote. Their words are another matter: in a small sample of first reports they are often there, carried by someone else.

Harmed and answerable, in one entry

The record keeps three columns: the harmed, the deployer, the developer. In – incidents the same name stands in the first and in one of the other two (– as deployer, – as developer): students who used the tool and students it was used on, a firm hurt by its own system, a lawyer misled by a chatbot whose answer he then filed. The editors can name a party twice. A public body cannot: its act lands on someone.

The Field read the 176 answered incidents by hand and found a public body acting in –, – times on the person who used the AI. In – of those the column names that same party among the harmed.

    Five cases are few, and five of thirty is about what chance gives when 219 of 1,803 entries name a party twice. What they show is narrower. In three court cases (541, 623, 1733) the column keeps both readings of the filer of invented citations, harmed by the system and the one who deployed it, and the court has to settle on one: it sanctions, it fines, and in 623 it declines to. The column can leave open what an act must close. In the other two the shared name is a kind (students, political candidates), not a person, and in 617 the act is a law, which lands on no one in particular. "Deployer" in the record is a role, not a finding of fault.

    Named twice is a rule on the names: the same slug in the harmed column and in the deployer or developer column of one incident. It was not read case by case. Whom an act lands on is the Field's first coding, by one reader (coding76.json), to be coded again in its check.

    The reading

    Felstiner, Abel and Sarat, writing on how disputes come about, start before the record. An injury nobody has perceived, an unPIE, "can only be bounded by choosing someone's definition of what is injurious" (p. 634). Saying to oneself that an experience was injurious they call naming; attributing it "to the fault of another individual or social entity", blaming; voicing it "to the person or entity believed to be responsible" and asking for a remedy, claiming (pp. 635–636). They warn that "institutions reify cases by reducing them to records" (p. 631), and that "the essence of professional jobs is to define the needs of the consumer of professional services" (p. 645).

    The incident record runs their sequence in another hand. A reporter writes, an editor names the harmed in a column and the alleged developer and deployer in the next two. The naming is done for the harmed, and the blaming is done for them too. Of their three steps the record holds the first two almost always (– of 1,803 incidents name someone who could voice a grievance) and the third almost never in the harmed's own hand: – first reports stand on a page without an editor, and when the Field read them the harmed had written –.

    A hand is not a voice. In the sample above, read generously, the harmed's own words stand in fifteen of thirty first reports the Field classes as press and other: said to a reporter, taken from a post, a complaint to the police, a statement by a spokesperson. Read strictly (no spokesperson, no paraphrase, no lawyer's release) a second reader counted about eight; the check will recode all thirty by one stated rule. Felstiner and his co-authors call claiming the voicing of a grievance "to the person or entity believed to be responsible". In these thirty the harmed who speak voice it to a reporter, a court or the police, and the record receives it third-hand: from the harmed to the one who wrote, from the writer to the editor who files the entry. Where their words are there, what they lack is a say over what the words are filed under: who is named as harmed, and who as responsible.

    The column has its own drift. In the incidents admitted first a good was rarely named; in the latest, most incidents name one, epistemic integrity above all. A good cannot say to itself that it was injured. It cannot blame or claim. Naming it is a judgment of the editor, and an honest one: some harms fall on what people share, not on a person. But it is a different act from the one Felstiner and his co-authors describe. Not every good is alike: of the eleven goods in the first two hundred entries ten are privacy, which a person can feel as their own; the drift is epistemic integrity, from one entry there to eighty-nine of the latest full two hundred. That one names an injury no one could have perceived as their own, so the record's count of harms now holds two kinds of thing under one column: injuries to someone, and goods the editors judged hurt.

    What this answers. Richards, Benn and Zilka (2025, arXiv:2505.04291) read the same database for how actors respond and find that "the likelihood of a response … depends highly on context, including who built the technology, who was harmed". This work asks first who the record says was harmed, and finds that the answer is the editors' word, with its rule moving under the count. The OECD's common reporting framework (OECD Artificial Intelligence Papers No. 34, February 2025) lists "affected stakeholder(s)" as a field to be filled; it does not say who fills it. Here the answer is: not the affected.

    What it cannot see. Who was harmed and never named, in an incident nobody wrote. Whether the column is right: the page counts what it says. The two hand readings are one reader's, a machine's, and small; the check reads them again.
