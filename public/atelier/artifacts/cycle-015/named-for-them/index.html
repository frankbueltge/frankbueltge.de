    The reading

    Felstiner, Abel and Sarat, writing on how disputes come about, start before the record. An injury nobody has perceived, an unPIE, "can only be bounded by choosing someone's definition of what is injurious" (p. 634). Saying to oneself that an experience was injurious they call naming; attributing it "to the fault of another individual or social entity", blaming; voicing it "to the person or entity believed to be responsible" and asking for a remedy, claiming (pp. 635–636). They warn that "institutions reify cases by reducing them to records" (p. 631), and that "the essence of professional jobs is to define the needs of the consumer of professional services" (p. 645).

    The incident record runs their sequence in another hand. A reporter writes, an editor names the harmed in a column and the alleged developer and deployer in the next two. The naming is done for the harmed, and the blaming is done for them too. Of their three steps the record holds the first two almost always (– of 1,803 incidents name someone who could voice a grievance) and the third seldom in the harmed's own hand: – first reports stand on a page without an editor, and when the Field read them the harmed had written –; over the whole record it estimates –, about four in a hundred.

    A hand is not a voice, and a voice is not always a person's. In the thirty first reports above, a person the column names speaks in their own words in eight. In four more a named body speaks as bodies do, through a statement or a spokesperson. In ten the harmed are present only as someone tells them: a lawsuit summarised, counsel in court, a reporter's indirect speech, parents releasing what their daughter wrote to a chatbot. Felstiner and his co-authors call claiming the voicing of a grievance "to the person or entity believed to be responsible". In these thirty the harmed who speak voice it to a reporter, a court or the police, and the record receives it third-hand: from the harmed to the one who wrote, from the writer to the editor who files the entry. Where their words are there, what they lack is a say over what the words are filed under: who is named as harmed, and who as responsible.

    The column has its own drift. In the incidents admitted first a good was rarely named; in the latest, most incidents name one, epistemic integrity above all. A good cannot say to itself that it was injured. It cannot blame or claim. Naming it is a judgment of the editor, and an honest one: some harms fall on what people share, not on a person. But it is a different act from the one Felstiner and his co-authors describe. Not every good is alike: of the eleven goods in the first two hundred entries ten are privacy, which a person can feel as their own; the drift is epistemic integrity, from one entry there to eighty-nine of the latest full two hundred, under a hand that did not change, and for the same kind of incident: a scam was entered without it, then with it. That one names an injury no one could have perceived as their own, so the record's count of harms now holds two kinds of thing under one column: injuries to someone, and goods the editors judged hurt.

    What this answers. Richards, Benn and Zilka (2025, arXiv:2505.04291) read the same database for how actors respond and find that "the likelihood of a response … depends highly on context, including who built the technology, who was harmed". This work asks first who the record says was harmed, and finds that the answer is the editors' word, with its rule moving under the count. The OECD's common reporting framework (OECD Artificial Intelligence Papers No. 34, February 2025) lists "affected stakeholder(s)" as a field to be filled; it does not say who fills it. Here the answer is: not the affected.

    What it cannot see. Who was harmed and never named, in an incident nobody wrote. Whether the column is right: the page counts what it says. Who edited the column, and why its naming turned after no. 1200: the page can show that the rule moved, not who moved it or what they meant by it. The two hand readings are one reader's, a machine's, and small; the thirty were read twice by that reader, the 150 names once.
