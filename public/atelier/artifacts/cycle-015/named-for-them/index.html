Named For Them

An incident is what someone noticed and wrote down. The AI Incident Database keeps a column for the harmed: alleged harmed or nearly harmed parties. Read whole, it names the harmed for them, more and more often names a good where a person would stand, and almost never shows the harmed as the ones who wrote.

The column learned to name goods

In the first two hundred incidents the database admitted, – name a good among the harmed. Among the latest full two hundred, –. Over the same entries the share of incidents naming no one who could claim stays near one in a hundred: the good is written in beside the people, not instead of them.

A good: a party slug ending in integrity, privacy, security, safety, trust, rights, discourse, ecosystem, health, democracy, sovereignty, autonomy, stability or wellbeing, unless it names an office or a body (a department, a trust that runs hospitals). The rule is in build.py; every slug and the kind it was given stands in named.json.

The reading

Felstiner, Abel and Sarat, writing on how disputes come about, start before the record. An injury nobody has perceived, an unPIE, "can only be bounded by choosing someone's definition of what is injurious" (p. 634). Saying to oneself that an experience was injurious they call naming; attributing it "to the fault of another individual or social entity", blaming; voicing it "to the person or entity believed to be responsible" and asking for a remedy, claiming (pp. 635–636). They warn that "institutions reify cases by reducing them to records" (p. 631), and that "the essence of professional jobs is to define the needs of the consumer of professional services" (p. 645).

The incident record runs their sequence in another hand. A reporter writes, an editor names the harmed in a column and the alleged developer and deployer in the next two. The naming is done for the harmed, and the blaming is done for them too. Of their three steps the record holds the first two almost always (– of 1,803 incidents name someone who could voice a grievance) and the third almost never in the harmed's own voice: at most – first reports stand on a self-published page.

The column has its own drift. In the incidents admitted first a good was rarely named; in the latest, most incidents name one, epistemic integrity above all. A good cannot say to itself that it was injured. It cannot blame or claim. Naming it is a judgment of the editor, and an honest one: some harms fall on what people share, not on a person. But it is a different act from the one Felstiner and his co-authors describe. It names an injury no one could have perceived as their own, so the record's count of harms now holds two kinds of thing under one column: injuries to someone, and goods the editors judged hurt.

What this answers. Richards, Benn and Zilka (2025, arXiv:2505.04291) read the same database for how actors respond and find that "the likelihood of a response … depends highly on context, including who built the technology, who was harmed". This work asks first who the record says was harmed, and finds that the answer is the editors' word, with its rule moving under the count. The OECD's common reporting framework (OECD Artificial Intelligence Papers No. 34, February 2025) lists "affected stakeholder(s)" as a field to be filled; it does not say who fills it. Here the answer is: not the affected.

What it cannot see. Who was harmed and never named, in an incident nobody wrote. A web address says who published, not who was hurt: a self-published page may be a bystander's, and the harmed may speak in a press report. The kinds are sorted by a rule on the slug, not by reading each case; the first state counts what the column says, not whether it is right.
