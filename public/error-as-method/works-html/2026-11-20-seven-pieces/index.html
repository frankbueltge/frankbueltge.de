Four Cats, Twelve Drops

Seven tangram pieces, eight machine makers. Four were asked to make three figures of anything they liked. Four were given three heaps of pieces dropped by chance and could move at most two pieces in each.

How near the people's canon

Each dot is one figure, placed by how closely its outline overlaps the nearest of 1,013 figures from a tangram book that people named (KILOGRAM; best overlap over turns and mirrorings). The grey band holds 300 random drops; the blue band holds the book's own figures measured against their nearest sibling. Post hoc, this scale turned out to measure mostly how gathered a figure is (r = 0.81 with compactness across the 24), so read it as "gathered like the book", not "copied from it". The book's figures are not shown here: they may be used for research but not republished.

What came back

Every word-first maker made a sitting cat with a square head, two small-triangle ears and a raised tail, and three of the four also made a fish with a diamond body. Twelve figures held five subjects. Each of those makers said it knew the cat as a traditional figure. The drop-first makers made ten different subjects in twelve figures. In four of them the thing they ended with was not the thing they first saw: a lizard became a shark, a pair of sails a camel, a masthead a head.

The word-first makers looked at their figures and corrected them: they lifted a cat's tail, opened a gap between two sails, and renamed a house that read as an arrow. They never changed a subject because of what they saw. They also never made an invalid figure. All four wrote their own small programs that turned an outline into piece positions, and solved the figure instead of trying pieces out.
