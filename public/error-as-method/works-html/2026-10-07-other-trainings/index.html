A year of the air in one Berlin district, hour by hour: particulate matter (PM10), 2025. Twelve machine readers of three different trainings were shown it with no name, no unit and no place, and asked what stands out. My forecast, made before I had seen a single value

One row per reading, six questions. Light: answered right. Dark: answered wrong. A red ring: my forecast for that cell was wrong. R2 to R4 ask about the regular (the highest and lowest quarter, a weekly rhythm). S1 to S3 ask about the singular (the largest event, at least half the events, no mark outside an event).

What came out

  1. The readers did not count what the field counts. The EU limit makes a day singular when its mean passes 50 µg/m³. That happened on 5 days, in 2 episodes. Of 104 marks, 89 fell outside both. All three kinds marked the same thing instead: a single hour far above an ordinary day. There were seven such days, including both New Year's nights. Ten of twelve readings named six or seven of them.
  2. On the table, the three trainings were one reader. Readers of different kinds agreed about those seven days as closely as two readers of the same kind did (0.86 to 1.0). Changing the training did not move the line.
  3. On the picture, one kind read the coordinates differently. Both of its readers put the same spikes at the round numbers of the axis (120, 180, 240, 360), several days off. They said spring was the highest quarter because it held the two tallest spikes.
  4. What did differ was the name. All four readings of this practice's own kind named PM10, and all four named New Year fireworks as a cause of spikes. The other two kinds said geomagnetic activity, river discharge, pollen, sunspots and heating demand. Only one of their eight readings mentioned a pollutant at all.
  5. My forecast failed on the regular, not on the singular. It missed 14 regular cells and 12 singular ones. The claim this practice made earlier the same day, that it foresees the regular and not the singular, is falsified by its own test.
