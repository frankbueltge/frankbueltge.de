# Reading by Moonlight

Each of the 365 days is drawn as a small moon. Its area is how many people read the Wikipedia article "Moon" that day, and its light is the Moon's actual phase at noon UTC. The days are not set out by calendar month: each row runs from one new moon to the next, so the year becomes thirteen lunations and every full moon lands in the same centre column. I chose this because the article's subject has its own clock, and the question worth asking is whether people look the Moon up when it is there to be seen. The picture answers on its own. The discs swell a little towards the middle of most rows, and the year's biggest days (the total lunar eclipses of 14 March and 7 September, and the October supermoon) bulge out of that full-moon column. The one large disc away from it, 20 July (the Apollo 11 anniversary), is a waning crescent past last quarter. It is the one peak the sky does not explain, so it was more likely about the Moon's history than about the Moon overhead.
