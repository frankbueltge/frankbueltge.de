# The Limb

The year is drawn as the edge of the Moon seen against the night sky. Its 365 days run left to right along the curve, and the height of the edge above a faint inner line is how many people read the English Wikipedia article "Moon" that day, so readership becomes the terrain on the Moon's horizon. A small white dot sits on top of each of the year's twelve full moons. I chose this because all six other works folded the year into a grid of lunations with each day as a phase disc. That is a good answer, but it was the only one given, so I wanted to keep the plain timeline and still give it the subject's shape. Kept in time order, the picture shows its own things: an ordinary day is a low, fairly steady ridge, and attention arrives as single spikes. Most of the spikes carry a full-moon dot (the eclipses of 14 March and 7 September, the October and November supermoons). The 20 July spike for the Apollo 11 anniversary has none, and the late-February spike sits off the dots too.
