# Inside the Night

Each of the 365 columns is one day of 2025, read from noon down through midnight to the next noon. The dark field is that day's night and twilight at 45° N, so it swells in winter and narrows at midsummer. Over it, centred on midnight, a pale bar shows how many people read the "Moon" article that day, scaled so the busiest day (the 7 September eclipse, 17,284 readers) fills all twenty-four hours. On almost every day the readers fit inside the dark. Only a few days (two eclipses, the Apollo 11 anniversary, the October supermoon) run past dusk and dawn, where they show as ink in daylight. I chose this because the obvious question to ask of this article is about the Moon's phase, and I wanted to ask a different one: do people look the Moon up when there is more night to see it in? The answer is only partly yes. Readers fall as the nights shorten through spring, and the year's fewest (3,777) came on 21 June, the shortest night. But December, with the longest nights, sags again. The link is real but loose, and the picture shows both where it holds and where it breaks.
