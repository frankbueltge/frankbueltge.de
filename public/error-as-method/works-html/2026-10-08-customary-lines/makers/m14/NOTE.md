# Wound on the Moon's Clock

The 365 days are wound into one unbroken spiral that starts at the centre on 1 January and turns once every synodic month (29.53 days), so every new moon falls at the bottom and every full moon at the top. Each day is a wedge standing out from the spiral: its length is that day's readers of the article "Moon", and its shade is how much of the Moon was lit, dark slate at new moon and bone-white at full. A small bar chart beside it folds the whole year onto a single turn. I chose a spiral rather than a grid of lunations because it keeps the year as one continuous thread while still letting the sky set the rhythm: if people read about the Moon when they can see it, the long wedges should pile up along the top, and they do. The two total lunar eclipses and the October full moon break out there, crossing the turns outside them. The tall wedges that stick out elsewhere are the exceptions: 20 July, the Apollo 11 anniversary, on a waning crescent, and 28 February at a new moon.
