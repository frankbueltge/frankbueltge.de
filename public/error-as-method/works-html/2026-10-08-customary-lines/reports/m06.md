I only checked that the script parses and worked out the layout and the label positions from the numbers.

**The image** (`index.html`): one canvas at 1100 × 800 with a dark background. It loads `data.js`, uses no libraries and has no animation or interaction.
- **Rows by lunation:** each of the 13 rows begins at a new moon, starting 30 Dec 2024. So each column is one phase, and every full moon falls in a single band down the middle. Faint guide lines and labels mark new, first quarter, full and last quarter. Each row is labelled with its new-moon date.
- **Each day is a small moon:** it is drawn at the phase it had that night. Its area matches that day's views, and busier days are also slightly brighter.
- **Labels and key:** the five busiest days carry their date and view count, with neighbouring days skipped so the labels don't overlap. A three-size scale key and a source credit run along the bottom.
- **Phase accuracy:** phases come from an average lunar month of 29.53 days, not exact dates. That can put a day up to about half a day off its true phase. The footer says so.

**What it shows:** the biggest days sit in the full-moon column. These are the eclipses of 14 March and 7 September (17,284 views, the year's top) and the 7 October supermoon. The clear exception is 20 July (13,576 views), the Apollo 11 anniversary, which falls on a thin waning crescent.

**The note** (`NOTE.md`): the title as a heading, then one paragraph. It says the form lays the year out by lunations so you can see at a glance whether readers follow the sky. My reasons for the dates are outside knowledge, not the data. The eclipse and supermoon dates are known events, but the note's line that something other than the sky drove the 20 July spike is my guess.
