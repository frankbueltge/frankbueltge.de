I made "The Readership Coast". The six other works all do the same thing: a grid of moon-phase discs, one row per lunation. Mine is an old-style nautical chart instead.

**How it reads:**
- **The year:** north is 1 January and each day is one horizontal line (a parallel), running south to 31 December.
- **The readers:** on each line, the sea comes in from the left edge as far as that day's page views. The scale is linear, so ordinary days make a ragged shore and spike days cut long, narrow inlets into the land.
- **The chart details:**
  - Engraved water-lines follow the shore.
  - Numbers in the sea give the readings in thousands, with hundreds as a subscript, about one a week.
  - The borders are marked in months down the sides and in readers (0 to 20,000) along the top and bottom.
  - Dash-dot lines on the land mark where each month starts.
  - There is a title box, a compass rose whose north points to 1 January, and a table of each month's average daily views.
- **Named places:**
  - Eclipse Sound: 7 Sep, 17,284, the year's highest day.
  - Apollo Inlet: 20 Jul, 13,576.
  - Blood Moon Inlet: 14 Mar, 11,854.
  - Harvest Moon Fjord: 7 Oct, 10,896.
  - Supermoon Cove: 5 Nov, 7,624.
  - Seven Planets Cove: 28 Feb, 8,534.
  - Solstice Point: 21 Jun, 3,777, the year's lowest day.

Two of these names rest on outside facts I did not check against a source. 7 Oct being the Harvest Moon is my own reasoning (it is the full moon nearest the September equinox). Linking 28 Feb to the planet alignment of that date is a likely cause, not a confirmed one.

The page is drawn on a single canvas with plain JavaScript and loads only `data.js`. I rendered it at 1100 × 800 with headless Chromium. It drew without errors, and I moved labels until none overlapped. A small routine places the month names so they avoid the inlet labels, the title box, the compass and the table. The screenshot script was kept outside your folder.

`NOTE.md` has the title and one paragraph explaining the form. My reason for the form: the Moon moves the tides, and readership behaves like a tide, with a low steady swell and sudden surges that ebb within a day or two.
