I made "Thirteen Lunations" and wrote the two files to `/tmp/claude-0/s116/mk/m07/work`. I haven't seen the image itself because no browser was available. The script ran without errors against a stand-in canvas in node, which let me check where the text lands, but not how the drawing looks.

**What the image is:** a single still canvas at 1100 × 800, using only `data.js` and no other files, libraries or network. It lays out 2025 by the Moon's months instead of calendar months:
- **Rows:** there are 13, and each one runs from one new moon to the next. I typed the new-moon times (UTC) for 30 Dec 2024 to 18 Jan 2026 into the page from my own knowledge. Each row is labelled with the date its lunation starts.
- **Days:** each of the 365 days is a small moon. It sits at its age within that lunation and is drawn in its phase for that day: lit on the right while waxing, on the left while waning. This puts every full moon in the middle column. Faint vertical lines mark new moon, first quarter, full moon, last quarter and new moon.
- **Readers:** the size of each disc grows with the square root of that day's reader count. Its brightness follows the count on a log scale, from dim grey to warm white with a glow. Busier days are drawn on top of quieter ones.

**What the data shows:** two very large discs sit in the full-moon column on the two total lunar eclipses: 14 Mar (11,854 readers) and 7 Sep (17,284, the year's highest). Smaller bulges appear around most full moons.

**Labels:**
- 14 Mar and 7 Sep are labelled as total lunar eclipses. I didn't take those events from the data. They are my own knowledge, and I'm confident of both.
- 20 Jul (13,576) and 7 Oct (10,896) are labelled with the date and count only, because I'm not sure what caused those spikes.

The page also has a title, a two-line explanation, a size key at 4,000, 8,000 and 17,000 readers, and a source line.

`NOTE.md` has the title as a heading and one paragraph. It says the form uses the Moon's own month as the calendar, so you can see whether people look up the Moon when it is full: mostly a little, and a lot when it is eclipsed.
