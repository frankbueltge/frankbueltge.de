**How it differs from the six others:** all six use the same form. Each day is a moon drawn in its phase and sized by views, laid out one lunation per row with the full moons in a centre column. I left out moons and lunations entirely and asked a different question: do people read about the Moon more when the nights are longer?

**The form:** it is one canvas, with the data loaded through `<script src="data.js">` and no libraries.
- Each of the 365 columns is one day of 2025, read from noon at the top, through midnight in the middle, to the next noon at the bottom.
- Night and twilight at 45° N are drawn in four shades of blue, worked out from the Sun's position for each day. The dark area is widest in winter and narrowest in June.
- A pale bar centred on midnight shows that day's readers. It is scaled so the busiest day (7 September, 17,284) fills the full 24 hours, which works out to 720 readers an hour.
- Where a day's readers last longer than its night, the bar runs past dusk and dawn and is drawn as dark ink on the pale daylight. Only a few days do this.
- Five days are labelled: the eclipses on 7 September and 14 March, the Apollo 11 anniversary on 20 July, the supermoon on 7 October, and 21 June.
- The page also has a title, a two-line key, month labels, clock-time labels down the side, and the data source (CC0).

**What the data shows, as written in the note:** views do follow night length, but only loosely. The correlation between the logarithm of views and night length is about 0.40. Readers fall through spring, and the year's lowest day (3,777) was 21 June, the shortest night. December has the longest nights but its views drop again.

**One choice to know about:** 45° N is a stand-in for where the article's readers live; the data doesn't say where they are. The key on the page states which latitude is used.
