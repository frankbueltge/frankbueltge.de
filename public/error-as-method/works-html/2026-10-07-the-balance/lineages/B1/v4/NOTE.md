## v0

The work plays the record one day at a time (about two and a half minutes for 48 years): a white, ragged disc whose area is that day's ice extent breathes out each winter and in each summer, while a dotted ring holds the 1979–1990 average for the same calendar day, so whatever is missing shows as open water between the two, and beneath it a thin line writes the difference down as it happens, drawing a slow descent that is not there until you have lived through it. I chose area-to-scale and real-time pacing because a number like 5.96 means little to a stranger, but watching the ice fail to reach its old edge, season after season, against a fixed memory of what it used to be, makes the imbalance felt rather than read; pausing and dragging the line lets the viewer stop at any day and weigh it.

## v1

**(a)** The disc (the felt magnitude), the strip (the record and the way through time) and the year's clock converge in one dial: angle is the day of the year, radius is the square root of that day's extent, so a year swept around the centre is itself the disc, area to scale, and every finished year stays behind as a closed contour, the record now lives inside the figure instead of under it. **(b)** The seasonal cycle was the obstacle: it made the disc breathe by ten million km² while the loss was a hairline ring nobody could see; now the season *is* the shape (the angular form of the loop), and the deficit appears at every angle as a rust band between the year being drawn and the dotted 1979–1990 year, whose area is the year's mean shortfall, the balance, read out as a running figure. **(c)** Struck: the anomaly strip below, the invented ragged edge (the edge is now the real daily record), the breathing whole-disc, and the separate scrubber — you wind the dial itself backwards or forwards by dragging around it.

## v2

**(a)** The reading and the ice converge in one surface: the year, the day, today's extent and the running balance are no longer a column beside the dial but are written at its centre, on the ice itself, inked dark only where this year's ice has already been swept; and each finished contour now carries its own year's balance as its tint, so the record of shapes and the ledger of shortfalls are one line per year, and the long descent that v1 lost with the strip returns as a drift from grey-blue to rust, inward. **(b)** The obstacle was the white wedge: a great opaque sector that dominated the figure, covered its centre and left the numbers to crowd the month labels on the left; now the wedge is the page, and its incompleteness is the means: the year's name and figures are dim until the ice reaches them, so the inscription fills in at the pace of the year. **(c)** Struck: the left column of three large figures, the "today against 1979–1990" figure (the band at the hand already says it), the four-line legend reduced to a short key, and the off-centre placement that made the dial collide with its own text.

## v3

**(a)** The ice, the record and the reading converge in one act of covering: the opaque ice of the year being drawn now buries every past contour it exceeds, so what stays visible beyond its edge is exactly the years that had more ice on that day — the record is sorted by the ice itself, and the balance is read off the hand as that split, beads of earlier years left out in the water against marks sunk into the ice, counted at the centre. **(b)** The obstacle was the wedge's opacity, which in v2 swept over the record and erased it; now erasure is the means: to be covered is to have been beaten, and as the record lows arrive the water beyond the edge fills with the lines of every year that had more. **(c)** Struck: the dotted 1979–1990 ring and the rust/blue band (the early contours, crowding the rim, are now the old memory, and the open water striated with them is the shortfall), and the running "balance so far" mean, replaced by the count.

What changes: the contours of past years are drawn stronger so that they read in the open water, the ice buries them, a bead for every earlier year sits on the hand at that year's extent for the same day (light in the water if it had more ice, dark on the ice if it had less), and the centre reads "more ice in n of m years before". Why: v2's balance was a mean against a fixed baseline that the viewer had to take on trust; now the comparison is with every year the viewer has already watched, and the figure performs it rather than reports it.

## v4

**(a)** The ice and the record converge in one whole year: the white shape is no longer the year swept since 1 January but always the last twelve months, a closed disc whose edge ahead of the hand is still last year's and behind it already this year's, so the covering of v3 now works at every angle at once — the whole water, all around, holds exactly the years that had more ice than the latest ice on that day — and the hand is a seam where today meets the same day a year ago. **(b)** The obstacle was the January reset: every year the ice was wiped back to an empty dial, so for most of the year most of the record lay uncompared in the water, and the opening screen was a lone wedge in the dark; now the cut is the means: the seam at the hand is the step between this year and last, drawn as a short radial stroke (rust where the ice is less than a year before, blue where more) and read at the centre as the year-on-year balance. **(c)** Struck: the wedge with its two radial edges and its origin at the top, the 1979–1990 baseline that still tinted the contours and beads (the comparison is now only with years the viewer has watched, performed by the covering), and the inscription that fills in with the sweep, which survives only in the first year of the record, before there is a year to close the disc.

What changes and why: the ice is drawn as a rolling year with the seam at the hand, the contours lose their baseline tint and are weighed only by being covered or left in the water, and the centre adds how much more or less ice there is than a year ago — so that at any moment the whole dial, not one sector of it, is the balance.
