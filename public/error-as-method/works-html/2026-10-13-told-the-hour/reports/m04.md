I made "Thirty-Six and a Half Hours" and wrote its note.

- **The work:** /tmp/claude-0/s123/mk/m04/work/index.html is one standalone SVG page at 1100 × 800. The original JPEG is embedded in it as data. It loads nothing else: the only URL in the file is the SVG namespace string, which fetches nothing.
- **The note:** /tmp/claude-0/s123/mk/m04/work/NOTE.md
- **Scratch:** /tmp/claude-0/s123/mk/m04/scratch/ holds the generator `build.py` and `preview.png`.

**What I did first with the file.** I dumped the greyscale values of all 480 pixels. Then, for each column, I measured the top and bottom of the dark band, its centre and its total ink. The main fact is the arithmetic: 1,464 hours across 40 columns is 36.6 hours per pixel column. Each column is a day and a half, so no pixel lines up with a day, and any pattern within a day cannot survive as rhythm.

**How I got to the form.** I decided not to fake a curve and to show only what the picture kept.
- The work enlarges the 40 × 12 grid. Each pixel's original grey is a pale wash.
- On top are exactly 1,464 dots, one per hour. Each column gets its 36 or 37 hours, and they are shared out among the column's pixels by darkness. I treated very light pixels as background before sharing them out.
- Inside a pixel the dots sit on a neat grid, so they read as counts, not positions.
- Each column's hour count is printed above a ruler. The ruler sets the 41 pixel boundaries against the 62 day boundaries, so you can see they don't line up.
- The original image appears at its real size (40 × 12) beside a two-line caption.

**What the commissioner should know.**
- The number of dots in each column is exact, because time runs evenly across the picture.
- How the dots are shared out within a column is my estimate from JPEG darkness, which includes compression noise. Their exact places inside a pixel are invented on purpose and say nothing.
- I could not open the page in a browser here. I checked the layout with a raster of the dots and washes, which looks right; I did not see the text as rendered, so it's worth a quick look in a browser.

**What I think was measured.** I don't know, and I wouldn't name a quantity. What the file shows:
- The dots fill a thick band covering about two-thirds of the height in almost every column. So within any day and a half the value ranges widely, which fits a strong regular cycle within the day, though that cycle can't be seen directly.
- The band holds level for roughly the first half.
- From about column 22 to 32 (around days 34–50) it sags: the top drops by two or three pixel rows while the bottom stays put or goes slightly lower.
- In the last six columns (about the final 9 days) the whole band jumps up and its top reaches the edge of the picture, so the highest values may be clipped.

A large daily swing plus a shift lasting several weeks is the kind of pattern you'd expect from something tied to day and night and to weather or season, such as air temperature. That is a guess resting only on the thickness of the band and its slow drift.
