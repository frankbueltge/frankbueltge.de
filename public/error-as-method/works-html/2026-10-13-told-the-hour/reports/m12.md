I made **Folded at Seven**, a single still SVG. It cuts the record every 24 hours and lays the days in rows of seven: 61 days make nine rows, and the last row stops after five days. The files are `/tmp/claude-0/s123/mk/m12/work/index.html` (about 10 KB, loads nothing from outside) and `/tmp/claude-0/s123/mk/m12/work/NOTE.md`. My working scripts and screenshots are in `/tmp/claude-0/s123/mk/m12/scratch`.

**What I did first with the file.** I looked at it, then printed its pixels as a text grid and measured each column. The dots are faint grey, blurred over about 1–2 px, and they often overlap. They run from x≈3 to x≈297, so each pixel column holds about 5 hours and one day is about 4.8 px wide.

**How I got to the form.**
- **Weekly rhythm.** I averaged where the dots sit in each 24-hour slice of the image. One pair of days in every seven is low throughout: days 0–1, 7–8, 14–15 and so on. The other five days rise to a high level during the day.
- **Start time.** When I folded the slices by time of day, the busy days are low in hours 0–4, high from about 8 to 16, and fall back by 22. That fits a record that starts at midnight.
- **Recovering the dots.** I found 699 dots in the picture and measured each one's position to a fraction of a pixel. Using how dark each dot was, I estimated how many hourly values it hides (overlapping values at the same level are taken as consecutive hours). That gives 1,244 drawn dots out of 1,464.
- **Why this fold.** With seven days to a row, the quiet pairs form two columns down the left edge. The record happens to begin with a quiet pair.

**What you should know.**
- **Time precision.** Each dot's time is only known to within about ±2.5 hours, because the picture holds about 5 hours per pixel column.
- **Missing values.** About 15% of the values cannot be separated where dots sit on top of each other, so the work shows 1,244 dots, not 1,464.
- **Where the days start.** I cut the days at the record's first hour and did not shift them. The midnight start is my inference, not something I was told.
- **Text on the page.** The work adds a title, a one-line subtitle and a caption. It adds no weekday names or dates.

**My view of what was measured (an inference, moderate confidence).** I think this is something driven by people being present in a workplace or institution: most likely a building's electricity use, or possibly indoor CO2. It rests on four things in the file:
1. **Office-hours shape.** Five busy days and two quiet days repeat every week. On busy days the value is high for about 8–10 daytime hours, with fairly sharp rises and falls.
2. **A quiet break mid-record.** Days 35–50 are unusually quiet:
   - Days 39, 40 and 41 are as low as weekends.
   - Days 46 and 47 are low again, two weekdays the following week.
   - Days 37–38, 44–45 and day 48 are only half-busy.

   If days 0–1 are a Saturday and Sunday, then days 39–41 fall on Wed–Fri and days 46–48 on Wed–Fri a week later. That matches 24, 25 and 26 December, then 31 December, 1 January and a bridge day on 2 January, in a year when Christmas Day is a Thursday (2025 is one). The record would then run from Saturday 15 November to Wednesday 14 January, in the Northern Hemisphere.
3. **Higher values in January.** The busy days just after the break (days 53–55) reach the highest values in the record.
4. **What it probably isn't.** The rises and falls are too sharp, and too tied to the week, for an outdoor natural quantity like air temperature.
