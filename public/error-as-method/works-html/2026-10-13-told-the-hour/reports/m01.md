I made a still image called "Thirty-Six and Three Fifths". The finished files are /tmp/claude-0/s123/mk/m01/work/index.html (one canvas, all the data inside it, nothing loaded from elsewhere) and /tmp/claude-0/s123/mk/m01/work/NOTE.md. My working files (pixel dump, a preview script and preview.png) are in /tmp/claude-0/s123/mk/m01/scratch.

**What I did first:** I printed the grey value of every one of the 480 pixels (40 × 12). That showed what the file really is. A 40-pixel width cannot show 1,464 separate dots. Each column of pixels squeezes together about 36.6 values (1464 / 40). Each column shows up as a dark vertical band across the range those values covered, softened by the JPEG compression. No single value can be recovered. All the file holds is, for each column, which of 12 height levels were inked and how darkly.

**How I got to the form:** I decided not to invent a smooth curve. Instead I gave all 1,464 values their own hairline, placed at its exact position in time across about 980 px. Each hairline is shaded with the 12 greys of the picture column it falls in. Where a value straddles a pixel boundary, it gets a weighted mix of the two columns. Small red ticks mark the 39 pixel boundaries, and a row of 1,464 ticks along the bottom counts the values. A few lines of small text say what the image is. The result is the record at its full count and at the coarse detail the picture actually has: the loss of detail is what you see.

**What the commissioner should know:**
- The image looks mostly like a dark mass, and that is accurate. In most columns the values spread over 7 to 9 of the 12 levels, so the source carries very little.
- I hid the faintest JPEG haze (greys within about 12% of white), so blur from compression isn't drawn as data.
- I had no browser here, so I couldn't render the HTML itself. I checked the look with a Python copy of the same drawing (scratch/preview.png). The text labels and red ticks were only checked by reading the code.

**My view of what was measured (a guess):** 1,464 = 61 × 24, which fits hourly readings over 61 days, such as two summer months. It also equals 4 × 366, which fits readings every six hours over a leap year. Both are guesses from the count alone. What the file does show:
- In almost every column the values cover a wide band, which suggests a strong cycle shorter than a column (about 1.5 days if the readings are hourly), such as a daily cycle.
- The band slowly sinks over roughly columns 22 to 31: the highest values drop away and lower levels fill in.
- In the last six or seven columns the band jumps clearly upward (the top row inks for the first time).

That pattern fits something like air temperature, with a daily swing, a cool spell, then a warm spell at the end. The picture can't confirm it, so I kept any claim about what was measured out of the work.
