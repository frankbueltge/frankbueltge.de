**What I did first with the file**
I looked at it, then printed its pixels as text and measured them. The data runs across pixel columns 3–296, so 294 pixels hold 1,464 hours: about 4.8 pixels per day and about 5 hours per pixel column. The hours can't be pulled apart from the picture. Each pixel column is a vertical smear of about five values.

**How I got to the form**
- **Repeating patterns:** I averaged each pixel column's height and checked how strongly the record repeats itself at different shifts. It repeats every ~4.8 pixels (one day) and every ~34 pixels (seven days).
- **Per-day pattern:** in every regular week the record shows five high days and then two low ones. The record starts on the first of the two low days.
- **Two broken weeks:** days 37–48 break the pattern. Two high days are lowered, then three days are as low as a weekend, then a week where nothing reaches the usual level. After that the normal rhythm returns, a little higher than before.
- **Within the day:** if the record starts at midnight, values are low in the early hours and high from about 10:00 to 19:00.

The form follows from this. I cut the strip into its 61 days and folded it into a calendar: 10 columns of weeks, read left to right as the strip was, and 7 rows of weekdays, with the two low days at the bottom. Every mark is an actual pixel from your image, drawn as a short dash as wide as the hours that pixel column covers. That's why each day shows as five columns of dashes. I kept that coarseness on purpose rather than invent hourly values. Behind each day, faintly, are all the other days in the same row, so the broken weeks show up as days sitting below their usual shape. The page has no words or axes, like the source.

**Things you should know**
- The values are only positions in the picture. The values are only heights within the original plot. There's no zero or scale, so nothing can be said about absolute amounts.
- Putting the two quiet days at the bottom of the grid is my reading, but it's well supported by the 7-day repeat.
- The slots before day 1 and after day 61 are left empty on purpose.
- The JPEG compression makes some marks a little fuzzy, and pixel columns that straddle two days are split by time.

**My view of what was measured**
Something driven by people's working hours, most likely electricity demand for a region or a large set of buildings, or possibly traffic. This rests on the daily rise and fall, the five-days-on, two-days-off week, and the level rising slightly toward the end.

More specifically, the broken weeks fit Christmas and New Year closely. If day 0 is a Saturday and Christmas fell on a Wednesday:
- days 37–38 would be 23–24 December (lowered),
- days 39–41 would be 25–27 December (low),
- day 46 would be 1 January (low).

That would make the record run from about Saturday 16 November to Wednesday 15 January, in a year like 2019 or 2024, in the northern winter. The higher January level fits a demand like heating or lighting. This is inference from the shape alone, not something the file states.
