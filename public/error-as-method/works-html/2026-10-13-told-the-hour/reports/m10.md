I made "Five Hills, Except": the 1,464 values folded into ten stacked weeks. It's in `/tmp/claude-0/s123/mk/m10/work/index.html` (about 10 KB, with the data inside it) and `/tmp/claude-0/s123/mk/m10/work/NOTE.md`. I rendered the page at 1100×800 in headless Chromium: no script errors, and it loads nothing from outside.

**What I did first with the file.** I looked at it, then printed its pixels as text. The dots are about 2–3 px across, and there are about 4.9 values per pixel column, so they overlap heavily. You can't read off each value exactly. Measuring how the picture repeats from left to right gave a period of about 4.86 px, which is about 24 values, and a stronger one at about 34 px, which is 7 of those periods. Magnified, the pattern is plain: two low days, five high days, over and over, broken once in the second half.

**How I got from it to the form.**
- **Recovering the values.** I first traced a single path through the dots, but it covered only 59% of the ink. So I switched to a per-column method. Each column gets as many values as fall in it (4 or 5), spread to match where its ink is. Their order within the column is set by whether the neighbouring columns rise or fall.
- **What that recovery shows.** Hourly lags of 24–25 and 167–168. Read as 61 days of 24 values, the day averages run 2 low, 5 high, repeated, apart from one disturbed stretch.
- **The form.** I cut the series into 168-value rows, aligned so each day of the week sits in the same column, and stacked them as ridges. Each tinted band runs down to the next week's line. The weekday hills line up down the page and the two quiet days form low columns. Two rows sag in mid-week where the hills should be. The only text is a small title and the line "1,464 values, folded every 168"; there are no axes or numbers, as in the source.

**What you should know.**
- The values are my estimate from a compressed 300 px picture, not the original data. They are on a relative scale with an unknown baseline.
- Detail finer than about 5 hours is guesswork, because the order within a column is inferred. I smoothed over about that span.
- Starting the first row on day six of the week, and reading 24 values as a day starting at midnight, are my assumptions. They aren't given anywhere in the file.

**My view of what was measured.** I think it is hourly electricity demand, or something just as tied to the working week (a building's energy use, or traffic), for 61 days from mid-November to mid-January, most likely in a European country. I can't rule out the other two from the file alone. This rests on:
- **Hourly over 61 days.** 1,464 = 61 × 24, and the cycles come out at 24 and 168 values.
- **A working week.** Five high days and two low ones repeat very regularly. Averaged over the day, the values are lowest about 4 positions into each 24 and highest around 12–13, which looks like a night low and a midday high.
- **Christmas and New Year.** If the first day is a Saturday, the collapsed days fall on Wed–Fri (days 39–41) and then Wed–Thu (days 46–47). That is exactly where 24–26 Dec and 31 Dec–1 Jan fall in a year when Christmas is on a Thursday (2025 is one). The Monday and Tuesday before are also lower, and things recover the following Monday.
- **Europe and winter.** Christmas Eve is as low as Christmas Day, which suggests somewhere it is effectively a holiday, as in German-speaking or Nordic countries. Day 52, which would be 6 January, is normal. The level in January is higher than in November, which fits colder weather pushing demand up.

The date, the country and the variable are inference, not fact. The 24/168 structure is solid.
