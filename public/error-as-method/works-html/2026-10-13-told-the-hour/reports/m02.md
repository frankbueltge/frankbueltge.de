The work is "Against the Usual Week": the 1,464 readings cut into weekly rows of 168 and stacked like lines in a ledger, each row set against the median of the ordinary weeks. Under the record I think two weeks of a holiday fortnight, most likely Christmas to New Year, interrupt a weekday/weekend rhythm. That reading is likely but not proven, and the work itself makes no claim about it.

**What I did first with the file.** I looked at the picture and printed it as text, character by character. The dots are only 1–2 px wide and overlap heavily: I could separate only about 400–490 dark blobs for 1,464 values. So the exact values can't be read back from the picture. That shaped everything after it.

**How I got from it to the form.**
- **Two rhythms.** I measured the average dot height in each pixel column (dots run from x 0 to x 298) and searched it for repeating patterns. Two stood out, at 4.8 px and 33.5 px. At about 4.9 values per pixel, that is about 24 and about 164 readings, close to 168. With 1,464 = 61 × 24, this reads as hourly readings over 61 days, with a daily and a weekly cycle.
- **The weekly pattern.** Folding by 168 readings, the first two days of each week are low and the other five are high. Days 35–48 break the pattern: they are low throughout. After that the level ends higher than before.
- **Rebuilding approximate hourly values.** I assigned each reading to its pixel column. Within a column I took the values from the spread of ink, and ordered them up or down to follow the neighbouring columns.
- **The image.** It is an inline SVG with no scripts or external files, and I checked a 1100 × 800 screenshot. Each row has a pale "usual week" behind it: for each hour, the median of the seven ordinary weeks. The readings are drawn as dots. A red stroke fills the gap where a reading falls well short of the usual week, and a slate stroke where it goes well beyond. Rows 6 and 7 show up as a large red hollow, and the last week and a half mostly as slate.

**What you should know.**
- **The dots are a reconstruction.** They come from the overlapping dots in a small, compressed picture, not from the original numbers. Each value is known only to within about a 5-hour block, and the order inside each block is my guess. The weekly and fortnight-scale shapes are solid; the fine shape within a day is approximate.
- **No clock times.** I didn't put clock times or dates on the image, because the start of the record is not known. The small tick marks fall every 24 readings, counted from the first reading.

**My view of what was measured.** Something driven by human activity, most likely hourly electricity demand (or something like it, such as traffic) over Christmas and New Year. It rests on these points:
- **The cycles.** A strong 24-reading cycle and a strong 168-reading cycle. A cycle of exactly one week points to people's working week, not weather.
- **Weekends.** In each week the first two days are low and the next five high, so the record would start on a Saturday.
- **The low fortnight.** Days 35–48 stay low the whole time. Inside it, days 39–41 (Wed–Fri if day 0 is a Saturday) and day 46 (a Thursday) are the lowest. That matches 24–26 December and 1 January if day 0 is 15 November, which happens in years where 24 December is a Wednesday (2003, 2008, 2014, 2025).
- **The level afterwards.** It rises above the earlier weeks, which fits demand in a cold January.
- **Peak time.** In the rebuilt values the daily low and high are about 9–10 readings apart, which fits a night low and an evening high.

This is my reading of the shape, not something I checked against any other source.
