The work is finished. It's called **One Day, Sixty-One Times**: the record is cut into 61 equal pieces of 24 values each, and the pieces are laid on top of each other. That stack shows one lopsided arch. I opened `index.html` in a headless browser at 1100 × 800: it shows as intended, gives no errors and makes no network requests.

**What I did first with the file.** I looked at it, then printed its pixels as text. The dots sit about 2 px across, at roughly 4.9 values per pixel column, so most dots overlap into vertical streaks. That limits what the picture holds: about five pixel-columns per cycle. Single values can't be recovered, and I didn't try to rebuild the full series of 1,464.

**How I got to the form.**
- **Finding the rhythm:** I measured the repeating patterns in the dots' average height across the picture. The strongest one repeats exactly **61 times**, and 1,464 / 61 = 24. Other nearby repeat lengths line up far more weakly.
- **Stacking the pieces:** I overlaid all the pixels by their position within each 24-value piece. Each day starts slightly further along the pixel grid, so the 61 days together sample the cycle much more finely than any one day does.
- **What the stack shows:** a clear, lopsided profile. It rises over about 40% of the cycle and falls over about 60%.
- **Day by day:** I estimated each day's level and the size of its swing. Most days swing widely. About seven days in the middle (days 17–23, roughly picture x 85–120) are very calm. The level drifts upward over the whole record.

I tried two spiral forms first, one turn per day. I dropped them because the daily swing just looked like the rings being slightly off-centre.

**The final page.**
- **Bottom strip:** the record as it came, drawn from the picture's own dark pixels, with a hairline at each cut.
- **Main area:** the 61 pieces laid on one another, so the arch emerges. The dots are 722 dot centres I located in the picture to within a fraction of a pixel. A faint haze of all 3,207 dark pixels sits underneath. Dot colour runs from amber (first days) through madder to indigo (last days), so the stacked dots can be traced back to the strip.
- **Words:** a title and two italic captions.

**What you should know.**
- In the stacked part, each dot's height is measured from **that day's own level**. I estimated those levels from the pixels, so the stacked part shows each day's swing rather than its height in the original record. The bottom strip keeps the original heights.
- I chose where to cut: at the low point of the cycle. That puts the arch roughly in the middle of the page.
- The dot centres cover only about half the values, because overlapping dots merge into streaks. The faint haze is there so the rest still shows.
- All of the material (pixel list, dot centres, daily levels) is written into `index.html`. It loads nothing else.

**My view of what was measured: most likely hourly air temperature over 61 days.** This is an inference from the picture alone, and nothing in the file states it. It rests on four things:
1. **61 days of hourly readings.** 1,464 = 61 × 24, and the cycle repeats exactly 61 times. That fits a solar day sampled every hour.
2. **Daily, not tidal.** The cycle follows the solar day, not the slightly longer lunar day. Tide gauges, the other obvious candidate, follow the lunar day.
3. **Fast rise, slow fall.** The arch climbs in about 10 hours and falls over about 14. That is the typical daily temperature curve: coldest near dawn, warmest mid-afternoon, slow cooling overnight.
4. **Weather-like variation.** Wide swings on most days and a calm stretch of about a week look like clear spells and an overcast or wet spell. The upward drift could be a change of season, but I can't tell which season or place.
