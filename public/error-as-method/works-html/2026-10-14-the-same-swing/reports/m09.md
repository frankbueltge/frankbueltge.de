I made "Forty Widths" (`/tmp/claude-0/s124/mk/m09/work/index.html` and `NOTE.md`). The page is a single canvas at 1100×800 with the image data written into it, and it loads no other file.

**What I did first:** I read every pixel of `record.jpg`. It is pure grey (R=G=B), stored with two JPEG quantisation tables, and its only metadata is a stock sRGB colour profile copyright Google 2016. It does not look like the brief describes. There are no separate dots on white. Every one of the 40 columns is a solid dark run covering about 4 to 10 of its 12 pixel rows.

**How I got to the form:** The likeliest explanation is that the record has far more values than the picture has columns, so the dots in each column merged into one dark span. That means the individual values can't be recovered. What survives in each column is where the dark starts and stops: the top and bottom edge of the swing. I measured those edges by marking a pixel dark when its grey value is below 128 and estimating partial pixels from how dark the neighbouring pixel is. The work shows:
- all 480 original pixel tones, enlarged and split into 40 upright columns, compression smudges included;
- a thin red line tracing the top and bottom edges;
- a row of red strokes underneath giving each column's height of swing;
- a faint checker of the 8×8 blocks the compressor worked in.

I chose not to draw invented dots, because that would fabricate values the file no longer holds. There was no browser to render the page in, so I checked it with a Python mock-up of the same layout (`/tmp/claude-0/s124/mk/m09/scratch/mock.png`) and a syntax check of the script. The scripts are in the same scratch folder.

**What the commissioner should know:**
- The brief's "one dark dot per value on white" isn't visible at this size. Either the record is dense or the dots are drawn very large; in both cases each column is a range, not a value.
- The JPEG is about 936 bytes, so it is heavily compressed. Some steps in the edges fall exactly on the 8-pixel block boundaries (columns 8, 16 and 32, and row 8) and may be compression artefacts rather than data.
- Each edge is only known to within roughly one pixel.

**My view of what was measured (low confidence):** a fast, continuous oscillation recorded over a long stretch, such as a waveform, a seismogram or a tide-gauge trace. It rests on three things:
- Every column covers a large share of the full height, as a quantity swinging back and forth faster than one column would.
- The size of the swing rises and falls fairly regularly. It is widest around columns 5–7, 19–20 and 28–31, and narrowest around 12–15, 24–25 and 38–39, so it repeats about every 11–12 columns, about 3.5 times across the image.
- The middle of the band also drifts slowly.

That rise and fall is what you'd expect from tides over about seven weeks, with spring and neap tides about 15 days apart. But the picture has no units or time scale, so this is a guess and not a finding. The swing is also uneven: the top edge moves about twice as much as the bottom edge, which pure tides would not obviously produce.
