I made a still image called "Width of a Swing" (`index.html`) and wrote `NOTE.md` to go with it. The main thing to know is that the picture is not the dot plot the brief describes, so the work shows how far the values reached in each column, not the values themselves.

**What I did first with the file.** I read it as a 40 × 12 grid of greys, printed every pixel value and looked at a 20× enlargement. It is not dark dots on white. Every column is dark over a solid vertical stretch: between 4 and 10 of the 12 rows, top and bottom rows mostly white. There must be many values per pixel column, so the dots merged into one band. The low-quality JPEG then smeared away anything inside that band. The single values cannot be recovered. Only where the band starts and stops in each column survives, which is how far the values reached up and down.

**How I got to the form.**
- I measured each column's upper and lower edge at a finer step than whole pixels, as the point where the grey crosses mid-grey.
- The main field is 40 vertical strokes. Each stroke's ink is that column's 12 original greys, stretched and blended smoothly from row to row. Two red lines trace the edges, and a dotted line marks the band's average middle.
- Below are 40 bars showing each column's height alone (its reach).
- In the corner, the whole 40 × 12 source is drawn at 3× so its pixels can be counted.
- I chose not to draw any oscillation inside the band, because the file does not contain it.
- All 480 grey values are embedded in the page. It loads nothing else: it is plain JavaScript drawing on a canvas, with a system serif font.
- I checked it in headless Chromium. Everything fits within 1100 × 800, with the source inset ending at y≈776.

**For the person who commissioned this.**
- The brief's description ("one dark dot per value on white") doesn't match the file. Either the record has many more values than 40, or the picture was shrunk and compressed until the dots ran together. If a higher-resolution original exists, a different, more detailed work becomes possible.
- The edges come from a blurred JPEG, so they are accurate to roughly half a pixel. Small wiggles in the red lines may be compression noise rather than signal.
- The page is drawn at a fixed 1100 × 800 and does not adapt to other sizes.

**My view of what was measured, held loosely.** It looks like a quantity swinging quickly around a steady middle, with the size of the swing slowly rising and falling. That rests on three things in the file:
- **Steady middle:** the band's centre stays near row 5.5–6.5 across the whole width.
- **Changing width:** the band is about 9–10 rows wide at columns 0–8, narrows to about 4 at columns 12–15, widens to about 8–9 at columns 19–31, and narrows again to about 5 at the right edge.
- **Never zero:** at its narrowest the swing still keeps roughly 40–45% of its widest reach.

That pattern of two swells about 22 columns apart fits ocean tides seen over about a month, with spring and neap tides. It would equally fit beats between two close frequencies, or a vibration or pendulum record. Nothing in the file settles which, and it gives no time scale.
