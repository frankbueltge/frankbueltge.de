I rendered the page in headless Chromium at 1100×800. It threw no errors and made no network requests. Working files (scripts, the recovered values, screenshots) are in `/tmp/claude-0/s128/mk/m08/scratch`.

**What I did first with the file.** I looked at it, dumped its darkness as an ASCII map, and measured column statistics. Two things stood out. The values sit mostly in a dense high band, with a sparse tail of drops below it. Also, every column profile repeats about every 4.8 pixels, which is roughly one day.

**From the file to the form.**
- **Getting values back.** Only about 415 separate dots can be seen out of 1,464, because most of them overlap in the dense band. So I worked out the values in reverse instead of picking dots. I gave each hour its own x position (hour i at (i+0.5)·300/1464), modelled each dot as a soft, partly see-through spot, and moved each hour's height until the redrawn picture matched the JPEG. The fit accounts for about 88% of the picture's ink. When re-plotted, it looks like the original.
- **What the values showed.** The daily cycle is a drop of about 8 hours from a plateau of about 15 hours. Its period is not 24 hours. The peak in the frequency analysis is sharp at about 23.5 hours (23h33m), and its timing stays steady across the whole record. Cut at 24 hours, the drop arrives about half an hour earlier each day.
- **The form.** That drift became the work. The same record is cut into rows twice:
  - On the left, each row is 24 hours. The low hours make a diagonal that goes once around the clock in about 50 days.
  - On the right, each row is 23h33m, and the low hours stand as a straight column.
  - Each row shows its own cycle and the next one, the usual layout for activity charts in rhythm research. The hours that fall below the local typical level (the middle value over ±30 hours) are filled in ink.
  - The unfolded record runs along the bottom as it came: one dot per hour.

**Things the commissioner should know.**
- **Values are recovered, not read.** Heights are only in picture rows (0–90). Inside the dense band they could be off by a pixel or two. An hour's position in time could be off by about an hour, and the order of hours that overlap is guessed.
- **The first ~14 hours are unreliable.** They come out almost flat and are probably a side effect of the fit at the image edge.
- **Everything assumes the brief's time scale.** Whether the period is shorter than a day depends on the 1,464 values evenly filling all 300 pixels. The dots do reach both edges, so I believe it. But if the brief's sampling were wrong (for example, readings slightly more than an hour apart), the period could really be 24 hours, and the left panel's drift would then come from the sampling, not the thing measured. The captions state the 23h33m period as a fact.
- **Other features in the data.** There is a sudden drop in level at about day 37–38, after which the daily drops get deeper. There is a sharp dip and recovery around day 20–21. There is also a weak swing of about 5.6 days.
- **Text on the page.** The page carries a small title and captions that I wrote. I did not publish it anywhere.

**My view of what was measured (a guess).** I think it is a self-timed biological rhythm running freely, without daylight to reset it to 24 hours. An example would be the body temperature of an animal kept in constant conditions; laboratory mice typically run on a cycle of about 23.5 to 23.7 hours. It rests on three things:
- a strong cycle of about 23.5 hours with a steady shape, plateau-then-drop;
- its timing drifts against the 24-hour day but stays fixed against its own period, which a quantity driven by the sun or by weather would not do;
- the dense ceiling with a skewed tail of drops below it.

The other explanations I considered fit worse:
- **Weather (for example relative humidity):** it would lock to 24 hours.
- **Tides:** they run later each day (about 24.8 hours), not earlier.
- **A clock or sampling error:** possible, and it can't be ruled out from the picture alone.
