The work is "Two Clocks", a single still canvas at 1100 × 800, and its NOTE.md is written. My conclusion is that the record does not behave like tidal water level. It keeps a 24-hour clock, and I found no trace of the lunar tide in it.

**What I did first.** I looked at the image, then dumped its pixels as text to see the dots. Each dot is about 1 px, and there are about 5 hourly values in every pixel column, so single values can't be pulled apart cleanly. I worked from the darkness of each column instead: its highest and lowest marks, its average height and how much ink it holds.

**From the file to the form.**
- **The 24-hour test.** I ran periodograms on the column series. The only strong peak is at 24.0 h.
  - The lunar periods have almost no power. At 12.42 h (the main twice-daily tide) the lowest-mark series has power 3 against 2,330 at 24 h. At 24.84 h (the lunar day) it has 6. 25.82 h (the other main once-daily tide) is also near zero.
  - A twice-daily tide would still show in the column averages at about 75% strength even with 5 hours per pixel, so the blur does not hide it.
- **The fold test.** I folded the ink at different periods. At 24 h the lowest marks run from y≈35 for part of the day to y≈63 for a window of about 8–10 hours, every day. At 24 h 50 min and at 12 h 25 min the fold is flat.
- **The form.** Across the top is the record as received, with the falls below the usual level in rust. Below it, the same 61 days are cut into stacked rows twice: every 24 h 50 min (the moon's day, which tides follow) and every 24 h (the sun's day). In each row the colour shows how far the water falls below its usual height. Under the 24-hour cut the falls stack into one vertical band. Under the lunar cut the same falls slide diagonally. The page holds the extracted ink as an embedded string (about 23 KB in all) and loads nothing else. I checked it in a headless browser and it showed no errors and made no network requests.

**What the commissioner should know.**
- **Everything rests on reading pixels.** The values are not exact numbers, and the time resolution is about 5 hours per pixel. The colours in the cut rows are smoothed across that width, which leaves some slight diagonal texture from the pixel grid.
- **I assumed the time axis.** I took the first value to sit at x≈2.6 and the last at x≈296.4.
- **The start of the 24-hour rows is my choice.** The clock time of the record isn't given, so I placed the first cut 19.5 h before the first value to put the band mid-panel. A comment in the code says so. The band's position therefore says nothing about real time of day.
- **The captions keep the label I was given.** The title line calls it a tidal harbour record, and the closing captions say "a tide would line up on the left. this water lines up on the right." The piece shows the gap between the label and the data rather than hiding it.

**My view of what was measured.** Whatever it is, it is driven by the sun or by people, not the moon. It sits on a fairly steady high level, and once a day it drops in a V-shaped fall lasting about 8–10 hours, with weather-like drifts of several days on top. That shape fits something like relative humidity, which stays high at night and dips in the afternoon. It would also fit a water level that is managed daily, such as an impounded dock or a level driven by daily releases. As tidal water level it is implausible: there is no signal at 12.42 h or 24.84 h, and no 14.8-day spring–neap swing. Several stretches of about 5 consecutive hours also stay within 3 px of each other, which a dominant tide would not allow. I can't identify the quantity from this file alone.
