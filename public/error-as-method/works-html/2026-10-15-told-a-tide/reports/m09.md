I made **"Told a Tide"**: one still SVG drawing inside `/tmp/claude-0/s128/mk/m09/work/index.html`, with `/tmp/claude-0/s128/mk/m09/work/NOTE.md` beside it. My view is that the record doesn't behave like a tide: it repeats once a day, on what looks like the sun's 24 hours rather than the moon's tidal rhythm.

**What I did first with the file.** I looked at it, then turned it into a grid of grey values (ImageMagick, then Python) so I could measure where the ink falls in each column. The picture is 300 px wide for 1,464 hours, so each pixel column holds about 5 hours. The dots run from about x=2 to x=297 and y=4 to y=85, which suggests a small margin around the plot. I estimated those edges from where the ink starts and stops.

**How I got to the form.**
- **First check:** if this were a tidal harbour, the ~5 hours in a column should spread across much of the height, because a twice-daily tide moves fast. They don't. Most columns are tight, and about once per 5 px there is one column that drops sharply.
- **Repeating cycles:** I looked for repeating rhythms in the ink. There is essentially nothing at the twice-daily tidal period (12.42 h). The strong rhythm is about 24 h: the best fit lies between 23.7 and 24.0 h, depending on where exactly the plot's edges are.
- **Shape of the day:** averaged over all the days, it is a flat high level for about 17 hours, then one sharp low lasting about 6 hours.
- **Rebuilding the hours:** I turned the dots back into an hourly series. In each column I split dark runs into dots by their ink, then ordered them so the line runs smoothly into the next column. This is a reconstruction, not the original numbers.
- **The form:** the hours are wound onto a spiral, one turn per 24 h and 61 turns, with higher readings further out. Larger black dots mark each day's lowest hour, and they bunch on one side of the spiral. Red ticks show where the lows would fall if they kept the moon's time (12 h 25 min apart, counted from the first low), and those scatter all the way round. Because there are exactly 24 readings a day, the hourly dots also line up into straight lines running out from the centre, like a spider's web.

**What the commissioner should know.**
- **The values are approximate.** About 5 dots share each pixel column and overlap, and the JPEG blurs them. Which dot belongs to which hour within a column is my guess. Each day's lowest hour is reliable to within a few hours; individual hourly values are not reliable.
- **The start of the dial is arbitrary.** Nothing in the file gives the clock time of the first reading, so the top of the spiral is just where the record begins, not midnight. The picture has no units or scale either, and I didn't add any.
- **The 24-hour turn is my choice.** Within what the image can resolve, I can't tell 24.00 h apart from the 23.93 h daily tidal component. The shape is what makes me favour the sun.
- **Checking the page:** there is no browser here, so I checked the layout with a rough PIL rendering of the same drawing, without the captions. The page loads nothing else (the only URL in it is the standard SVG namespace label), uses plain serif fonts, and is about 87 KB.

**My view of what was measured.** This is probably not a tide-gauge water level, or at least not one where the tide shows. The evidence is:
- almost no twice-daily (tidal) signal;
- one dominant rhythm of about a day;
- a flat ceiling with a short, sharp drop each day, not the smooth rise and fall of a tide;
- day-to-day depth of the drop that is irregular, with no clear two-week pattern;
- a few separate days that sit higher (around days 1, 9 and 20) and a run that sits lower (days 38–45).

That pattern looks most like a weather variable such as relative humidity: high and flat overnight, dipping each afternoon, the dip shallow on overcast days and deep on dry ones. That is an inference from the shape alone. The material could also be a mislabelled series, a harbour level held steady by a lock, or something else with a daily cycle. The work itself only claims what the data shows: the lows keep the sun's time.
