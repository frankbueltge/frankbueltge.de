I made "Almost a Day": a still spiral in which each turn holds 24 hourly values, so the 61 days form 61 rings, and each hour is shaded darker the further it falls below the usual level around it. It's written as one stand-alone page (canvas, the recovered values embedded, no external loads) plus the note. I checked it in a headless browser at 1100×800: it draws with no errors and loads nothing else.

**What I did first with the file**
- Looked at the picture: a dense band of dots near the top, with a long sparse scatter of low values beneath it.
- Printed it as a character grid and checked where the data sits: the left and right edge columns are blank, so the data runs from about pixel 1.5 to pixel 297.5.

**How I got from the file to the form**
- **Finding the rhythm:** column-by-column statistics showed a strong cycle of 61–62 repeats across the width, i.e. one per day. The lower edge of the band carries it; the upper edge only drifts slowly.
- **Recovering the values:** a single dot is about 1.5 px across. I fitted all 1,464 dots at evenly spaced positions across the width, adjusting each dot's height until the redrawn picture matched the original.
  - Isolated low values come back reliably.
  - Inside the dense band, about 5 hours share each pixel column, so their order and exact heights are only roughly recovered.
- **What the values show:**
  - The daily cycle is lopsided: a long high plateau, then a sharp fall of roughly 5–6 hours.
  - There is no independent 12-hour signal; the 12-hour component is only a by-product of that lopsided shape.
  - The value distribution has a long tail downward.
  - Days 38–45 and 56–60 have the deepest and longest falls.
- **Choosing the form:** a 300 px timeline packs about five values into each column, which hides the daily fall. Wound on a 24-hour clock, the falls line up into one dark arm. I drew one tone per hour with thin paper gaps between hours. Because the shading emphasises the low hours, the work leans on the most reliable part of the recovery.

**What you should know**
- **The dip comes earlier each day.** Measured straight from the pixels, it repeats every 4.818 px, which with the data's roughly 296 px width is a period of about 23.8 h. Over the two months the dip moves about 8 hours earlier, from around hour 22 of the day count to around hour 15. In the spiral this shows as the arm turning.
  - This depends on how wide the data really is in the picture. A period of exactly 24 h would need the data to span only about 293.7 px, and the dots visibly reach about 297 px, so I don't think scaling explains it.
  - A sidereal-like period of about 23.93 h is at the edge of what the measurement allows.
  - The caption in the work states the drift as something you can see, not as a finding.
- **Hour 0 is simply the first value.** It sits at twelve o'clock; I don't know the actual time of day the record starts.
- **The page is fixed at 1100×800.** It doesn't reflow for small screens.
- **The folder name.** The working directory I was launched in is called "told-a-tide". I did not use that as evidence, and the file argues against a tide (see below).

**My view of what was measured (low confidence)**
I think it is a quantity driven by the sun and close to a natural ceiling, such as relative humidity or something that behaves like it. It rests on:
- one dominant cycle of about 24 h, with no 12.4 h tidal component (its strength is about 0.7 against about 7.6 for the daily one);
- the lopsided shape: a long high plateau, then a sharp, short daily fall;
- the long tail of low values below a dense upper band;
- day-to-day weather-like changes in how deep the dips go, with no regular fortnightly pattern.

It does not look like a tide: a tide would be twice-daily or close to a sine wave, with a 14-day spring–neap swing, and its timing would move later each day, not earlier. What I can't explain is why the dip moves earlier each day. Neither a pure 24-hour cause nor a tide predicts that.
