I made a work called **Sixty-One Ebbs**. It is in `/tmp/claude-0/s128/mk/m07/work/index.html` and stands alone: a canvas plus plain JavaScript, with the source picture's darkness values written into the page as a hex string, and no outside resources. `NOTE.md` is next to it. I checked it at 1100×800 in headless Chromium: no script errors and no requests except the page itself.

**What I did first with the file.** I looked at it, then read the darkness of each pixel column. Only columns 2 to 297 hold ink, so each pixel column carries about 4.96 hours of the record. I tried to find each dot separately and could not: there are about 650 separable dark spots for 1,464 values, because the dots overlap. So the order of the hours inside each column cannot be recovered. I worked from each column's vertical spread of ink and did not invent an hourly series.

**How I got to the form.**
- **Strongest rhythm.** A periodogram of the column means gave a strong once-daily cycle, best at about 23.8–23.94 hours. Folding the columns' lower edges on different periods gave the sharpest daily shape at 23.92 hours. There is a small peak near 12.0 hours and essentially nothing at 12.42 hours, the main lunar twice-daily tide.
- **First two layouts failed.** One was a row per day with each column as a thin mark; it came out as a sparse grid that showed mostly the picture's pixel lattice. The other was a row per day smeared into continuous bands; it washed out because the drops are deeper than a row is tall.
- **Final form.** All 61 days are laid on top of each other inside one turn of the 23.92-hour beat. Each day's height is measured from its own slow level, a running edge of the upper band over about two days, which removes multi-day swings about as large as the daily range. Each column is spread softly over its own five hours and layered on top of the others. The result is a dark steady band, with a soft basin falling away below it in the middle of every turn. A two-line caption sits at the bottom.

**Things the commissioner should know.**
- The two-day level is my processing step. Without it, the slow swings blur the daily shape.
- The 23.9-hour period depends on how I map pixels to hours. A shift of one pixel moves it by about 0.07 hours, so I cannot reliably tell 23.93 from 24.00 hours.
- The soft blur is deliberate. It shows that a 300-pixel picture cannot give back the timing of single hours.
- My scripts and screenshots are in `/tmp/claude-0/s128/mk/m07/scratch`.

**My view of what was measured.** The file does not look like a typical tidal harbour, where the twice-daily lunar tide dominates and the curve is roughly a smooth wave. Here:
- **No lunar twice-daily tide.** Nothing shows at 12.42 hours.
- **A once-a-day cycle dominates.** Its period is about 23.9 hours, which is closer to the main once-daily tidal period (23.93 hours) than to the 24.00-hour solar day, but only weakly: the fold's contrast score is 82 at 23.9 hours against 61 at 24.0.
- **The once-daily signal is not a clean tide.** The cycle 25.8 hours long that normally comes with it is weak. Its strength rises and falls but never comes close to vanishing, whereas a tide made of those two parts should almost disappear about every 13.7 days.
- **Lopsided shape.** The values sit in a narrow upper band for most of each day and then drop sharply and far for a few hours.
- **Slow swings.** Changes over several days are as large as the daily range.

So either this is a harbour with small, once-daily tides, sharp short low waters and strong weather effects, or the quantity is not water level but something driven by the sun's day. Relative humidity would fit the shape, since it presses against a ceiling and dips once a day. On this evidence I lean slightly toward the second, but I hold it loosely.
