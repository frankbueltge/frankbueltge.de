# The Hour It Falls

The record, read back out of its picture, mostly rests at a slowly wandering level and, once a day, drops away for a few hours before returning. So the work folds the 61 days on top of one another as a double-plotted day chart (the form chronobiologists use to ask whether a rhythm keeps to the clock). Each row holds one day and the day after it. A dark line marks where the record rests, and a rust-coloured hang below it shows how far the lowest readings fell. Above the fold, the original strip is redrawn as it was found. I chose this form because folding asks the question the record answers most clearly. The falls stack into upright columns, so whatever drives them keeps a 24-hour day (a 24.84-hour lunar rhythm would make them lean). The fold also keeps each day's variation, including the run of deep, low days late in the record. Because the source has only about five readings per pixel column, every hang is a smoothed, roughly five-hour reading, not an exact hourly trace.
