# Fifty Minutes Later

The 1,464 hourly readings are cut into rows of twenty-four hours and stacked, earliest at the top, as lines laid one over another. Each line hides whatever lies beneath it. Read this way, the record shows what the original strip only hints at. Each day carries two crests, and from row to row they slide steadily to the right: they arrive about fifty minutes later each day, the clock of a lunar tide (the record's main rhythm measures 12.42 hours). Every two weeks the crests swell and then flatten again. Low in the stack, one day lifts a single dome far above the rest, and it erases the days drawn above it. I chose the solar-day fold because it sets the clock we live by against the clock the water keeps, and the gap between them becomes the image's grain. Note that the hourly values were recovered from a small compressed picture. Within each five-hour pixel column, the order of the readings is inferred from the tide, not read off the picture, so the lines are a careful reconstruction, not a transcription.
