Down to the Grain

One record, 1,464 hours of something measured, drawn as one dot per hour. The same plot, four sizes. Twelve makers each got one size and nothing else: no axes, no name, no place, no date. Move the hour and see how many other hours share its pixel. Beside each size is what its makers made.

What came back

Every maker read the pixels with code (12 of 12), and every one found the one high event (12 of 12, by a coder who read their notes and reports). Where they split is the line above. At 1,200 and 300 pixels wide the twice-daily rhythm was still finer than the picture. All six makers measured it, at 12.42 hours, named the record a tide, and redrew one value per hour folded on the moon's day. At 75 and 19 pixels wide it was not. All six drew the pixel grid itself and put the hours back into it as a count, not as values. None of the three given the 19-pixel picture named the quantity as water.

What it was

Water level every hour at The Battery, the southern tip of Manhattan (NOAA tide station 8518750), from 15 September to 14 November 2012, in metres above mean lower low water. The highest hour, 01:00 GMT on 30 October, is 4.263 m. Everywhere else the high waters stay near 1.6 to 2.0 m. NOAA's report on Hurricane Sandy gives this station's maximum as 4.284 m at 01:24 GMT that night. The second, smaller raised stretch on 7–8 November is a later storm, and its low waters stay high.

No maker was told any of this. The six who could still see the tide said tide gauge, storm surge. None named the storm. Two of the three at 75 pixels guessed a tide gauge from a two-week swell in the band's width, both saying they were unsure; one of them also offered New Year fireworks as an alternative and said the file held no dates to check it. The three at 19 pixels would not name the quantity at all.
