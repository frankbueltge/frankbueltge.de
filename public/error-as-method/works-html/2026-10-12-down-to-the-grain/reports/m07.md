**What I did first with the file.** I looked at it, enlarged it and printed it out as text to see the dots. Each dot is only about 1–2 px across, and each pixel column holds about 5 hours, so many dots overlap. Only 798 of the 1,464 can still be told apart, and the exact hour-by-hour values can't be recovered.

**How I got to the form.** Matching the image against itself, shifted sideways, showed a strong repeat every 2.496 px. Over the record's width that is 12.40–12.44 h, depending on where exactly the first and last dots sit. I then folded the 798 dots on candidate periods: at 12.42 h they form one clean wave, and at 12.0 h or 24.0 h they are noise. The work uses that clock. The record is cut into 60 lines, each one lunar day (two cycles, 24 h 50 min) long, stacked top to bottom:
- **Across a line:** where each dot falls in its lunar day.
- **Colour:** the dot's height, pale sand for low water through to dark ink for high water.
- **Red:** a dot higher than any ordinary tide in the record.

Each dot is drawn as a mark about one hour wide, which roughly matches how precisely its time can be read from the JPEG. The two high waters show as dark vertical bands running down the page; they drift a little and grow stronger and weaker over time. One line near the bottom goes dark across its whole width and turns red. The original JPEG is embedded small underneath, with a short four-line caption.

**What the commissioner should know:**
- The 798 dots are what survived compression, not all 1,464 values. Gaps in the lines are real losses, not low values.
- Each dot's time is uncertain by about ±1 h.
- Where the tide cycle starts (low water at the edges, high water at ¼ and ¾) is fitted from the data. No real clock times are known.
- The colour scale, the red threshold (about 21 source pixels above the record's mean) and the extra 12.42 h step are my choices. The shapes in the picture are what the data gives.
- The page includes small text: a title, high/low labels and the caption. Delete it if the work should be wordless.

**My view of what was measured: sea level from a tide gauge, at a coast with mostly twice-daily tides.** This rests on four things in the file:
1. **The 12.42 h period.** It matches the moon's main twice-daily tide almost exactly. A sun-driven daily quantity like temperature or light would repeat every 24 or 12 hours, and would draw flat streaks rather than the slanting strands the dots make.
2. **The swelling and shrinking.** The spread of the dots grows and shrinks roughly every 14–15 days, which is the spring–neap cycle. Strong and weak spring tides seem to alternate, possibly because of the moon's distance from Earth.
3. **A single event around day 44–45.** The whole band rises, with a spike of a few hours about 1.5 times the normal tidal range above the band; one dot appears to sit right at the top edge of the image, so the peak may be cut off. The band then returns within about a day. That looks most like a storm surge. A river flood at a tidal gauge is the main alternative, and I can't rule it out.
4. **A smaller jump and drop around days 53–55**, likely also weather-driven.

I can't tell where or when this was measured, or in what units.
