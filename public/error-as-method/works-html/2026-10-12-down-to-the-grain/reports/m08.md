I made **"Fifty Minutes Later"**, a single still SVG image in `/tmp/claude-0/s122/mk/m08/work/index.html`, with the title and paragraph in `NOTE.md` beside it. The page loads nothing else: no scripts, no fonts beyond the system serif. The only "http" in the file is the SVG namespace declaration, which isn't a request. No browser was available here, so I checked it only through a Python render of the same coordinates (which leaves out the text) and an XML parse. Nobody has seen it in a real browser at 1100 × 800 yet; that is worth a quick look before it goes anywhere.

**What I did first with the file.** I looked at it, then printed its pixels as text and measured each pixel column. The dots form a band about 30 px tall, with one sharp spike near x≈220, a smaller rise near x≈263, and parallel wavy stripes running through the band. The data runs from about x=2.5 to x=296.5, not edge to edge, so each pixel column holds about 4.98 hours. I had to get that right before any timing worked: with the naive 300-pixel width, the main rhythm came out at a misleading 12.2 h.

**How I got from it to the form.**
- With the corrected time axis, I scanned the column means for periodic signals. The strongest line is at 12.44 h, and smaller lines appear at 12.00 h, about 12.68 h and about 24 h.
- I fitted harmonic tide terms (M2, S2, N2, K1, O1, M4, MS4) plus a smooth slow trend to the column means; the fit leaves about 2 px of scatter. I dropped K2 and NU2 because 61 days isn't long enough to separate them from S2 and N2.
- I rebuilt an hourly series by sharing each column's observed dark pixels among its roughly five hours, in the order the fitted model predicts.
- Re-plotted at the original size, that series reproduces the source picture closely, wavy stripes included. So the stripes are almost certainly a moiré of the tide against the pixel grid, not a real feature.
- Because 1,464 = 61 × 24, the form stacks the record as rows of 24 hours, earliest at the top. Each row is a line that hides the rows behind it. The two crests a day drift diagonally to the right, the twice-a-month swell shows as a broadening of the band, and the spike becomes one blank dome that erases the days above it.

**What the commissioner should know.**
- **The values are a reconstruction, not a transcription.** The vertical position of the dots in each column is measured. But which of the roughly five hours in a column each dot belongs to can't be seen in a 300 px JPEG; it comes from the tide model. The hourly values carry about 5 px of noise against a normal range of about 30 px.
- **The row start is my choice.** I shifted rows by 12 hours so that the spike falls mid-row rather than on a row seam. The first and last rows are therefore half-rows. The record's real clock time is unknown anyway.
- **The units are image pixels.** There is no scale; "up" means larger, as in the source.

**My view of what was measured.** Hourly sea level at a tide gauge on a coast where the twice-daily tide dominates, with one storm surge in it. The evidence:
- The main period is 12.42 h. That is the lunar M2 tide, which the 61-day record is long enough to tell apart from the solar 12.00 h.
- A secondary line sits at 12.00 h (S2), and the band's width varies in step with the beat between the two, roughly every 15 days. That is the spring–neap cycle.
- N2 and K1 are present, with the daily terms small relative to the twice-daily ones (ratio about 0.2).
- The spike, around hours 1076–1084 (about day 45), rises fast for about 5 hours, about 30 px above the predicted level, and lands close to a predicted high water. That fits a storm surge peaking at high tide. A smaller raised period comes about 9 days later.

I can't say where or when it was recorded, and the spike could in principle be another kind of event, such as a recording fault or a tsunami. But the 12.42 h line is hard to explain as anything except a lunar tide.
