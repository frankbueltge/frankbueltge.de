I made a still work called **Nineteen and a Half Hours** from the record. No browser was available here, so I never saw the page rendered. I checked the layout coordinates by hand and confirmed the script parses.

**What I did first with the file.** I opened it and printed all 75 × 23 grey values as ASCII to see what was actually there. Each pixel column has to cover about 19.5 hours (1,464 ÷ 75), so no single hour can be recovered. Each column keeps only how far its values spread, softened further by the JPEG compression. I then saved the exact grey values and worked out, for each column, its weighted centre and its top and bottom edges.

**How I got to the form.** I decided not to rebuild the hours, because any version of them would be made up. The work shows the loss itself:
- **The field:** the 75 × 23 pixels are enlarged exactly as they are, compression smudges included, into a large grid of grey cells.
- **The rulers:** under the field are 1,464 hairlines, one per hour, with longer ticks at each of the 61 days. Below that is a strip of the 75 pixel columns, and under each column the number of hours it holds (19 or 20).
- **The caption:** a two-line note in italics.

The day ticks and the pixel boundaries never line up. The page is a single SVG drawn by a short inline script, with the pixel values written into the file. It loads nothing else and is laid out for 1100 × 800. NOTE.md gives the title and one paragraph on the form.

**What you should know.**
- The detail in the image is entirely what survived the shrinking. Any finer pattern, such as a daily rhythm, cannot be seen at this size, and the work does not pretend otherwise.
- The colours are fixed as warm paper and dark ink in both light and dark mode, because this is a still image.

**What I think was measured.** I can only describe the shape of the record, not name the quantity.
- **A steady band:** most of the time the values stay in a band about 8–9 pixels thick (roughly rows 11–21 of 23), and the band barely drifts across the two months. Its thickness suggests a lot of hour-to-hour change, perhaps a daily cycle, but the image can't confirm that.
- **One rise:** a single sharp rise at columns 53–55 (about days 42–45) climbs to the very top of the image, more than twice the band's usual height, and then drops back within a couple of days.
- **Smaller dips:** shallower dips in the band appear near columns 8–9, 29–31, 42 and 64–65.

A steady fluctuating level with one short, intense event fits things like river level or flow after a storm, wind speed, or a pollutant reading during one episode. Nothing in the file picks between these, and I would not commit to any of them.
