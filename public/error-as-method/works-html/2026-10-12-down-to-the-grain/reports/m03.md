I made "Nineteen or Twenty". It is in `/tmp/claude-0/s122/mk/m03/work/index.html`, a single static SVG at 1100 × 800 that loads nothing else. `NOTE.md` is in the same folder. I had no browser to render the page, so I checked the layout with a PNG mock-up drawn from the same numbers (`scratch/preview.png`). The text lines were sized by estimate and may need a nudge once you see it in a browser.

**What I did first.** I dumped all 1,725 grey values of `record.jpg` (75 × 23) as numbers and looked at them. The key fact is that each pixel column covers 1,464 / 75 ≈ 19.5 hours. No individual hour survives. What each column still holds is a smear of ink showing the range and level of roughly 19 or 20 values. JPEG compression adds faint ringing in the white area and seems to blur the columns into blocks of about four that share the same edges, so detail finer than a few columns can't be trusted.

**How I got to the form.** I measured each column's band: its top and bottom edge, its width, and the centre of its ink (`scratch/prof.py`). Then I made the loss itself the subject. The 75 × 23 grains are enlarged as a faint grid. Onto it I put back exactly 1,464 dots, one per hour. Each dot goes into the column its hour fell into: 36 columns get 19 hours and 39 get 20. Within a column, the dots are spread up and down in proportion to the ink that column kept. A strip below ticks every hour inside its column, and under that are 61 day marks, with a longer mark every seventh day.

So all the measurements are present, but their order within each column is gone, and the time between them has turned into height. Nothing in the dot positions is random or invented beyond that ink-proportional spreading. The generator is `scratch/make.py`.

**What you should know.**
- Any finer reading of this file, including mine below, sits at the limit of what a 75-pixel JPEG can show.
- The work deliberately does not pretend to recover the hourly series; it can't be recovered from this file.

**My view of what was measured (tentative).** I think it is most likely a water level from a tide gauge on a sea with small tides, and that the spike is a storm surge. It rests on three things:
1. **A steady band.** Most of the record is a fairly level band about 6–11 pixels tall. That fits a quantity that swings within each day around a level that drifts slowly.
2. **A two-week rhythm in the band's width.**
   - Wide around days 4, 31 and 59, with weaker widening around days 15–18.
   - Narrow around days 9, 22–24, 38–39 and 50–52.
   - The spacing is about 14 days, with strong and weak wide phases taking turns. That matches the spring–neap cycle of tides, with its monthly unevenness.
3. **One sharp event.** Around days 42–45 a single spike rises to roughly four times the band's usual height above its centre. It climbs over about 40 hours and falls back in about 20.
   - A storm surge on a coast with a small tidal range (Baltic-like) would look like this.
   - Air pollution would be a plausible alternative, for example a fireworks spike. That would fit if the record spans New Year, but the file contains no dates to check.

The two-week rhythm is the main reason I favour tides over weather, though it is right at the edge of what the image can resolve.
