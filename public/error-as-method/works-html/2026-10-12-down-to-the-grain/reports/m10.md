I made "Fifty-nine Lunar Days": one still image that reads the record as a tide, cut into lunar days and stacked. It's in `/tmp/claude-0/s122/mk/m10/work/index.html`, with the note in `NOTE.md` beside it. The page loads nothing from outside, and a headless browser at 1100 × 800 rendered it with no errors and no requests to any other file. All my scripts and intermediate files are in `/tmp/claude-0/s122/mk/m10/scratch`.

**What I did first.** I looked at the picture, then printed every pixel as text and measured each pixel column. The scale sets the limit: 1,464 hours across about 294 px is roughly 5 hours per column. Most dots overlap, so I found only 645 separate dots for 1,464 values. Two things stood out:
- **A 5 px rhythm.** The average height of the dots in each column flips almost exactly every 5 px, and the rhythm shifts slightly near the spike.
- **A slower swell.** The vertical spread of the dots grows and shrinks about every 70 px.

**How I got to the form.** Those two patterns looked like a tide that twists and narrows as it repeats. I fitted a tidal model directly to the dark pixels: the usual tidal periods plus a slowly varying mean level.
- **Testing the reading.** I re-ran the same fit with the main period set to 11.5, 12.0, 12.2, 12.42, 12.66, 12.9, 24 and 24.84 hours. 12.42 h fitted best by a clear margin (score 0.337, against 0.278 or less for every other period).
- **What the fit found.** The 12.42 h term is about 2.8 times the 12 h term, the usual ratio for tides. The fitted strength peaks around days 2, 16, 31, 45 and 60, alternating stronger and weaker, which is the spring–neap cycle.
- **Check.** When I redrew the fitted hours as dots, they reproduced the banded texture of the original.
- **The spike.** It was too sharp for the model, so I read those five hours straight from the dots.

From this I built an estimated hourly series and folded it every 24 h 50 min. Each of the 59 overlapping lines holds two high waters, and the high waters line up down the page. You can read the spring–neap swelling, the drift in mean level and the one red high water without any explanation. The source sits beside it pixel for pixel at actual size, as a reminder of how little there was to read from.

**What you should know.**
- **The hourly values are an estimate, not a decoding.** Within each five-hour column the order of the dots can't be read from the image. The model decides it, nudged at most 1.5 px toward the nearest dot.
- **There is no per-hour "seen" flag.** I tried marking which hours matched an actual dot, but the dots are so dense that 99.9% matched something, so the flag meant nothing and I dropped it.
- **Units are the picture's own pixel heights.** The plot has no scale, so there are no real units.
- **The spike is the least certain part.** Its five values come straight from five dots, but which hour each belongs to is my guess.
- **The page has words on it:** a title, one caption, and labels for high water, low water, springs and neaps.

**My view of what was measured.** Water level from a tide gauge, hourly, over two months. It rests on:
- the dominant period of 12.42 h, which is the moon's half-day, not the clock's 12 h;
- the 12 h term at about a third of its strength, as the sun's is in real tides;
- the 14.8-day spring–neap swell, alternating strong and weak (the moon's changing distance would do that);
- one isolated, very high peak at hour 1,082 of 1,464, near a weaker spring tide.

I read that peak as a storm surge meeting a high tide, or perhaps a river flood or a short seiche. It rises about as far above a normal high water as the whole normal tidal range, and lasts about five hours. Afterwards the mean level stays lower for several days. A gauge glitch can't be ruled out, but the values step up to the peak and back down again, which looks physical. I can't tell where or when it was recorded.
