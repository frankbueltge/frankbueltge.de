I made **Held to the Moon**: a single still image that cuts the hourly record into 60 lengths of one lunar day (24 h 50 min) and stacks them down the page as ridgelines. I did not publish it as an Artifact; it exists only as the two files in the folder.

**What I did first with the file.** I looked at the picture, then pulled out the dots. Each dot is about 2×2 pixels, and successive hours are only about 0.80 px apart, so neighbouring dots overlap.
- I found 1,439 dot centres. Where dots had merged into one blob, I split them by size.
- I matched them to the 1,464 hour slots in left-to-right order, which left 25 hours unaccounted for. I filled those by straight-line interpolation between their neighbours.
- In a few places, dots less than about 1.7 px apart had been read in an order that made a one-hour zigzag. I swapped them into the smoother order (41 swaps). The picture can't settle the order of those hours, so this is a judgement call on my part.

**How I got to the form.** Checking how the series repeats over time showed its strongest period is 12.42 hours, not 24. A fit using standard tidal periods gives a dominant 12.42 h component (the moon's half-day tide, M2), with smaller ones at 12.66 h, 12.00 h and about 24–26 h. The strength of the swing goes up and down over roughly 28–29 days. Since the record keeps time with the moon rather than the sun, I cut it on the moon's clock.
- Each line is drawn through the actual hourly values, with one small dot per hour, like the original. The 25 filled-in hours are shown as open dots.
- Each line hides the ones behind it, like a range of hills. The two daily highs line up into two straight vertical ridges that swell and thin with the monthly cycle.
- Near the bottom, one high tide climbs over the rows above it.
- The page is one self-contained `index.html` (data embedded, canvas, no outside requests) sized for 1100 × 800. I rendered it in a headless browser: no errors, no network requests.
- The only text on the page is the title, one line of explanation, and "first hour" / "last hour" in the margin.

**Things you should know.**
- The values are pixel heights read back from a compressed picture, not the original numbers. Their units and true scale are unknown.
- The order of neighbouring hours is uncertain where dots overlap, and 25 hours are filled in rather than read.
- A fit using only the tidal periods explains about 73% of the variation. The rest is a mix of real non-tidal movement and error from reading the picture.
- The phrase "lunar day" on the page states my interpretation; the brief didn't supply it.

**My view of what was measured.** I think it is hourly water level at a coastal tide gauge, over two months. That rests on:
- the dominant 12.42 h period, which is the moon's half-day tide period and doesn't occur in daily or human rhythms;
- the 12.66 h component being stronger than the 12.00 h one, which gives the roughly 27.5-day swell in tidal range visible in the record (strongest around days 3, 32 and 60);
- one event near day 45. Over about two days the level runs higher than the tide alone predicts, by an extra amount that keeps growing. It peaks at about 188 px above the tidal prediction, coinciding with a high tide (about 4× the main tide's amplitude), then falls away within a day and dips slightly below. That shape looks like a storm surge.

A smaller rise of the same kind shows around days 53–54. The record could also be a tidal river stage or a coastal well, but open-coast sea level fits best. I don't know the place, the dates or the units.
