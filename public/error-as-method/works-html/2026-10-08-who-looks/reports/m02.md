**The form:** each of the 568 meteorites is a disc drawn at one scale with the same density for all, so its size follows the cube root of its mass. The discs are dropped one at a time into a trench in order of recorded year. Each one settles in the lowest spot that nothing above it covers, so small ones can't slip into gaps under the pile. That builds real layers:
- **Bottom:** 1576 to 1849, with the 50 t Campo del Cielo.
- **Middle:** the 20th century, including the 60 t Hoba.
- **Near the top:** thin, crowded layers for 2000–2024, with Gancedo (31 t) and El Ali (15 t) lying on the surface.
- **Very top:** the 38 meteorites with no year, drawn as hollow outlines.

**How it reads:**
- Darker means earlier.
- Rust means the record gives a place (106 meteorites); grey means it doesn't.
- The nine largest stones are labelled with name, mass and year.
- Labels on the left wall mark where each period sits.
- A key, a short caption and a source line are included.

**Things to know:**
- Meteorites under about 12 g would be smaller than a pixel at true scale. They are drawn as visible specks instead, and the caption says so.
- Some recorded places are where the meteorite is kept, not where it fell. Agpalilik's coordinates are Copenhagen. That is why the key says "a place is recorded" rather than "fall site known".
- The layout is worked out when the page loads. In Node it took about 1.6 seconds, so the image may appear after a short pause.
- Two facts in the note are checked against the data: the five heaviest meteorites hold 81% of the total mass, and 194 weigh under 100 g.
