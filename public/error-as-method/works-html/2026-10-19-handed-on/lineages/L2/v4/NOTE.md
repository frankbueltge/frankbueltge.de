# The Long Wait

Every laureate becomes one horizontal thread running from the year of birth (or founding) to the year of the prize, stacked in award order from 1901 at the top to 2026 at the bottom. The prize years make a hard staircase down the right edge, while the births fray loosely to the left, so each thread's length is that person's age at the prize. Read down the page and the fabric gets wider: the wait is visibly growing, from an average of 55 years in the early decades to 67 now. I chose lifelines over a chart of ages because they keep both dates, show the Second World War as a gap in the weave, and let the few red threads of women and the dotted blue ones of organisations (one of them reaching back to 1647) show where they sit.

## v2

**(a)** The thread, the age scale and the side panel of average ages converge into one structure: every thread is now hung from its prize, all right ends on one vertical edge, so a thread's left end *is* the laureate's age and the ragged left contour of the fabric *is* the age distribution, with its running median drawn through it.
**(b)** The diagonal staircase of prize years, which wasted half the page and made lengths incomparable, is turned upright; the diagonal survives as the means for keeping the calendar: lines of equal birth year now cross the fabric obliquely, so both dates stay readable.
**(c)** Struck: the calendar x-axis, the constant-age diagonals, and the separate bar panel of averages (its numbers now sit on the median line inside the fabric).

I shear the picture so that the prize, not the calendar, is the common origin: the growing wait becomes a left edge that visibly recedes as you read down, instead of a distance one has to estimate between two drifting edges, and the averages no longer need a chart of their own.

## v3

**(a)** Thread, age distribution and median converge into one structure: inside each decade the threads are re-sorted by age, oldest on top, so every decade's left edge becomes that decade's sorted ages (its quantile curve), and the median is no longer a line computed and laid over the fabric but simply the thread in the middle row of each band, marked where it ends.
**(b)** The overplotting, which turned a thousand threads into an undifferentiated grey mass with a noisy edge, becomes the means: once sorted, the threads fuse on purpose into solid wedges whose outline is the measurement, and the height of each wedge is the number of laureates that decade (rows, not calendar years, are now the unit of height).
**(c)** Struck: the award-year order within a decade, the year-proportional vertical axis with its 1940–42 gap, the overlaid running-median line, and the birth-year diagonals (no longer exact once rows are sorted by age).

I sort the threads by age within each decade so that the ragged noise of v2 resolves into ten-odd shapes that can be compared directly: where each decade's oldest, middle and youngest laureates stand, and how the whole wedge slides left as the wait grows.

## v4

**(a)** Thread thickness, the prize's share and the height of each decade converge into one structure: every thread is now as thick as the laureate's share of the prize, so a decade's band is exactly as tall as the number of prizes it awarded (45, 36 in the war-torn 1910s, 32 in the 1940s, then a plateau of 60 once six prizes a year run), and the wedge's outline is the age distribution of *prizes*, not of names; the median is the thread crossing the band's middle height.
**(b)** The sharing of prizes, which in v3 inflated the late decades into tall bands (123 laureates in the 2000s against 57 in the 1900s) and made the wedges incomparable in size, becomes the means: split prizes now show as a finer weave of thinner threads inside bands of equal height, so the crowding of the podium is visible as texture while the wedges become directly comparable shapes.
**(c)** Struck: the laureate count as the unit of height, the decade bracket, and the floating name annotations except those that mark the extremes.

I let each laureate weigh what their share of the prize weighs, so the page measures prizes rather than people: decades become equal blocks wherever the Nobel calendar was full, and the shift of the wedges to the left reads as a change in when prizes are given, not as a side-effect of more people sharing them.
