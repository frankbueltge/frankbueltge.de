# The Long Wait

Every Nobel laureate from 1901 to 2026 is one horizontal line, running from the year they were born (or, for organisations, founded) to the year they were honoured; rows descend by prize year, so all the right-hand ends fall on a single diagonal, the moment of the prize, while the left-hand ends fray back into the past. I chose this form because it turns a table of dates into lifetimes laid side by side, and the shape itself carries the finding: the diagonal advances a year per year, but the births lag ever further behind it, so the band widens as the median age at award drifts from the mid-fifties to near seventy. Women are drawn in red and organisations in blue against the grey mass of men, so how rare they are, and how late they thicken, can be seen without counting.

## v2

The form stays; what changes is that the ages can now be read. v1 claims the band widens as laureates get older, but the only way to read an age was the brown median curve and two numbers at its ends, which sat on top of the lines and were hard to read, as were several other labels (the Red Cross, the Friends Service Council, John B. Goodenough). I add faint dotted diagonals of equal age (20, 40, 60, 80 and 100 years at the prize), parallel to the prize diagonal and labelled along the top, so the left end of any line, and the drift of the median curve across them from 57 in the 1900s to 71 in the 2020s (11-year windows), can be read directly; the curve is labelled at three points instead of two. Every label now gets a halo in the paper colour so it stays legible over the lines; the Red Cross is labelled at all three of its prizes and Marie Curie at both of hers, since these repeats are what the rows otherwise hide; the curve's legend entry is renamed to what it shows, the median age at award.

## v3

The form and v2's reading aids stay; what changes is where the ages can be read. v2's equal-age diagonals are labelled only along the top edge, so in the lower two thirds, where most laureates are and where the band has widened most, a reader has to trace a dotted line up across hundreds of lines to learn whether it means 60 or 80. I repeat each diagonal's age as a small label written along the line itself, with the paper-coloured halo, at two points further down (once in the middle of the century, once near its end, shifted where the median curve or another label is in the way), so any left-hand end can be read against its nearest diagonal where it sits. The Friends Service Council label is also reworded to match the Red Cross's ("founded 1647: prize 1947, at 300"), since "(1947)" did not say what the bracketed year was.

## v4

The form and the reading aids stay; what changes is how much ink each laureate gets. Each year's row is only about five pixels tall but holds up to fifteen laureates, so in v3 the lines (0.7 px for men, 1.1 px for women and organisations, women drawn last and almost opaque) lay on top of one another, and every red or blue line covered two or three grey ones: the lower right of the picture read as heavily red although only 70 of 1,036 awards went to women. I now give every laureate an equal, non-overlapping slice of its year's row, drawn opaque, so the area of red, blue and grey is in proportion to the count and the late, slow arrival of women is shown at its true size rather than enlarged; men are drawn in a lighter warm grey so the slices stay distinguishable. I also move the mid-century "age 60" label off the median curve, where it sat.
