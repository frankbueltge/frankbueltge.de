# m10 (arm I) — hand-back report, verbatim

I made "838 Springs", a single still image in `/tmp/claude-0/s119/mk/m10/work/index.html`. The note is in `/tmp/claude-0/s119/mk/m10/work/NOTE.md`. I never managed to render the page, so nobody has seen it yet; it needs one look in a browser at 1100 × 800.

**What I did first with the file:** I looked at `bloom.png`. It is a plain scatter plot of year against day of year, 1100 × 500 pixels. The picture was the only source, so I set out to recover the numbers from it.

**From the picture to the form:**
- **Extraction.** No imaging libraries were installed, so I decoded the PNG with Python's built-in zlib. I set the scale from the tick marks and gridlines: 0.82 px per year and 8.95 px per day. The dots are about 4.4 px wide, so in crowded centuries neighbouring years overlap. To handle that I modelled each dot as an antialiased disc and kept the set of (year, day) points, at most one per year, that best reproduces the pixels.
  - 820 dots are directly explained by the picture.
  - The other 18 needed to reach 838 are completely hidden under neighbours. I placed them where they fit the pixels best, but their exact years are guesses.
  - The result looks right: 2023 comes out at day 84 (March 25) and 2021 at day 85, the known record-early years, and the latest year is 1323 at day 124.
- **The form.** There are thirteen columns, one per century (800s to 2000s), with years running top to bottom.
  - Each year is a thin line from a hairline at April 15 (the median of 812–1850) to a pink mark on its bloom day. Years with no record are left blank.
  - A small mark under each column shows that century's median. These sit at roughly day 101–107 until the 1900s, and at 95 for 2000–2026.
  - The last column, only 27 years long, leans clearly toward March. One note marks 2023, and there is a short key and credit line.
- **The page.** It is static inline SVG with no script and no external resources. The data is built into the drawing itself. The SVG is valid and every element sits inside 1100 × 800, but there was no browser or SVG renderer here.

**Things you should know:**
- **The values are read off the chart, not taken from the source dataset.** Most should be exactly right, since days snap to whole numbers. But in dense stretches, mainly 1400–1950, a few years may be shifted by a year or so, and the 18 hidden points are guesses: 1176, 1461, 1467, 1506, 1530, 1643, 1652, 1667, 1742, 1752, 1770, 1844, 1887, 1897, 1915, 1928, 1932 and 1993.
- **It probably gaps in years that are really recorded.** My reconstruction leaves 1895, 1919, 1921, 1945, 1949 and 1974 empty. Those are likely real years whose points I placed elsewhere. If you have the original table, swapping it in is easy.
- **Day numbers are turned into dates as if every year had 365 days.** So "April 15" is day 105 and can be a day off in leap years.
