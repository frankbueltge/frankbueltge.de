# m04 (arm T) — hand-back report, verbatim

I've made a still image called "Twelve Centuries of Spring", laid out like a hanging scroll: years run down the page from 812 to 2026, and the day of full bloom runs across it, with each recorded year drawn as one petal. I couldn't look at it rendered, because there's no browser here and you asked me not to write outside the folder. So the spacing of the labels near the bottom is unchecked. I did run the drawing code in Node with a stand-in for the page, and it ran without errors and drew 902 elements.

**What I did first with the file:** I read `bloom.csv` and summarised it before designing anything:
- It has 838 rows with two columns, `year` and `day_of_year`, ranging from day 84 to day 124.
- Records are thin early on (14 years in the 800s, 31 in the 900s) and close to complete from 1400 onward. The longest gaps are 20 years, in the 800s.
- The average date was roughly flat, around day 101 to 107 per century, until the 1900s (day 101) and the 2000s (day 94).
- The earliest bloom in the whole record is 2023 (day 84, 25 March), and the second earliest is 2021. The latest is 1323 (day 124, 4 May).

**How I got to the form:** The data is a chronicle that reads top to bottom with one moment of blossom per year, so I gave each year a single petal. A petal is a small pink ellipse, turned to a different angle for each year so the whole looks like falling blossom.
- Missing years stay blank paper. I thought the gaps in the record were worth showing rather than smoothing over.
- One thin ink line follows the average date across the 50 years either side of each year. It is drawn only where at least 12 of those years are recorded, and breaks where fewer are.
- Petals from 1990 onward are a deeper pink. Two small labels mark the earliest bloom (2023) and the latest (1323).
- The left column holds the title, a short explanation of how to read it, and the credit to Aono and Katata via Our World in Data (CC BY 4.0).

**What you should know:**
- **Self-contained:** the page draws inline SVG with plain JavaScript and has all 838 data points written into it. It loads no other file, library or font, and uses the system's serif typeface.
- **Fixed size:** it is built for exactly 1100 × 800 and won't adapt to other screen sizes.
- **Text in the image:** it carries a fair amount of explanation. If you want a purer image, the explanatory lines in the left column can come out and the form still works.
