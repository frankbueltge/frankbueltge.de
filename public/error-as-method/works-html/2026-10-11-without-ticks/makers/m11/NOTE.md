# Not on a Sunday

The work lays all 5,479 days out as a fifteen-row calendar, one thin column per day, darker for more births. Read that way, the count looks less like a natural rhythm and more like a set of appointments. Weekends show up as pale stripes that move one day later each year, and Christmas, New Year's Day and the Fourth of July are pale columns that run straight down through every year. Below it, the same 366 calendar days are shown again with the weekday and month effects removed. What is left is mostly human scheduling: deep red gaps where deliveries were kept away (Christmas −41%, Halloween, Thanksgiving, every 13th of the month) and a ridge in the last working days of December. Five ringed cells pick out the dates that repeat a number. 9.9.09 was the busiest day of the fifteen years, 10.10.10 was full even though it fell on a Sunday, and 6.6.06 was avoided. I chose a calendar and not a timeline because the story is the calendar itself: the dates people choose and avoid when a birth can be booked.
