I made **"The Day Takes"**: the 61 days of the record stacked as 61 black bands, one per day, with each band as thick as the value at that hour. I couldn't open the page in a browser because there isn't one here. I checked the script parses and previewed the layout with a Python copy of the drawing code.

**What I did first with the file.** I looked at the picture and then read its pixels. The dots run from x=2 to x=297 and y=5 to y=84, about 4.9 values per pixel column. The dots are only 1–2 px and the JPEG is noisy, so each hour's value has to be rebuilt rather than read off directly.

**How I got from it to the form.**
- **Rebuilding the hours:** I placed the 1,464 hours evenly across x. A first best-path trace through the dots was too smooth and skipped the troughs (it came near only 44–58% of the dark pixels). So I refined it with a numpy fit that draws the dots and moves them until the drawing matches the image, which raised that to 68%. On days where the fitted trough still stopped more than 3 px above the darkest dot in the image, I stretched that day's dip down to it.
- **The cycle:** the spectrum peaks at 24.0 h, with a harmonic at 12 h. On average each day is high and flat for about 15 hours, then drops sharply for about 8–9 hours.
- **First form, dropped:** 61 overlapping curves tangled into noise.
- **The form I kept:** thickness bands. The daily dips line up as a pale clearing down the middle of the plate. The days with no dip (4, 13 and roughly 40–43) are solid dark rows. Thin labels mark the days and hours, and the uncut record runs along the foot of the page.

**What the commissioner should know.**
- Single hourly values are approximate, to within a few pixels of the source plot. A handful of troughs had to be corrected by hand as described above. The daily cycle, the days without a dip and the multi-week drift are solid.
- Thickness starts at the bottom edge of the source plot, not at a true zero, so the bands show relative values only. No units or scale are known.
- "First hour of the record" is the record's own clock, not local midnight.
- The page is a single canvas with the data inlined (about 11 KB) and loads nothing else. It is sized for 1100 × 800.

**My view of what was measured.** This is a guess. I think it is something the sun draws down each day and that recovers overnight and on wet or overcast days. My best candidate is near-ground relative humidity. Close alternatives are tree-stem diameter or a stream or groundwater level affected by evaporation. It rests on these things in the file:
- The period is exactly 24.0 h, not tidal. Tides would show about 12.42 or 24.84 h.
- The daily shape looks like air temperature turned upside down: a long flat high and a short sharp dip.
- The days when the dip almost vanishes (daily range 4–17 px against the usual 40–50) are the days the whole level jumps up and gets near the top of the plot. That suggests a common ceiling, like saturation.
- After each jump, the overnight level falls for weeks while the daily lows keep hitting about the same floor (about 7–10 px from the bottom). That looks like a drying spell between rain events.
