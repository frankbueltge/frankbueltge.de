The work is **Sixty-one Nights**, in `/tmp/claude-0/s131/mk/m07/work/index.html`, with its note in `/tmp/claude-0/s131/mk/m07/work/NOTE.md`. It is a single static SVG of 1,464 dots, one per hour, with the data written into the page. It loads nothing; the only URL in it is the SVG namespace. There was no browser here, so I checked the layout with a Python preview drawn from the same geometry, not by viewing the HTML itself.

**What I did first.** I looked at the image, enlarged it, then read the dark pixels column by column. The dots run from x=2 to x=296, so one day is about 4.84 px wide. A Fourier scan peaks at exactly 4.84 px, which confirms a 24-hour cycle: one tooth per day, 61 teeth.

**From the file to the form.**
- **Rebuilding the values.** I placed hour i at x = 2 + i·294/1463. In each pixel column I split the dark pixels into as many groups as there are hours in that column (4–5), weighted by darkness. Within a column I ordered the values rising or falling, depending on the columns either side.
- **Finding the daily phase.** Folding the 1,464 values into a 24-hour profile puts the daily low at index 15 and the high around index 4.
- **Drafts.** I tried a stacked-ridge drawing (one line per day, later days in front) first. It failed because the days with a high level all day hid everything behind them.
- **The final form.** It is a halftone. Each of 62 columns is one day, left to right as in the source, read downward from one daily low to the next. The first and last columns are partial. A dot's radius grows linearly with the value. The nights form a dark band through the middle, a few days are dark from top to bottom, and each of those fades out over the following weeks.

**What the commissioner should know.**
- **Hour order within a day is partly inferred.** The JPEG holds only about 5 pixel columns per day. Each dot's level is read from the image, but its exact hour within each ~5-hour column is my reconstruction.
- **Values are relative.** The source has no axis, so dot sizes run from the lowest hour in the record to the highest, at roughly 1/80 of that range per step. There are no ppm figures behind them.
- **The captions assume the phase.** The page text says "afternoon low" and "nights". That reading of the clock is my inference from the shape (see below), not something the file states.
- **Sizing choice.** Dot radius rather than area grows with the value. This was for contrast, and it exaggerates differences somewhat.

**What I think was measured.** I think this is a station on land with plants growing nearby, during the growing season, near the ground or a low inlet. This rests on four things in the file:
- **Size of the daily swing.** It covers most of the plot's height every day. A remote background or marine station would show a nearly flat line.
- **Shape of the day.** There is one sharp low per day, and the swing is lopsided: a quick climb, a long high plateau, then a quick fall. That fits plants respiring into a shallow night-time layer of air, followed by photosynthesis and daytime mixing in the afternoon.
- **The jumps.** At about days 4–5, 8, 13, 40–43 and 59 the whole level jumps and the afternoon dip almost vanishes. Days 40–43 never fall below about 0.6–0.9 of the range. Each jump is followed by a slow drain over one to two weeks, while the daily swing returns. That looks like weather: a stagnant or cloudy spell, or a change of air mass, rather than a seasonal trend or an instrument fault. I can't rule the instrument out from the file alone.
- **Day zero (conjecture).** If the low falls at about 15:00 local time, index 0 would be local midnight.
