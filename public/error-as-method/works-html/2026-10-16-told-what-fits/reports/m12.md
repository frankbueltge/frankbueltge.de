**What I did first with the file**
I looked at it, enlarged it, and turned it into an array of how dark each pixel is. The plot fills columns 2–297 and rows 4–85.

The first thing I learned limits everything after it: each pixel column holds about 4.9 hours of dots, and the JPEG blurs them together. That means the 1,464 hourly values cannot be put back in their real order. Within a column you can only see the spread of levels, not which hour came first.

To check the daily rhythm, I ran a frequency analysis on the upper edge of the dots. It has a sharp peak at 61.35 cycles across the plot, almost exactly one per day. So each "tooth" in the sawtooth is one day.

**How I got to the form**
- **Cutting days:** I cut the plot into 61 equal slices, one per day, placed so each slice is centred on that day's peak. The day boundaries are therefore my estimate, not clock midnight.
- **Turning each day into 24 hours:** for each slice I added up the dot darkness at each height. I then took 24 evenly spaced points from that spread of levels, which gives 24 estimated hourly levels per day and 1,464 dots in total.
- **Drawing:** in the work, each day is a column of its 24 dots sorted by level, with a fine line from the day's highest to lowest hour. Dots at about the same level push each other sideways. So each day widens where the air stayed a long time and thins where it changed fast.
- **Source and caption:** the original plot is reproduced underneath at its own size, next to a short caption.

**What the commissioner should know**
- The values are only estimates of position on the plot, about one pixel accurate at best. They are not CO2 amounts: there are no units, and no ppm values can be recovered.
- The order of hours within each day is deliberately left out, because the file does not hold it.
- I could not open the page in a browser, because none was available. I checked it with a Python drawing that copies the same layout, and checked the script for syntax errors with Node. The text block was not included in that copy.

**My view of what was measured, and what it rests on**
I think this is CO2 near the ground at a site with plants and soil, but that is my interpretation, not something the file proves.
- **A strong one-day cycle:** this is what you would expect from plants taking in CO2 by day and breathing it out at night, plus the night-time air near the ground staying still and trapping it.
- **The daily swing is large compared with the overall range:** it often spans most of the plot's height. That fits a land site with vegetation more than a clean remote station.
- **A longer pattern of a few weeks on top:** there are sudden jumps to a higher level, near days 4, 13, 24 and 40, and the one near day 40 is the biggest. Right after each jump the daily swing is small. Then, over one to two weeks, the top level slowly falls while the daily lows sink deeper.

That pattern looks like weather to me: a new air mass arriving, perhaps under cloudy or windy conditions that hold back the daily swing, then clearer days with a growing daytime drop. I can't confirm the season, the location, or whether low values really mean daytime.
