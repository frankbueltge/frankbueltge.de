I made a still image called **The Drying**: the 61 days stacked as 61 lines, one per day, each running through 24 hours from left to right. It is one self-contained `index.html` with a static inline SVG and no scripts. It was never rendered in a browser, because no browser or SVG renderer was available. I checked the layout only with rough line-drawing previews made in Python, which leave out the text and the smoothed curves.

**What I did first with the file.** I looked at the picture, then turned it into numbers. The values form a saw-tooth with a fine vertical comb on top. Measuring the comb gave a period of about 4.83 pixels. At about 4.96 hours per pixel, that is a cycle of about 23.9 hours, so it is a daily rhythm. I then listed each day's high and low.

**How I got to the form.**
- **Rebuilding the hourly series:** I mapped the 1,464 hours onto the inked columns. In each column I spread that column's values over its ink. Whether they run up or down depends on whether the line is rising or falling there. I then smoothed the result lightly.
- **Checking it:** cut into 24-hour rows, the series makes sense. The daily low falls in the middle of every row, at the same place throughout. A few days are almost flat and high.
- **The form:** each day is drawn as a ridge that hides the rows above it, so a high day rises and covers the days before it. The daily low runs as a valley down the middle of the page and gets deeper through each dry spell. The flat days lie across the stack like sheets.
- **Choices:** the first row starts at the first value in the record. The clock time is unknown, so I added no hour labels. The only words on the page are a small title and one italic caption. The last row is one hour shorter than the others because the record ends there.

**What you should know.**
- **The values are estimates.** They are rebuilt from a 300 × 90 JPEG, and each pixel column holds about five values, so the order of the hours within a column is inferred. The overall level, the timing of the daily low and the jumps are reliable. The detail within each hour is not.
- **The scale is relative.** No units or axis were given, so the values are scaled from 0 to 1.

**My view of what was measured** (a guess, not confirmed):
- **What it rests on:**
  - The rhythm repeats every 24 hours, not the 24.8 hours of the tides.
  - Each day is mostly high, with one deep low partway through.
  - Rises are sudden and irregular, on about days 4, 8, 13, 40, 48 and 59, with no weekly pattern. That points to weather, not human activity.
  - On the days just after a rise, the daily swing nearly disappears and the values stay near the top. Day 40 is the highest and flattest of all.
  - Over the following days both the daily high and the daily low slowly fall, and the daily low gets deeper.
  - The daily swing is larger than the jumps.
- **My best guess:** outdoor relative humidity. It is high at night and drops in the afternoon, it stays near saturation on rainy or foggy days, and the air dries between fronts.
- **Next most likely:** a shallow water table or a small stream. Plant water use draws these down during the day and rain raises them suddenly. I think this is less likely because here the daily swing is larger than the jumps, and in those records it is usually the other way round.
