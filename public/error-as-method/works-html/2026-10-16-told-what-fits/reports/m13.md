I made "Dew Line": 61 days stacked as ridges, first day at the back, with 24 hours across each and the value as height. Each ridge is opaque, so it hides the days behind it. It's a single static SVG written straight into the page: no script, no external resources (the only URL in it is the SVG namespace). I couldn't open it in a real browser here (there wasn't one, and ImageMagick couldn't draw SVG), so I checked it with a small PIL script that redraws the same curves. That check leaves out the two text lines.

**What I did first.** I looked at the picture, then printed it as a character grid of dark pixels. That showed a repeating pattern about 4.9 px wide, which is 300 px ÷ 61 days, so one repeat per day. Each repeat is a tight cluster of dots near the top (many hours at almost the same value), then a quick fall, a few hours at a low, and a climb back.

**How I got to the form.**
- **Rebuilding the hours.** Each pixel column covers about 4.8 hours. For each column I gave it exactly the number of hours its x-span holds, read their values off as quantiles of the column's ink, and put them in order (rising or falling) from whether the neighbouring columns were higher or lower.
- **Checking the rhythm.** The rebuilt series has a clear 24-hour period (autocorrelation peaks at lag 24, at 0.82). On average each day stays high for about 6 hours, falls over about 6, sits low for about 3, and takes about 9 to climb back.
- **Trying forms.** My first two attempts hung each day's shortfall below a line. Both merged into an even grey with no structure, so I dropped them. The upward ridgeline with occlusion is what made the structure readable: the days that barely dip become flat shelves hiding the days behind them, and between shelves the afternoon valley gets deeper day by day.

**What the person who commissioned this should know.**
- The hourly values are reconstructed from a 300 × 90 JPEG, not read from the original data, and I smoothed them lightly. Each day's shape and the day-to-day changes are reliable; the exact value of any single hour, and the order of hours within one pixel column, are not.
- Where each row starts in the day is arbitrary: I cut at the first value. The record's start time is unknown, so the hours across the page are not clock hours. The page's caption says only "24 hours across".
- The vertical scale runs from the lowest to the highest value in the record. There are no units.

**My view of what was measured (moderate confidence): hourly relative humidity at an outdoor site.** It rests on four things in the file:
- **The daily shape** is the mirror image of an ordinary temperature day. Air temperature rises fast in the morning and cools slowly through the evening and night; this record falls fast and recovers slowly, with a long, flat high stretch where night would be. A flat top is what humidity does when it nears saturation and dew forms.
- **A ceiling.** About eight separate episodes push up to nearly the same top value and almost lose their daily swing (around days 4–5, 8, 13, 24, 40–44 and 59–61). That looks like humid or rainy spells pressed against a hard limit.
- **The recovery after each episode.** The daily lows fall day by day, and the swing widens, over a week or more, like ground and air drying out after rain.
- **The sizes.** The daily swing is about half the whole range or more, and on dry days the lows reach close to the record's bottom.

The one alternative I can't rule out from the picture alone is a quantity that moves opposite to air temperature.
