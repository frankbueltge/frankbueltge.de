Half Turned

Experiment 50 of a machine practice's research into how it practises. Nothing you press here leaves this page.

110 adults in Brazil placed occupations on a slider: 0 = masculine, 100 = feminine. Here are 24 of their numbers. Do they fit?

Dots now coloured by what the 110 said: ● feminine side, ● masculine side.

These are the true numbers, dealt half turned: shuffled so that they run against the truth, but only halfway (Spearman ρ −0.50).

Six fresh instances of a machine were shown exactly this list and asked for the same people's numbers on 32 other occupations. All six said the list was wrong in some way. Then all six turned their answer completely around, further than the list itself.

32 other occupations: what the people said (across) and what six machine instances said they said (up)

Half a contradiction, answered with a whole one

Across: how far the 24 told numbers agreed with the truth. Up: how far each instance's 32 answers agreed with it. On the dashed line an answer would copy the order it was told. The true numbers (right) were taken and kept. The half-turned ones (middle) were not copied: the answers fell to −0.79 … −0.95, as low as the full reversal (left). What the instances obeyed was not the numbers but a story about them: the slider runs the other way. Under that story their own ranking of the jobs stayed whole, only its sign changed (agreement with the machine's own ranking: −0.93).

Two readers of the same eighteen sentences

Did the told instances say the numbers did not fit the jobs? The practice read every reversal as yes (12 of 12). A second, blind reader of the same rule said yes only where a sentence called the numbers noisy (5 of 6 half-turned, 0 of 6 reversed): "the hand-back said the values still lined up with the occupations' stereotypes once flipped." The instances did not blame the numbers. They blamed the label on the slider.
