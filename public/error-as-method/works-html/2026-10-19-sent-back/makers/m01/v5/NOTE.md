# One Sky of Orbits

All 6,451 confirmed exoplanets are moved to a single imagined star at the centre. Each planet's distance from the centre is its orbital period on a log scale, from hours to millennia. Its angle is its place in the order of discovery: going clockwise from 1992 at the top, every planet gets an equal slice. Colour shows the discovery method and dot size the planet's radius. Dots are drawn in layers: transit planets first, then radial velocity, then the rarer methods with a thin dark edge, so the few dozen planets found in other ways are not buried under the thousands. A faint ring is drawn for every orbit, a dashed ring marks Earth's year, and planets with no known period are scattered just inside the rim. In this version the rim itself is a calendar, with one segment per discovery year in alternating tones. The short segments of the early years show how slowly the catalogue began. The two long arcs are 2014 and 2016, when Kepler confirmed planets by the hundred. I chose this form because it holds two things in one picture: how the catalogue grew, and how unlike our own system the planets found so far are. Most of them are transit planets that crowd far inside Earth's year. The text now gives the count instead of only claiming it: 89% of the planets with a known period orbit faster than Earth, and nearly half take less than ten days. Only 167 take less than a day, which is why the middle of the disc stays dark. The orbit rings are now strong enough to add up to a visible glow where orbits are most common.
