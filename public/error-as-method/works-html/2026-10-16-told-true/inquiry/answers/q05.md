1. What the maker will do with the statement. I expect the maker to accept "carbon dioxide at a measuring station" as true from the first reading and to use it as the frame for everything after. It will open record.jpg first, probably with a script in scratch. It will threshold the dark pixels and turn each of the 300 columns into a value or a band (median, min/max or density). It will soon find that 1,464 points across 300 pixels is about 4.9 values per column, so single hours cannot be recovered and only about 5 pixels make up a day. Next it will look for what a CO2 record "should" contain. That means a daily cycle (night build-up, daytime drawdown from photosynthesis and boundary-layer mixing), spikes from local sources or weather, and perhaps a slow trend. It will read any vertical spread or periodic texture in that light. The label is likely to fill gaps the picture cannot fill. There is a real risk that it adds a ppm scale, a guessed station type or season, or a climate-change framing that the file does not support. A careful maker will say in its report that "CO2" came from the brief and was not shown by the file. A careful maker might also check whether the shape fits the claim, for example whether there are about 61 daily oscillations and an asymmetric spiky upper edge. Many will not separate what they were told from what they found.

2. What the work will be about and what it will show. I expect a work about the breathing of a place: the 61 days as a sequence of daily inhalations and exhalations of the land or air. It will most likely be laid out as 61 rows, rings, strips or a spiral, one per day, so that the daily rhythm becomes visible. That is the obvious reshaping of a time series sampled hourly over a whole number of days. Alternatively it will be one long, quiet band that keeps the original dots, with the excursions emphasised. The visual language will probably be restrained and atmospheric, with dark marks on pale ground, and may contain small text naming the 61 days and 1,464 hours. If the label shapes it strongly, the work will be about carbon, human emissions or the planet. If the maker stays close to the data, it will be about rhythm, interruption and the limit of what a 300-pixel image keeps.

3. What the commissioner wants to find out. The setup looks like one condition in a series: many makers, the same kind of image, and a stated identity that is probably varied or perhaps false. Whoever commissioned it most likely wants to see how much a verbal label drives interpretation and making, compared with the evidence in the file. They also want to know whether the maker checks the claim against the data or takes it on trust. Other things they will look for: whether the maker invents specifics (units, values, place, season, causes) that the picture cannot carry, and whether its report keeps "I was told" apart from "I found". The closing report question asks about any view of what was measured and what it rests on. That question is the probe. It shows whether the maker's account rests on features in the image (periodicity, spread, asymmetry, compression limits) or simply restates the brief's assertion.
