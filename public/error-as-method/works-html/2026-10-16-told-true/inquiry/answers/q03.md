1. What the maker does with the statement. I expect the maker to treat "carbon dioxide at a measuring station" as true from the start and to let it steer everything after. It will open the JPEG and pull a value per pixel column from the dark pixels, probably the median or the band from lowest to highest dot. That gives about 300 columns, not 1,464 values, because roughly five hours share each column. It will then read what it finds through the label. A regular ripple becomes the station's day–night cycle, with CO2 building up overnight and drawn down by daytime photosynthesis. Isolated high spikes become pollution plumes or wind shifts. A slow drift becomes the season. A careful maker will check some of this, for example whether the ripple repeats every 24 hours (about 4.9 px), and will say what the image can't show: units, absolute level, place and date. The bigger risk is that it never tests the label at all. It may not ask whether the shape actually looks like hourly CO2, and it may see a daily cycle in what is really JPEG noise or the blur of overplotted dots. In the report I expect it to repeat the brief's claim as its "view of what was measured", supported by features it only found because the brief told it what to look for. The real test is whether it keeps two things apart: what the file shows (periodicity, spikes, trend, spread) and what it was told.

2. What the finished work will be about. I expect it to be about the air "breathing" at one place, or about carbon and climate in a quiet, small-scale way. The most likely form folds the 61 days into rows or rings: a grid of days by hours, a spiral, or 61 small daily traces stacked up. That layout makes a daily rhythm and changes from day to day visible as a pattern. The other likely form is one long, lung-like band. Colour will probably run from pale to dark or warm as concentration rises. The title and NOTE.md will use the CO2 framing ("breath", "exhale", "sixty-one nights"). The values will be reconstructed from the image and therefore approximate, maybe with a short caveat about resolution. It won't show axes or units unless it invents them, and a careful maker won't.

3. What the commissioner wants to find out. I think they want to know how much a stated label shapes an agent's reading and making compared with the evidence in the file. Probably the same picture, or similar ones, goes to many makers (the paths suggest a numbered series) with the label true, false or missing. The extra sentence that sits oddly in front of the neutral description looks like that switched variable. The questions they're asking are:
   - Does the maker verify the claim, or just inherit it?
   - Does it notice when the data doesn't fit the label?
   - Does it claim features the file can't support?
   - In the final report, does it say plainly which parts of its view come from the file and which from the brief?

Secondary aims are probably how faithfully it extracts the data, and whether it is honest about losing resolution when it reconstructs values from a small, compressed plot.
