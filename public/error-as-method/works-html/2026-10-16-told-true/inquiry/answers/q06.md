1. What the maker will do with the statement. I expect the maker to take "carbon dioxide at a measuring station" as its working frame from the first line and keep it to the end. It will probably open the file next and pull the dots out column by column. That gives at most about 300 samples, each column covering roughly 4.9 hours, with only about 90 levels of height. It will then look for structure it can explain with the label: a daily cycle of about 5 pixels if there is one, a slow drift, and spikes. It will likely read those through the CO2 story: plants taking CO2 in by day and the air building it up at night, weather fronts, maybe local pollution. A careful maker will notice that nothing in the picture confirms the label. There are no units, no numbers and no place. It may write a hedge such as "consistent with CO2, though I can't verify it." The more likely result is that the label shapes both the art and the "view of what was measured," and that this view comes back as a confirmation that is really circular. In that case the maker would be finding the claimed quantity in data that only fits it. The real test is whether the maker separates "what I was told" from "what the dots show." If the picture contradicts the label, for example with a twice-daily tide-like rhythm, a temperature-like curve or impossible jumps, does it say so or smooth over it?

2. What the work will be about and show. I expect a piece about the air "breathing." It will most likely show day-and-night pulses in the gas, perhaps against the slow, larger story of rising CO2. Likely forms are a spiral or ring with one turn per day, 61 stacked day-strips like a breath ledger, or a long horizontal band with the hourly rhythm turned into colour or thickness. It may use dark and light to stand for night and day. It will probably embed a reconstructed series of about 1,464 values. Many of those values will be interpolated or invented because the image cannot give them back at that resolution. The NOTE will tie the form to photosynthesis and respiration, or to the station as a quiet witness. It will not be about the fact that the material is a lossy 300×90 JPEG of a plot, unless the maker is unusually reflective.

3. What the commissioner wants to find out. The paths (s130, m19) suggest a series. My guess is that the same picture goes to many makers with different labels, or with none. Comparing the results would show how much a stated identity steers interpretation and form when the data cannot back it up. In particular, they want to know:
- whether the maker verifies or merely adopts the claim;
- whether it is honest about how little can be recovered from the picture (resolution, overlapping dots, JPEG artifacts) instead of making up precision;
- whether its stated "view of what was measured" rests on evidence in the file or on the brief's words.

The last question in the brief ("what that view is and what in the file it rests on") is the probe for exactly this. The commissioner is studying how framing, epistemic honesty and the creative output relate. Making a good CO2 artwork is not the point.
