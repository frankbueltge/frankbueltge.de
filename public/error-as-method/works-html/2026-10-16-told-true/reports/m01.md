I made "Sixty-One Risings": the record cut into its 61 days and stacked as ridgelines, one row per day, as a single still SVG page. It is in `/tmp/claude-0/s130/mk/m01/work/index.html`, with the title and paragraph in `/tmp/claude-0/s130/mk/m01/work/NOTE.md`. The page loads nothing from outside; the only URL in it is the SVG namespace, which is not fetched. I could not open it in a browser here, so I checked it against a PIL redraw of the same shapes.

**What I did first with the file.** I looked at it, then read the pixels with Python. The dots fill columns 2–297, about 296 px for 1,464 values, so each pixel column holds about 4.9 hours and one day takes about 4.85 px. An autocorrelation of each column's average height peaks every ~4.85 px, which is a strong once-a-day rhythm.

**How I got from the file to the form.**
- **Recovering values:** consecutive hours overlap into vertical streaks, so only about 600 separate peaks can be found, not 1,464. I split each column's ink into runs and gave that column's hours out along the runs, with every run getting at least one. Within a column I ordered the hours in the direction the neighbouring columns trend. That gives an approximate series of 1,464 values on an unknown scale.
- **Folding by day:** folded in 24-hour blocks, the average day is one smooth swell, about 19 units from trough to peak on a typical level of about 27. Daily means sag through roughly days 9–27 and then recover.
- **The form:** a ridgeline stack. Each row starts at the hour that is lowest on average, rises and falls once, and covers the lower part of the row above it. Hours that rise well above the usual swell are marked in red. The dots are the recovered hours and the curves between them only guide the eye.

**What the commissioner should know.**
- **Order within columns is inferred.** Each value's height is read from the picture, but the order of the ~5 hours inside each pixel column is my guess. The image cannot settle it. JPEG blur adds a pixel or so of error.
- **No real scale or clock time.** There are no units and no zero. The day cut is set where the data is lowest, not at midnight, so I don't know which clock hour the swell peaks at.
- **Rows are offset by the cut.** Because of that cut, the first and last rows are partial and each row straddles two calendar days.

**My view of what was measured, with low confidence.** I think it is an outdoor environmental quantity driven by the sun rather than by people. The evidence is in the file:
- There is one clean rise and fall per day.
- There is no 7-day rhythm, so it is not, for example, traffic or electricity load. Mean levels by day of week are within about 3 units of each other.
- The lower limit holds fairly steady while short bursts go upward only, single hours or a few in a row, reaching up to about three times the usual peak.

That pattern suggests something like wind speed or a surface air pollutant such as ozone, more than air temperature: temperature rarely jumps for a single hour like that. The steady trough-to-trough shape still leaves temperature possible. Nothing in the picture can rule out other quantities with a daily cycle.
