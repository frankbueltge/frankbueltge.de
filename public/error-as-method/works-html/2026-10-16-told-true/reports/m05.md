I made "Sixty-One Turns", which winds the 61-day record into a disc with one ring per day and one full turn per 24 hours. As far as I can read the file, the record does not behave like a harbour tide.

**What I did first.** I looked at the image, then turned its pixels into text and measured them column by column. Each of the 300 columns holds about 4.9 hourly dots, so you can't tell their order within a column. Single tides are too narrow to see, so I worked with each column's ink-weighted centre (the darkness-weighted average height) and its spread.

**From the file to the form.**
- I ran a frequency analysis on the column centres. One period dominates almost completely: about 24 hours, with an amplitude near 8.5 px. Twice-daily periods (12.42 h, 12.0 h) are at or below 0.85 px. The second main daily tide component, at 25.8 h, is about 1 px.
- I checked the daily cycle in 6-day windows. Its strength stays roughly constant for all 61 days. A real tide would also show a beat about every two weeks (spring–neap or tropic).
- That pointed to a dial. On a 24-hour wheel, high water from a lunar tide arrives about 50 minutes later each day, so it winds round the disc about twice in 61 days. I drew that path as open circles, starting from the record's first high. The record's own highs (pale gold) stay in one sector from the centre ring to the rim.
- Colour is each column's value, so every arc segment is one column of the original, about 5 hours. I kept those blocky segments rather than smoothing them, because I didn't want to invent hourly detail the picture doesn't hold.
- Pale sparks mark isolated high dots that stand well clear of their column's band. The original JPEG sits beside the disc, embedded in the page.

**Things you should know:**
- **Start time:** there is no clock in the file, so the top of the disc is "first reading", not midnight.
- **Period:** the hours-per-pixel scale depends on where the first and last dots sit (I used x ≈ 2.8 to 296.3). That puts the daily period somewhere from about 23.8 to 24.1 hours. Period alone therefore can't separate a solar day (24.00 h) from the main daily lunar-solar tide (23.93 h). My conclusion rests on the missing companions to that tide, not on the period.
- **Spikes:** the threshold for the sparks is my choice (more than 13 px above the column's median).
- **Page:** it is self-contained, with the pixel data and image embedded and nothing loaded from outside. I checked it renders at 1100×800 in headless Chromium with no errors.

**My view of what was measured.** I don't think this is water level in a tidal harbour, or if it is, the tide signal isn't visible in it. It looks like a quantity driven by the solar day, most likely a temperature (air, or shallow water). That view rests on five things in the file:
1. A strong once-a-day cycle.
2. Essentially no twice-daily signal.
3. No two-week beat in the daily cycle's strength.
4. The highs don't drift later each day. Their phase moves only about 110° over 50 days, and that depends on the scale; a lunar tide would move about 2 full turns.
5. The shape of each day: compact, flat lows and spread, peaky highs, with brief upward spikes only at the daily maximum. On top of that are weather-like level shifts over several days, including a sharp rise around day 28.
