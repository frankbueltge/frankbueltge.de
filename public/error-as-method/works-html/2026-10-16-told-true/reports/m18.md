I made a still image called **Sixty-One Breaths**. It stacks the record as 61 lines, one per day, so the daily cycle in the carbon dioxide shows up as one ridge running down the page.

**What I did first with the file.** I looked at the image, then read its pixels into Python as text so I could see exactly where the dots fall. The dots fill rows 27–85 and run from x≈2 to x≈297. That makes about 4.9 hourly values per pixel column, so the hours overlap and can't be read back exactly. I also found one faint dot near the top (y≈5, column 173, around day 36). It is the highest value in the record and easy to miss.

**How I got to the form.**
- **Finding the rhythm:** an autocorrelation of the column centres shows a strong period of about 4.84 px. That is 1,464/24 values per day spread across the plot's width, so the record has a clear 24-hour cycle.
- **Rebuilding an hourly series:** my first method, a smoothed path through the ink, cut off the tall peaks. Instead, each column's ~5 hours are placed at the ink-weighted quantiles of that column's dark pixels, so every isolated high dot gets an hour. A small two-state step then chooses rising or falling order within each column to keep the series continuous. Redrawing the result next to the original matched its texture well.
- **Folding by day:** if each 24-value block is taken as a day, values climb slowly for about 10 hours, peak at block-hours 10–11, fall fast over about 5 hours, then stay low and flat for about 8.
- **The layout:** a ridgeline of 61 days, each line drawn over the one before. My first version measured every day from a shared baseline, and days with high overall levels buried the rows above them. So each line now rises from its own day's lowest hour, and those lowest hours get their own thin trace at the left. That keeps the daily cycle and the slow drift on separate scales.

**Things the commissioner should know.**
- **The values are estimates.** The JPEG is lower-resolution than the data, so each hourly value is uncertain by about ±1 pixel of the original (roughly 1/60 of its full range). The order of hours within each 1-px column is inferred, not read.
- **There is no scale.** All heights are relative, and the caption on the page says so.
- **Text on the page:** it has a title and a few lines of small caption text.
- **Not seen in a browser.** No browser or SVG renderer was available here. I checked the layout with a Python drawing of the same geometry, and checked that the SVG parses, but I never saw the page itself rendered, so it's worth opening once at 1100 × 800. The page is static, with no scripts or outside resources.

**My view of what was measured.** I think this is a vegetated inland site during the growing season, recorded in UTC at a station several hours west of Greenwich. That is an inference from the shape of the curve alone:
- The daily cycle is large compared with the variation from day to day. The slow rise, short peak, sharp drop and long flat low look like carbon dioxide building up overnight under a still, shallow layer of air near the ground. After sunrise, growing plants take it up and stirring air mixes it away, which explains the drop and the well-mixed low afternoon.
- The peak falls at hours 10–11 of the record's own day. If each day starts at midnight UTC, that matches dawn about 5–6 hours behind UTC, for example in the Americas. If the record uses local time instead, the clock is unknown.
- The daily lows drift down over roughly the first four weeks, consistent with plants drawing more carbon dioxide out of the air. Then on day 29 the low jumps up by about a third of the full range, with a high, flat day. That looks like a change of air mass, such as a weather front.
- Isolated single-hour spikes, including the extreme one around day 36, look like nearby sources or very still nights.
