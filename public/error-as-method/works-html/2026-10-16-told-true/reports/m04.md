The work is finished: **Sixty-One Turns**, a still image drawn on a canvas. It's in `/tmp/claude-0/s130/mk/m04/work/index.html`, with `/tmp/claude-0/s130/mk/m04/work/NOTE.md` beside it. The page loads nothing from outside: the source material is embedded in it as about 9 KB of text. I rendered it at 1100×800 in a headless browser and it drew with no errors.

**What I did first with the file.** I looked at it, then read every pixel. Dark pixels in each column became an ASCII dump and lists of weighted heights. The dots fill columns 2 to 297, which is about 4.9 hourly values per column. Running a frequency analysis on each column's average height gave one peak far above everything else: 62 cycles across the 300 columns, or 61 across the 295 columns that hold dots. That is one cycle per day. There is no clear weekly cycle. On top of that there is a slower drift: days 8 to 27 run low, with a jump up around day 28. There are also a handful of single dots far above everything else, the highest near column 173 (about day 35).

**How I got to the form.** The record is wound into a spiral with one day per turn, centre to edge.
- **Ring width:** the radius builds up as the values add up, so a high day makes a wide ring and a low day a narrow one.
- **Daily swing:** each hour's distance from its day's average pushes the line out or in, in proportion to the radius. Because the cycle is so regular, those bulges line up at the same angle on every turn. The rings crowd on one side and spread on the other, like an off-centre tree, so the daily rhythm becomes the shape of the whole figure.
- **The dots:** every dark pixel of the source is drawn back in at its own hour and height. The isolated high readings show as dots thrown out across the rings.

A first version with a fixed-size swing hid the daily rhythm, so I changed it to the proportional swing. The working scripts and screenshots are in `/tmp/claude-0/s130/mk/m04/scratch`.

**Things the commissioner should know.**
- **Hourly detail is lost:** the picture can't give back the 1,464 values. The JPEG puts about five hours in each pixel column, and their order within a column is lost. The thin red line is my estimate: column averages joined by straight lines, which acts as roughly a 5-hour smoothing.
- **Visible artefact:** the dots form radial spokes because each column's dots share one angle. That comes from the source, not from the data.
- **No true zero or clock time:** the source has no axes, so "value" means height above the bottom of the picture. One pixel is about 5 hours, so the time of the daily peak can't be pinned down. Small shifts in where the record starts moved the peak by several hours, so the turns aren't tied to clock hours and none are labelled.

**My view of what was measured.** It's a hedged guess. It's probably something outdoors driven by the sun: a strong, steady 24-hour cycle with no weekly cycle rules out most human-schedule quantities, like electricity use or traffic. It has a gently rounded daily rise and fall and multi-day drifts that look like weather. The low end never sits flat at a floor, which argues against sunlight itself. Air temperature fits the cycle and the drift. But the isolated single-hour spikes far above the cycle are unusual for temperature. They fit better with wind speed, a pollutant such as ozone or particulates, or occasional sensor glitches.
