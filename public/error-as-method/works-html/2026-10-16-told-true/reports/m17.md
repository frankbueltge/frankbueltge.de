I made a page called "Sixty-One Breaths". It winds the 61 days into a coil, one turn per day, so the daily rhythm in the CO₂ record lines up from one day to the next.

**Files**
- `/tmp/claude-0/s130/mk/m17/work/index.html`: a single still image on a canvas. The data is embedded in the page and it loads nothing external. I rendered it at 1100 × 800 in headless Chromium and it looks right.
- `/tmp/claude-0/s130/mk/m17/work/NOTE.md`: the title and a paragraph on what the form does and why.
- `/tmp/claude-0/s130/mk/m17/scratch/`: the scripts (`ext.py`, `fold.py`, `recover.py`, `build.py`), the page template, the recovered data (`cols.json`) and the screenshot (`shot.png`).

**What I did first.** I looked at the picture, then printed its pixels as text. The dots are about 2 px wide. The data runs from about x = 2 to x = 297, so each pixel column covers about 5 hours (about 4.9 columns per day). The individual hourly values can't be recovered from a picture this small. What I could measure was how much ink each column holds and where it sits vertically.

**From the file to the form**
1. **Finding the rhythm.** I tested each column's middle height against a range of possible cycle lengths. A 24-hour cycle stood out strongly: about 4.6 px, against at most 0.7 px for every other length from 8 to 240 hours. There is also a weaker 12-hour component.
   - Folded over one day, about 8–10 hours run high with a wide scatter.
   - About 10 hours sit low and tight. In those hours the columns hold less ink, because dots land on top of each other.
2. **Rebuilding the hours.** I turned the ink back into dots: 1,464 of them, placed by how the ink is spread down each column. Within a column the order of the hours is lost. I put them in an order that follows the trend between neighbouring columns and spread them across the column's ~5 hours. So the fine detail in time is a reasonable guess, while the heights are measured. The note in the page doesn't claim per-hour accuracy.
3. **The form.** It is a clockwise coil with day 1 at the centre. A faint spiral marks the lowest reading in the record. Each dot sits further out the higher its value, and a faint thread joins the dots in time order. The high-scatter part of each day is turned to face the top, so the coil swells and frays at the top and lies tight at the bottom.

**What you should know**
- **No scale.** The picture has no axes, so the work only shows more and less, never ppm.
- **Clock time is unknown.** Which part of the coil is "night" is my reading, not something marked in the file. The orientation was my choice.
- **Hour order is approximate.** The hours inside each ~5-hour column are placed by the method above, not read from the file.

**My view of what was measured.** I think this is a station surrounded by plants, in the growing season. CO₂ builds up overnight, when the air near the ground is still and plants are only breathing out. It is drawn down to a steady floor by day, when the air mixes and plants take it in. The evidence in the file:
- the strong 24-hour cycle;
- the pattern of a short, wide, high stretch against a long, flat, tight floor;
- isolated upward spikes;
- a few multi-day episodes where values stay high (the clearest is around day 28–29).

The daily floor falls over roughly the first 3 weeks, then climbs through the rest of the record. That fits late summer into autumn in the northern hemisphere, but it is weak evidence and only a guess.
