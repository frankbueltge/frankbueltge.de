I finished the work. It is called **Sixty-One Lines**. The page is `/tmp/claude-0/s130/mk/m02/work/index.html`, a single canvas with the data built in and no outside requests. The note is `/tmp/claude-0/s130/mk/m02/work/NOTE.md`. My scripts and test renders are in `/tmp/claude-0/s130/mk/m02/scratch/`. I rendered the page in headless Chromium at 1100 × 800: no script errors, no network requests, and everything fits.

**What I did first with the file.** I looked at the image, then turned the pixels into an ASCII map to see how the dots behave. Each dot is about 2 px across. The ink runs from x = 2 to x = 297, so each pixel column holds about 4.9 hourly values. The picture cannot resolve single hours.

**How I got to the form.**
- **Daily cycle:** an autocorrelation of each column's average ink height repeats every ~4.85 px. That works out to 24 hours exactly. Folding the image at that period shows a clear daily shape.
- **No weekly cycle:** a check of the daily averages showed no 7-day pattern.
- **Spread over weeks:** the record sags lower from about day 8 to day 27, jumps up on day 28, then settles.
- **Lopsided:** there are many brief, isolated jumps upward far above the rest, and almost none downward.

That led to a drum-chart layout (like a seismograph drum): the record is cut every 24 hours and the 61 days are stacked as lines, so each hour of the day lines up vertically. The day boundaries come from the dot centres, about 0.2007 px per hour.
- Each source column becomes a dot, giving 4–5 per day.
- The curve between the dots is interpolated, and the page says so.
- A pale band shows the spread of values hidden inside each column.
- 37 columns whose top dot sits more than 15 px above the column's average are marked in red.

**What the commissioner should know.**
- **Interpolated curves:** the picture holds about 296 columns of information, not 1,464 values. I could only recover column averages and spread. The curves are drawn between those points, not measured.
- **Hour labels:** "hour 0–24" counts from the record's first value. It is not the time of day, because the start time is unknown.
- **Approximate:** the day cuts are estimated from dot edges in a compressed JPEG. A small error would slowly shift where each day starts, though the daily swell lines up well across all 61 rows, which supports the cut.
- **Choices of mine:** the vertical scale is my own choice, and so is the 15 px threshold for the red marks.

**My view of what was measured (low confidence).** It is probably something in the environment, not human activity. I rest this on four things in the file:
- It has a strong 24-hour rhythm.
- It has no weekly rhythm, which argues against things like power use or traffic.
- It drifts over periods of several days, which looks like weather.
- It has sudden, short upward jumps of an hour or two and no matching drops.

The upward-only bursts fit wind speed, or the level of a gas or pollutant near the ground (CO2, ozone, particles), better than air temperature, which rarely jumps for a single hour. The peak falls about 5–11 hours after the record starts. Because the start time and time zone are unknown, I can't use that timing to choose between these. It would fit, for example, afternoon temperature in UTC+8 or a night-time gas peak in the Americas on UTC.
