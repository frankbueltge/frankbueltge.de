I made "Wound on a Day", a spiral that winds the record one turn per 24 hours. On it, the record's daily high stays at the same hour all the way out. That is not what a harbour tide does, and I think the record may not be a tide record.

**What I did first.** I looked at the picture and then read its pixels. It shows a band of dots with a fairly sharp lower edge, a long upper tail and a few isolated dots high above the band. The picture is 300 px wide for 1,464 hours, so each pixel column holds about 5 hours and overlapping dots cannot be separated. I treated the picture as a smoothed record: for each column I took the darkness-weighted average height, which gives about 5-hour resolution.

**How I got to the form.**
- **No tide in it.** Spectral analysis of that column profile showed a sharp line at 24.0–24.07 hours and a small harmonic at 12.00 hours.
  - The main lunar tide line (M2, 12.42 h) is about zero.
  - O1 (25.82 h) is barely above noise, though in real tides it is usually close in size to K1.
  - The daily cycle keeps a steady amplitude for all 61 days. A real once-a-day tide would almost vanish twice a month.
- **Why the spiral.** Folding the record onto a 24-hour clock is itself the test. The spiral has one turn per day, day 1 at the centre and day 61 at the rim. Each hour is coloured by how far it is above or below the 24 hours around it.
- **The reference line.** A dashed line shows where a moon-driven high water would fall: about 50 minutes later each day, so twice round the clock over 61 days. The record's pale ridge does not follow it and stays at roughly the same hours (about 6–14 h after the first reading).
- **Other elements.** The isolated high readings are marked as points of light. A small redrawing of the record as received sits beside the title.

**Things the person who commissioned this should know.**
- The values are reconstructed from a 300-pixel JPEG, not real data. Each turn of the spiral is blurred to about 5 hours, and the picture has no units or scale.
- "Hour 0" is the first reading, not a known clock time.
- I removed slow drift by comparing each hour with the 24 hours centred on it. That is a design choice that brings out the daily rhythm. It hides the slower swings in level, which come and go over about two weeks.
- The pixel-to-hour scale is only known to within about 0.5%. So I can say the period is the 24-hour solar day, but I cannot tell it apart from the 23.93-hour K1 tide on period alone. The case against a tide rests on the missing M2 and O1, the steady daily amplitude, and the 12.00-hour (not 12.42-hour) harmonic.
- The work's text says plainly that the readings follow the sun, not the moon. That contradicts the brief, so you may want to check how the file was labelled.

**My view of what was measured.** I don't think this is a tidal water level, or if it is water level, the tide has been removed. What the file shows:
- A rhythm locked to the 24-hour solar day, with one broad rise and fall per day.
- No weekly cycle, so probably nothing driven by human activity.
- The level wanders over days to about two weeks, like weather.
- A lower edge that is fairly firm, with upward skew and rare isolated spikes.

That fits something driven by sun and weather: air or surface temperature, or wind speed (a sea breeze), are the likeliest kinds. The upward spikes fit wind or a sensor fault better than temperature. That last part is an inference, not something the file settles.
