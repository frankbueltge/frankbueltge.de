**My main finding:** I don't think this record is an astronomical tide. Its rhythm follows the sun's 24-hour day, not the moon.

**What I did first with the file:** I looked at it, then turned the pixels into numbers.
- The dots run from x≈3 to x≈295, so each image column holds about 5 hourly readings.
- The distribution of values has one peak and a long upward tail. One reading near the top of the frame stands far above the rest, and a few short bursts of 3–4 hours rise well above the band.
- A sampled tide would usually pile up at its high and low edges. I tested this by drawing a made-up tide the same way: it came out as a flat-edged band, nothing like this record.

**How I got to the form:**
- I took the ink-weighted height of each column and ran a periodogram. There is one sharp line at 1.00 cycles/day (24.0 h) and a weak one at 2.00 cycles/day (12.0 h).
- I found no sign of the moon. There is no line at 0.93 cycles/day (25.8 h, the lunar diurnal tide), no 12.42-hour semidiurnal tide, and the daily amplitude doesn't swell and fade over 14–15 days.
- I made matching made-up tides as a check. My method did pick up the 25.8-hour line when it was there. It was weaker at catching a 12.42-hour tide, because a column of about 5 hours blurs it. So the case rests mainly on the single solar line, the missing 25.8-hour line, and the shape of the distribution.
- That led to the form. It is an actogram, the layout circadian biologists use: 61 rows, one per day, each showing that day and the next. Darkness means higher than the surrounding 24 hours (I subtract a 24-hour running mean). A red line marks where moon-driven high water would fall, 50 minutes later each day, starting from the record's first crest.
- The dark band of each day's high runs nearly straight down the page. The red line cuts across it at a steep diagonal.
- The page carries `record.jpg` inside itself as a data URI and redraws everything from those pixels when it loads.

**What the commissioner should know:**
- I can't recover individual hourly values. The 5 readings in each column overlap, so the image's tones are blends across about 5 hours, smoothed between columns.
- The darkness shows only the shape of each day. Slow changes from day to day are deliberately removed, so the one high spike reads only as a dark spot within its day.
- The hour scale depends on where I place the first and last dots (x=3.0 to 295.3). Moving the last dot by 1 px shifts the end of the record by about 5 hours.
- The daily crest drifts from about hour 7 to about hour 15 over the two months. That could be a real seasonal shift or a small error in my reading of the edge. Either way it is far from the 51 hours a lunar tide would drift.
- The hours are counted from the first reading; I don't know the clock time.

**My view of what was measured:** The main rhythm is the solar day, with a smaller 12-hour echo. The rest is irregular, skewed upward, with short bursts. I found no tide in it. It could be a water-level record from a harbour where the tide is negligible or has been removed. More likely it is a sun-driven quantity such as wind speed, air or water temperature, or an air-quality reading. It shows only a faint weekly cycle, which argues against something mainly driven by human activity. I can't tell which from the file alone.
