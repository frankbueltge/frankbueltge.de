I made a still image called "Sixty-One Piles": a row of 61 wooden piles, one for each day of the record, each stained from below by that day's water.

Files:
- /tmp/claude-0/s130/mk/m08/work/index.html (a single canvas page with the data inside it; it loads nothing else)
- /tmp/claude-0/s130/mk/m08/work/NOTE.md
- Working scripts are in /tmp/claude-0/s130/mk/m08/scratch: analyse.py, days.py, build.py, and preview.js, which drew the page offline to check it.

**What I did first.** I looked at the image, then read its pixels with Python and printed a character grid of the 300×90 image along with enlarged crops. This showed what I could and couldn't recover. Each pixel column holds about 4.9 hours, and the dots are roughly 2 px blobs that overlap. So the hourly values can't be reliably read back in order, but the heights can: I could measure how much time the water spent at each height in each window of time.

**From the file to the form.** I split the 300 columns into 61 day-slices of about 4.92 columns each. In each slice I added up the ink in every pixel row and scaled it so each day totals 24 hours. That gives an hours-at-each-height profile for every day. From it I worked out, for every height, how many hours the water was at or above it. That number is how wet a pile at that height would be, so it sets the stain colour:
- The foot of each pile, always under water, is near black.
- The zone the tide moves through fades through olive-grey.
- A single hour is enough to leave a faint mark.
- A thin pale line marks each day's highest reach.

I didn't try to rebuild an hourly curve or fit tidal components, because the picture can't support that precision. The page is drawn in canvas and fits the 1100 × 800 view.

**Things the commissioner should know:**
- **No browser here.** I couldn't open the page in a real browser. I checked it by running its script through a minimal canvas substitute I wrote in Node. The caption text, drawn with a Georgia/serif font, wasn't shown in that check.
- **Overlap undercounts dense hours.** Where dots overlap, ink undercounts hours, so the dense middle of each day is somewhat understated.
- **Approximate day edges.** Day boundaries assume the record starts at the left edge of the image.
- **Single high readings kept in.** I didn't remove the stray single readings above the band. Each one shows as a faint wash and sets that day's salt line. On a few days, about 8 of 61, a lone reading puts the line far above the main band.

**What I think was measured.** I believe this is an hourly sea level from a harbour tide gauge, and it fits what you told me. Here is what that view rests on:
1. In enlarged crops the dots form short vertical strokes, about 15–20 px tall, alternating from column to column. That is what a twice-daily tide looks like at about 2.5 px per 12.4-hour cycle.
2. Days where the tide reaches unusually high come around x ≈ 10–15, 90–95, 135–145, 160–175, 220–230 and 280–285. That is very roughly every 70–75 px, or about 14–15 days, which matches the two-week spring–neap cycle. The pattern is suggestive, not clean: I measured it by eye, and an autocorrelation of the high-to-low spread didn't confirm it.
3. The middle of the whole band drifts by about 10–15 px over several days, which is comparable to the daily tidal range of about 15–20 px. That suggests a harbour with a small tide, where weather and air pressure move the level about as much as the tide does.
4. Isolated single dots sit 20 or more px above the band, up to near the top of the image around x ≈ 173. They are one-off readings rather than sustained rises, so I'd guess they are sensor spikes or splash rather than storm surges, but I can't be sure.
